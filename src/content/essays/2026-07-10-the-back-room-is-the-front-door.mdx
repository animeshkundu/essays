**1. The secret is the size.**
On average a museum shows less than five percent of what it owns. The British Museum displays about one percent of its eight million things. The rest was never part of the visit: boxed, sorted, and shelved in rooms the public route did not reach.

**2. The old front door was an edited sentence.**
A curator picked a small fraction of the collection, lit it, labelled it, and set it in an order that made an argument. You were handed the conclusion. The reasoning stayed in storage.

**3. A museum earns trust by the mess, not the story.**
The most honest future is not smoother telling. It is supervised access to the room where the past is still being sorted. That is a real claim, and a contestable one. The people who build good galleries think it is backwards.

**4. Their objection is not weak: the edit was a mercy.**
Fewer things, a clear order, a label right where you need it, one person answering for why this object and not that one. Hold that as the live doubt. The rest of this is an argument with it.

**5. One museum turned its store into the show.**
On 31 May 2025 the Victoria and Albert Museum opened a working store, V&A East Storehouse, in the former London Olympics media centre in east London. More than half a million people have now walked into a building whose whole point is that the sorting shows.

**6. The story did not get clearer. In places there is barely a story.**
Inside, most objects carry minimal information, and many wear just an old barcode on a tag. One critic called the place a museum without labels, and meant it as an attack. Whatever changed here, it was not better telling.

**7. Spectacle is the oldest trick in the building.**
You cross a glass floor with an eighteen-tonne marble colonnade from Agra beneath you, and the thrill is staged. But awe was always on the menu. That is not what changed either.

**8. Visible storage let you look, not choose.**
Museums have put their storage on show since the 1970s. Dense racks behind glass, a route the institution picked for you. You could see more of the collection. You still could not ask for a piece of it.

**9. The study room already let you request the hidden object.**
Long before Storehouse, the V&A's study rooms let people inspect more than 750,000 objects kept off the gallery floor. In the prints and drawings room, that meant an appointment, two weeks' notice, up to five items or boxes at a time. That door was real. But it was around the back, built for researchers, and you had to know it was there.

**10. What is left standing: you can order the object.**
Search the catalogue, drop up to five things into a crate, book a slot at least two weeks ahead, and the object is made ready for you, at a table or in the store where it sits. The visit begins where the institution used to end. The break is operational access, not a wider window onto the collection.

**11. Closeness is built out of limits.**
The nearer they let you get, the more rules appear. One guest. No bags, no water. Fifteen minutes of safety briefing. Wash your hands, take off your rings, tie back your hair. Some things you may hold with gloved hands, some with bare ones, and some you may only look at. Access and restraint are the same gesture.

**12. One booked hour has a machine behind it.**
Before a single visitor could sit with a single thing, the move into that store put roughly 500,000 barcodes on objects, took over 140,000 photographs, gave 9,936 items conservation treatment so they could travel, ran 579 truck journeys, and stuck 63,123 labels on storage locations. Access is not a door. It is a payroll.

**13. Access became somebody's job.**
The ordering service created about twenty new jobs, most of them filled by people who live nearby. The work is searching, fetching, unwrapping, and standing by the table while you look. Every object is checked first for fragility, hazards, and security, and some you may only see, never touch. The people who now hand the collection across the table are, more and more, the neighbours it hired.

**14. Some visitors were paid to help choose.**
A group of young Londoners, the exact audience a museum spends its budget trying to attract, were brought in and paid as consultants. They shaped gallery design, the cafe, and curatorial themes. They crossed a line most visitors never cross: from looking at what a museum chose to helping choose it.

**15. Four of them ran a day in the library.**
For a public open day, four young co-producers spent two months planning with the library team, did a full practice run, and gave themselves jobs: making holes in paper, embroidery, stamping. On the day they demonstrated Japanese pin binding. Staff opened the library's normally closed back entrance for step-free access. All three bookbinding sessions were fully booked. One visitor wrote that the best part was "learning how accessible the library is to the general public. I had assumed it was private access." That assumption is the whole old arrangement, said out loud.

**16. Some of their words now hold up a window.**
At the threshold of the V&A's new sister building, the East Museum, young co-producers helped write a short civic contract. A stained-glass artist widened the lead so the sentences could be engraved into the very lines that hold the window together. One reads: "A civic museum recognises local knowledge as expertise and gives credit for its use." Not a caption beside the art. Part of the structure that keeps it standing.

**17. The record is not the thing.**
An artist came to see what she thought, from the website, was a drawing. On the table it turned out to be a physical mosaic, thick with texture. "It was one of those moments," she said, "where you realise it is better to see a thing in real life because it might surprise you." A gallery shows you the front, at a gallery's distance, often behind glass. The store lets you ask for the thing itself and get close to it: the back, the underside, the texture a photograph flattened.

**18. Now the hard case: choice is a burden, and curation is mercy.**
A reviewer sat down to order from 1.2 million catalogue entries and wrote: "I froze. Where do you even begin?" That freeze is the whole case for the edit. A good gallery has already faced the million doors and opened a few for you, so the choosing is finished before you arrive tired, curious, and without a question of your own to ask. Isn't that, as another writer asked, what curators are for?

**19. Transparency can be a costume.**
Showing the racks is not the same as showing the meaning. You can walk a mile of shelving and leave knowing nothing, the objects present and mute. Even an admirer of the place noticed that one display chose not to name a slave owner, and said it would be "better history" if it did. Openness can perform honesty while quietly withholding it.

**20. The open door is narrower than it looks.**
Half a million people walked the floors. But the one-on-one appointments, the part that brings an object within supervised reach, were imagined before opening at around twenty-five thousand slots in a year, a figure the museum expected to sell out and then review. Bring the standard guest and that is fifty thousand people, against half a million on the floor. Most come to look, not to order. And the store is easiest for the person who already knows how to ask a museum a question. Raw access rewards the confident. Curation was also a kindness to everyone else.

**21. Still, you can argue with a storehouse.**
A gallery's omissions are hard to see; you cannot miss what you were never shown. A store that puts its own sorting on display changes where some of the leaving-out can be seen. On one children's suggestions board, cards went up in anger that the displays had left out the East End's Anglo-Jewry. A working store does not surface every omission. It just moves a few of them into view, where someone can write the card.

**22. So the question does not close.**
It was put plainly, from a panel: "How does what you make with people, as opposed to for them, become the centre of how museums function in the future?" The young co-producers' answer is already set in glass at the sister building's door, west-facing, changing colour as the day moves across it. Whether the rest of the enterprise lives up to the sentence they cut into that window is not a thing you can order in advance, and not a thing anyone can honestly call settled yet.
