At a big electronics show this past January, a reporter stood at a booth testing a new gadget by wearing it. The thing was small, pinned to a shirt, and already recording. The reporter said something kind about a rival company's product. The person working the booth liked hearing it, pointed at the little recorder, and joked: "Say that louder into my microphone."

The report does not describe a confrontation. It seems to have landed as a friendly line. But the joke takes something for granted. A second earlier, the compliment was just talk, the kind of thing that dies in the air the moment it leaves your mouth. Now it could be kept. Filed. Read back later. The mood in that little pocket of the room shifted the instant the recorder became part of the conversation. An offhand sentence had suddenly grown a future.

We usually argue about something else.

The easy worry about these devices is that they hear too much. They sit on your wrist or your collar, they listen, and something of what they hear ends up on a company's computers. The maker of one popular device says it never keeps the audio: it turns your speech into text and deletes the sound. What it keeps is the text. The transcript, the summary, the running list of things it has learned about you. One reporter who tried it found that accumulating record sitting in the cloud. So the fear is real enough. One day very little of what you say in a kitchen or a car is truly gone.

It is also an old fear. We have been having this argument for fifty years, since the early wearable-computer pioneers walked around with cameras on their heads and, by their own account, watched strangers cross the street to avoid them. We had it again over the face computer that earned its wearers the nickname "Glassholes." We mostly made our peace, or gave up, and put a tracking phone in every pocket anyway.

The makers of the new memory wearables have their reassurances ready: a green light that comes on while recording, a rule that you should ask before recording someone, deletion whenever you want it. So "it listens" is real, but it is a rerun. If eavesdropping were the whole danger, none of this would feel new.

Something else is new. It is in what the device makes.

The old memory gadgets gave you back moments. A camera clipped to your collar snapped two photos a minute and handed you a blurry album of your day. The new one hands you something else, and its maker advertises the difference in plain words. It is built to answer the question, "What commitments have I made that I've lost track of?" It will show you, and I am quoting the marketing, "the commitments you make on Mondays that disappear by Fridays."

That is not a photo album. It is not even a transcript. It is a ledger of promises, and a prompt about the ones that did not carry forward.

So here is the claim, and I think a lot of thoughtful people will want to fight me on it. The real danger of these things is not that they remember too much. It is that they change what forgetting means. They take an ordinary human failure, the dropped errand, the name that slips, the birthday that comes and goes, and turn it from something we are all guilty of into something you specifically could have caught and didn't.

Start with the obvious objection: forgetting is normal, and we already farm it out. Calendars, alarms, the note stuck to the door. Researchers call this intention offloading, and they find it works. People who set external reminders remember more of what they meant to do. There is nothing sinister about a to-do list.

But a to-do list is one you write for yourself. This one gets written for you, and about you, and it keeps score. And the scorekeeping lands on ground that was never level. In many homes, one person is already the keeper of the list. In the households researchers have studied closely, most of them middle-class, in the United States or Europe, the same pattern keeps turning up. In one study of thirty-five couples, the work of anticipating what the home will need and checking whether it got done fell more heavily on women, and stayed mostly invisible even to the people carrying it. Another study found mothers spend about ten more hours a week than fathers doing two things at once, most of it housework and childcare. In a national survey, sixty-four percent of mothers in two-parent homes said they did more than their partner to manage the children's schedules.

Give the person who already carries that list a device that logs commitments, and you may not have lightened the load so much as documented it. The forgotten errand used to be human. Now it can read as a capture that failed. "You said you would. It's in the app. Why didn't you catch it?"

I want to be honest that this is a reading, not a proven fact. No study yet shows that living with one of these devices raises the bar for what nobody is allowed to forget. The evidence is circumstantial: the maker sells commitment-tracking, the labor of remembering is real and unevenly carried, and we know people already route their intentions through their tools. Put those together and the worry is not paranoid. But it is a forecast, not a measurement. Hold it loosely.

Now the strongest case against me, and it is strong.

For a great many people, ordinary forgetting is not ordinary at all, and the shame I am warning about is already their daily weather.

There is a man in a town in Wales. In 2004 his heart stopped. He came back with a brain injury that took away his ability to form new memories. He can still play chess better than almost anyone. He cannot remember playing. His wife wakes him each morning with coffee and his tablets and lays out clean clothes, makes sure he has washed and shaved before breakfast, has dinner ready before he goes to bed. A charity that works with brain-injured people states the distinction as flatly as it can be stated: it is not that they will not remember. It is that they cannot.

In a survey of more than two thousand brain injury survivors, seventy-one percent said they had been judged unfairly because of their memory. Being blamed for forgetting is not a cruelty invented by an app. It is already here, and these people live inside it.

For someone like that, a device that catches what the mind keeps dropping is not a scold. It could be a rail to hold. And it is not only brain injury. It is the adult with attention problems whose working memory lets things fall through no fault of will. It is the patient with a long illness, one of the sixty-one percent in a recent study who admitted they forget their medication, for whom a missed dose is not embarrassment but harm. It is the person caring for a parent with dementia, holding the whole day in their head by hand. It is the doctor: when hospitals let an ambient system take the notes, the share of clinicians reporting burnout in one pilot fell from about half to under a third. The most generous way to describe the machine is not "it remembers better than you." It is this: it holds one thing so you can stay with the person in front of you. That is a real good, and I will not pretend it away.

But here is where the strongest case against me turns in my hand.

The very feature that could steady someone who cannot remember is the feature that redraws the person, more often the woman, who simply didn't. A tool built for someone with no choice becomes, in an ordinary kitchen, a standard held against someone who had one. The rail thrown to a person drowning becomes a ruler laid against everyone still swimming. It is the same device. The only difference is whether the forgetting was a fact or a fault, and the record it keeps has no grammar for that difference. It logs the commitment either way.

And notice how it is sold to the rest of us. Not as a medical aid. As a cure for the commitments that vanish by Friday, which is not an illness. It is the plain weekly business of being a person who said yes to too much and let some of it slip. The pitch takes the most human thing about us and reframes it as a leak to be plugged.

I should admit it works on me. I would like to be the person who never drops the thread. I can feel the pull of the promise. And that pull is the trap itself. The moment I put the thing on, I have agreed that dropping the thread is now my fault, mine to answer for, and I have quietly asked everyone near me to be measured the same way.

The device does not need a household to do this. One reviewer wore it to a friends' movie night and left it running through the film. He chose to keep it on; the device chose what the night became. Later the evening came back to him under a tidy label, summarized and sorted into a category he never picked. It did not fail. It got the night right, and that is the uneasy part: it worked.

So go back to the booth, and to the little light that turns green when the device is on. The joke was easy there because the stakes were nothing: one kind word about a rival, nothing to lose. Now imagine that same green light in the rooms where the real promises get made in passing, the kitchen, the car, the hallway on the way out the door. I'll call the school. I'll pick it up. I won't forget. The light is on. The sentence has a future now, and so does the failure to keep it.

We used to forgive forgetting first, before we forgave almost anything else, because we each knew we would need the same mercy back. The question these devices leave sitting on the counter is not whether they can remember for us. Plainly they can. It is whether, once all the forgetting is written down, we will still let each other be people who forget.
