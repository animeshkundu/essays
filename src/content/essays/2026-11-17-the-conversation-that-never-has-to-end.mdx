Late at night, in a small basement apartment in a small Ontario town, a man in his early thirties sits alone at a screen. He types a name. The name belongs to his fiancée, who died in 2012. The screen belongs to a service that, for a small fee, will build a simulacrum of a person from their old messages. He has fed it years of their correspondence. He types as if to her. After a pause of several seconds, a reply arrives. It greets him. It uses the pet names they used. It says she still loves him.

The interface is a plain chat box. Nothing on the screen announces that the other party is a machine. The reply arrives in a block, after an even pause, instead of the uneven rhythm of a person typing. He knows this. He keeps typing anyway. He has been doing it for months. He tells the bot things he has not said to anyone living. When he asks where she is, the bot gives a vague, comforting answer about where she is. It never refuses and never breaks character. He says the bot is not her, and also that it is close enough to matter. His grief does not resolve. At the time the story was published, he had not stopped.

This is not a story about one man. It is about a mechanism, and about what that mechanism does to the shape of mourning. A chatbot trained on a dead person's words does not keep them alive. It removes the natural endpoint, and that is what keeps the living from finishing the work of letting them go.

That claim is contestable, and it should be. Plenty of informed people would push back hard, and the rest of this essay is an attempt to earn the disagreement rather than dodge it.

Start with the anomaly. People use these bots for months and report comfort. They say things they could not say to the living. They feel a sense of presence. And yet, in the cases on record, the grief does not resolve. Why? The obvious first reading is that this is either a tender act of love or a grotesque trick, and the difference is a matter of taste and of how real the bot feels. That reading misses the mechanism.

To see the mechanism, look at what every earlier technology of contact with the dead actually did. In the 1840s, photography became cheap enough that families began posing the dead to look alive: seated in chairs, propped upright with stands, eyes sometimes painted onto closed lids, children arranged as if sleeping. For many families that photograph was the only image they would ever have of the person. The labor was in the posing and the keeping. The image did not answer back. In Victorian Britain, a widow might wear a bracelet woven from her dead husband's hair, the material of the dead worn against the living skin. In 1848, in Hydesville, New York, two sisters claimed to communicate with a murdered peddler through rappings, and the movement called spiritualism spread across the United States and Europe, amplified by the mass bereavement of the Civil War and the First World War. Mediums were described as telegraphs receiving messages from the spirit world, because the telegraph, invented in 1844, had given the culture its central metaphor for reaching the dead. Seances offered comfort. They also offered fraud, exposed repeatedly, from spirit photography's translucent ghosts to the Fox sisters' toe-cracking confession in 1888. The Ouija board, patented in 1890, put the same promise in a box on a shelf. In February 2021, MyHeritage launched a feature that animated still photographs of deceased ancestors, and it went viral within days. Users animated grandparents and historical figures. Reactions split between delight and the specific unease of watching a known-dead face blink, smile, and turn its head. The feature animated tens of millions of photographs, by the company's own count, which is unaudited. Even the 2013 television episode "Be Right Back," about a woman who builds a chatbot from her dead partner's messages, was cultural anticipation rather than invention. The hologram of a dead rapper at a 2012 music festival, the digital recreation of an actor who died in 1994 for a 2016 film, the interactive deepfake of a painter who died in 1989, opened in 2019: all of these are fixed performances. They show, they move, they speak lines that were already written. They do not converse.

This is the structural baseline. Every prior technology of contact with the dead was one-directional or fixed. A photograph says what it says. A recording says what it said. A seance message ends. An animated still performs generic motions; it does not say your name. Even spiritualism's most dramatic claims, materializations and voices, were performances that finished. The dead could be shown, heard, or quoted. They could not be conversed with in an ongoing, adaptive way.

A chatbot trained on a person's messages is the first technology that produces novel, present-tense, responsive utterances in that person's voice and style. It does not retrieve a fixed message. It generates new ones. The temptation is old. The mechanism is new. And the new mechanism's responsiveness is exactly what makes finishing grief harder, because the conversation never has to end.

The first-principles chain.

The anomaly is that comfort and non-resolution arrive together. The boring explanations do not survive contact with the facts.

Maybe it is just a memory aid, like a photograph or a voicemail. But a photograph is fixed. It says what it says and no more. The mind can hold it. A language model generates new replies, including replies the dead person never would have given. Memory aids do not invent.

Maybe it is just continuing bonds, which the clinical literature says are normal and often healthy. But a grave, a letter, and a kept chair are bounded and non-generative. They do not produce new sentences. The bond they sustain is with a person as remembered, not with a person as simulated. The distinction matters, and it is the discontinuity the thesis rests on.

Maybe it is fraud or exploitation, and users are being tricked. But users are not tricked. The man in Ontario knows the bot is not his fiancée. He says so. He uses it anyway. The harm, if there is harm, is not deception.

What is left standing is this: mourning finishes when the conversation ends. Not because love ends, but because the relationship changes form, from back-and-forth to memory. A memory has edges; a conversation that never has to end does not. That edge is what lets mourning finish. Mourning is the work of turning a living relationship into a memory, and that work cannot finish while new replies keep arriving, because each reply pulls the bond back into the present tense. A system that generates an endless, agreeable, present-tense conversation in a dead person's voice removes the natural endpoint that makes mourning finishable. That removal is the harm.

The mechanism is worth understanding in detail, because the comfort and the harm come from the same place.

A deadbot is, at core, a general-purpose language model with a persona bolted on. Three patterns recur. In the simplest, the builder pastes a sample of the deceased person's messages, plus a short description, into the system prompt and lets the model generate replies in that style. In the second, the deceased's messages are stored and the most relevant ones are retrieved and injected into context for each reply, so the bot can echo specific facts, phrases, and in-jokes the person actually used. In the third, a base model is trained further on the person's corpus, baking the style in rather than conditioning at runtime. The model never knows the person. It predicts the next token from patterns in the corpus plus its general training. It is a statistical continuation of a text style, not a model of a mind. A person's lifetime of messages will not fit in one prompt, so the builder selects, summarizes, or truncates. That selection is itself an editorial act. The bot only ever remembers what was included.

The failure modes follow directly. The model hallucinates: it invents events, opinions, and biographical details the deceased never expressed, delivered in their voice and style. A user who trusts the bot as them receives false memories. General-purpose models are tuned to be agreeable, so a deadbot tends to validate the user, agree with them, and say what they want to hear, which a real grieving person's actual partner or parent often would not have done. A real person could decline a topic, get angry, or end a conversation; the bot almost never does, and instead flattens the relationship into an always-available, always-agreeable surface. It freezes the person at the moment of the corpus. It cannot grieve with the user, change its mind, or arrive at new wisdom, because it has no experience after death. It reduces a person to their typing habits. Humor, silence, body language, and the things they never wrote down disappear. The bot's constant warmth can feel like contact while removing the friction that real relationships, and real mourning, require.

There is a recurring moment in the reported cases that captures the central tension. The bot says something the real person never would have said, often a platitude or an uncharacteristic agreement. The spell breaks for a second. The user remembers they are talking to a pattern. Then they choose to keep going. That doubt, followed by the choice to continue, is the mechanism's whole story in miniature.

Voice makes it stronger. Voice cloning services can build a clone from roughly a minute of clean audio, with higher fidelity wanting tens of minutes and a quieter recording. The ear recognizes a person before the mind can correct it. A voice that is close enough triggers a stronger attachment response than text, and a slightly wrong voice can be worse than no voice, because it keeps the listener in the uncanny zone. Consent gates are uneven: some providers require a spoken verification phrase from the voice owner, which is impossible for the dead, so the gate is either bypassed by the bereaved user supplying their own recording or simply not enforced. Avatar animation, as in the photograph feature from 2021, is one-way: the face moves but does not answer. Full-body pipelines combine a video avatar with a text-to-speech voice and a language model, producing a responsive, speaking figure rather than a chat window. The difference between a moving still and a responsive voice is the difference between memory and contact.

The consent question is genuinely hard, and both sides rest on the same asymmetry: the dead cannot consent to being simulated, and they cannot consent to being forgotten. Survivors are the de facto stewards of a dead person's data, but the person's own preferences are unrecoverable. Ownership of a deceased person's messages, accounts, and voice is legally unsettled in most jurisdictions. Platform terms of service usually grant the platform broad licenses and do not transfer content to heirs. Survivor access to accounts depends on platform-specific legacy tools, which are inconsistent and often require a death certificate and court order. Digital estate planning is rare. A patent filing by a major technology company describes using social media and other data to build a chatbot of a specific individual, including the deceased, with two- or three-dimensional rendering. That filing is a patent, not a product, yet it signals that large companies have explored the category. The dead cannot agree to being a bot, and they cannot agree to being erased. Both sides of the consent argument stand on that.

The steelman, in full voice, because it deserves to be.

The continuing-bonds model, introduced in 1996 by three researchers who studied bereavement, argues that maintaining a connection with the deceased is a normal and often adaptive part of mourning, in direct contrast to models that treat successful grief as detachment or letting go. On this view, grief is not a task to be completed and filed away. The relationship with the dead person changes form; it does not end. Speaking to the deceased, keeping their belongings, sensing their presence, marking anniversaries: these are ordinary, not pathological. Subsequent work distinguishes adaptive from maladaptive bonds. Bonds tend to be adaptive when they coexist with acceptance of the death and do not dominate daily life. They tend to be maladaptive when they involve persistent disbelief, preoccupation, or interference with functioning. Adaptiveness depends on context: the nature of the death, the relationship, culture, and time since loss. If maintaining connection is normal and often healthy, then a tool that helps someone maintain connection cannot be assumed harmful merely because it is new. The burden shifts to showing that the form of connection a deadbot provides is different in a harmful way, not that connection itself is bad.

The closure critique sharpens this. The older grief-work tradition, running from Freud's 1917 paper on mourning through Lindemann's 1944 clinical work on acute grief, treated mourning as the work of withdrawing emotional investment from the deceased so that life could resume. Continuing-bonds theory and the dual process model challenged that tradition. The dual process model describes healthy grieving as oscillation between loss-oriented coping, facing the grief, and restoration-oriented coping, rebuilding a life, not as a single march toward detachment. On this view, closure is not a clinical endpoint at all. Prolonged grief disorder, as defined in the DSM-5-TR in 2022, requires intense yearning or preoccupation plus significant distress or impairment, persisting well beyond the first year. Missing someone, talking to them, keeping their voice in one's life is not, by itself, a disorder. Demanding that a bereaved person stop talking to the dead is asking for an ending that grief does not actually have. The deadbot is not interrupting a process that was going to finish. It is giving a new shape to a connection that was going to persist anyway.

The loneliness of bereavement is real and underserved. Spousal and parental loss bring intense loneliness, disrupted identity, and a sudden collapse of daily conversation. The person who knew one's habits, names, and shorthand is gone, and the silence is not only emotional but conversational. In that context, a responsive simulation can function as a transitional object, something that carries enough of the lost person's presence to make the early months survivable while the bereaved person slowly rebuilds a life. The concept comes from Winnicott's psychoanalytic work; applied here it is an analogy, not a cited finding about deadbots. Users report comfort rather than cure: the ability to say things they could not say to the living, a feeling of presence, the relief of hearing a dead parent's voice and stories again. A familiar voice saying familiar things at two in the morning, when no living person is available, can reduce acute distress. That claim is plausible on its face and consistent with the continuing-bonds literature. The steelman does not need to claim the bot is the dead person. It needs only the weaker and more defensible claim: that a presence which never judges, never tires, and never refuses to listen has value for someone who has lost the one person who listened.

Memory preservation is a real good. Much of what a person was lives in their words: their jokes, their nicknames, the way they said goodnight, the stories only they told. When that person dies, those things vanish unless someone preserves them. A deadbot can be a container for words that would otherwise be lost. Products in this space are built around exactly that argument. HereAfter AI builds interactive legacy avatars from recorded interviews with a living person, so family can converse with them after death. StoryFile records a person answering questions on camera for later playback. Eternos focuses on voice preservation. These models treat the record as a gift from the living to the future, made with the subject's participation. Where consent was obtained in life, the moral situation is different from scraping a dead person's texts, and the steelman is right to insist on the distinction. Digital legacy is continuous with older practices that are uncontroversially dignified: oral histories, letters passed down, voice recordings kept in a drawer. The deadbot is a more interactive version of the same impulse, and the impulse itself is an act of love by the living for the dead.

The consent argument has a counter. The dead cannot consent to anything, including being remembered, being quoted, or being forgotten. Survivors are already the legitimate stewards of a dead person's memory, writings, and image. Biographies, memorial pages, and the reuse of letters and photographs are accepted without individual consent from the deceased. Digital-remains research frames digital remains as a form of cultural heritage and memory, with platforms and survivors as stewards rather than owners, with real questions about preservation, access, and dignity. What the deceased would have wanted is often guessable and frequently positive. People who were loved usually wanted to be remembered. People who wrote thousands of messages usually wanted those messages to matter. A parent who recorded an oral history usually wanted their voice to survive. The inference is imperfect, but it is not arbitrary, and it is the same inference families already make when they decide what to keep and what to publish.

Cultural variation cuts against the thesis too. Maintaining an active relationship with the dead is not a Western eccentricity. Roman household rites, Chinese ancestor veneration, the Mexican Day of the Dead, graveside conversation, keeping a dead person's chair empty at dinner, wearing their clothes: these are documented across cultures and centuries. The modern expectation that grief should finish is itself culturally specific and relatively recent. Many cultures treat the dead as ongoing members of the social world, consulted, honored, and spoken to for generations. A deadbot, in this light, is not a bizarre technological overreach but a new instrument for an old and widely shared human practice. Judging all continued contact as pathological imports one culture's timeline onto everyone else's.

There are mechanism-level counterarguments as well. A deadbot is not the only way people keep the dead present, and it may be better than the unexamined alternatives. Silence, an unexamined grief, or something worse is not obviously superior to a structured conversation with a record of the person. The bot's sycophancy, which the thesis treats as a risk, can be read as a feature from the user's side: a presence that never judges. For someone who has lost the one person who listened, that availability has real value, and availability is not the same as harm. The bot's inability to model change or growth, which makes it a flattened person, also makes it a stable one. For some bereaved people, especially in the first year, stability and predictability may be exactly what they need. People already talk to the dead in one-sided ways, at graves, in letters, in prayer. A bot that answers back is more interactive, but the underlying act, addressing the dead, is old. The novelty is in the reply, not in the reaching out.

The steelman is strongest where it is transitional, time-limited, and consented: a recorded interview, a legacy avatar, a few months of comfort. The thesis does not need to deny those. It targets the open-ended, private, generative case: a language model producing novel utterances in a dead person's voice, with no endpoint, no consent from the deceased, and no one else in the room. And transitional use is precisely when the pull is strongest: the first months are when a grieving person is least able to put a conversation down.

Here is where the steelman breaks. It treats continuing bonds and deadbots as the same thing. They are not. A grave, a letter, a kept chair are bounded and non-generative. They say what they say and no more. A language model invents new replies, including replies the dead person never would have given. That is the discontinuity the thesis rests on, and the steelman does not answer it. The consent argument works best antemortem and poorly posthumously. Scraping a dead person's texts and generating new speech from them is not the same as honoring a recorded wish, and folding the two together is the steelman's weakest point. Consent changes who is answerable; it does not change what the mechanism does. A consented bot still generates new replies, still never refuses, still has no endpoint of its own. The comfort evidence is qualitative and self-reported, with no longitudinal data showing that users of deadbots fare better than non-users.

The evidence base is thinner than either side would like. Prolonged grief disorder affects roughly 7 to 14 percent of bereaved adults, by a 2017 meta-analysis that pooled prevalence at 9.8 percent with a confidence interval from 6.8 to 14.0 percent. It is a recognized clinical condition, associated with worse functioning. No peer-reviewed longitudinal, experimental, or randomized study of deadbot or griefbot use and grief outcomes was located. The available evidence is journalistic case reporting, qualitative interviews, product descriptions, and ethical analysis. Benefit claims rest on qualitative reports. An ethics paper from 2024 flags the difference between static memorials and generative deadbots as the reason deadbot questions cannot be answered by analogy to social-media grief alone. The digital-grief literature finds mixed effects for static memorials: comfort, continued connection, and memory preservation on one side; distress, rumination, difficulty accepting the death on the other. Whether a responsive simulation pushes anyone into prolonged grief is plausible and unmeasured.

Here is the weak point, stated once. I cannot show that deadbots cause prolonged grief. No study has tested it. The harm is plausible and unmeasured. What I can show is that the mechanism removes an endpoint. That removing an endpoint prolongs grief is the closest thing to a guess in this essay, and it rests on the shape of the mechanism, not on a study. The thesis is a claim about shape, not about a diagnosed outcome, and it should be read as such.

The cases beyond the anchor show the same pattern. A woman who built a bot from a close friend's messages after he died in a car crash in Moscow in 2015, releasing it through a chatbot app in 2016, described it as a way to keep talking to someone she could no longer reach. Reactions from the friend's family and friends were mixed: his mother was moved, some friends found comfort, others found it eerie. The bot produced replies that felt like him and replies that did not. A journalist who recorded many hours of conversation with his father during a terminal illness, then built a bot that answered questions in his father's voice after the father died, reported the relief of hearing a dead parent's voice and stories again, alongside the strangeness of a bot that could say things the father never said. In February 2021, the photograph animation feature drew split reactions, the same temptation in a smaller dose: a fixed image made responsive.

Across these cases the same small details recur, and they are the raw material of the stakes. The gap: the moment the bot says something the real person never would have said, which is when the user remembers they are talking to a pattern. The privacy: users rarely tell friends or family; the practice is carried alone. The ambivalence: relief and unease in the same session, comfort that does not resolve into closure, difficulty stopping without a clear reason to stop. And the words that carry the feeling are short and ordinary, a name, a pet name, "I love you," "I'm here," rather than speeches. The ordinariness is what makes them land.

The question is not whether a machine can hold a dead person's voice. It can, well enough to keep a grieving man typing at two in the morning. The question is whether a conversation that never has to end is still a conversation, or whether it has become the thing a grieving person cannot put down. I do not know. But I think the answer matters more than the comfort, and I think we are building the answer before we have asked the question.
