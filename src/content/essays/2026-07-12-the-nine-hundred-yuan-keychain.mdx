At a Pop Mart store in Beijing, two young women crouch by a low wooden shelf packed with small monster dolls and ask which ones they can take home. None of them, the clerk says. None of these is in stock. Scan a QR code, join the fan chat, and wait.

The chat is called Pop Mart Beijing First Store Group No. 35. It is capped at two hundred people. If the thirty-four groups before it filled up like that, then something like seven thousand people are waiting to buy a doll at that one store. At 10:57 one morning the chat lights up: a pink plush keychain, one hundred yuan, about fourteen dollars, will restock in three minutes. Everyone waits. When the window opens, the group fills with disappointment. One woman writes that she got it.

The others immediately ask her to sell. She writes, I'm not selling. The bidding starts anyway, and in under a minute it reaches nine hundred yuan, about a hundred and twenty-six dollars, for a keychain she paid fourteen for and does not want to give up. Then she stops replying. The group goes back to venting about how hard it is to buy one of these at the price on the label.

I have read that scene a dozen times, and I still cannot decide what I watched.

The easy thing to say is that these are adults losing their minds over a toy, that the toy is ugly and cheap and the whole thing is a mania that will burn out. That reading is comfortable, and I do not trust it, partly because it explains nothing and partly because the numbers are too large to wave off. In the first half of 2025 the company that makes these dolls took in about 13.9 billion yuan, close to two billion dollars, more than triple the year before, at gross margins above seventy percent. That is the kind of margin you expect from software, not stuffed animals. One character line, the one those two women were crouched in front of, was more than a third of the whole business. People do not build a company that size out of a passing joke.

So something real is being sold. The question is what.

Start with the strangest part, the one the group chat makes impossible to ignore. The same object was priced at one hundred yuan and bid up to nine hundred inside the space of a minute, in the same window, to the same people. Nothing about the doll changed. It did not get cuter or rarer or better made in those sixty seconds. What changed is that one person had it and the rest did not, and the rest had just watched, in real time, how close they came and how completely they missed. Whatever they were bidding on, it was not the keychain. You can buy a keychain. What you cannot buy, once the window closes, is what the winner has: she was fast enough, or lucky enough, and everyone saw it.

The first explanation people reach for is the character. Labubu is an elvish little creature from a picture book, mischievous, meant to be kind and forever getting it wrong; one collector called the dolls so ugly they were cute. People hang them off handbags, dress them, carry them to work. This is true, and it matters, and I want to give it full weight, because it is the part the cynics skip. There are people who genuinely love this creature. A coffee-shop supervisor in England spent more than a thousand pounds on thirteen of them, calls the resale market a battlefield, and says the chase is the fun. A fashion content creator in Los Angeles clips them to her bags and her belt, dresses them to match her outfits, and says she would wear them as earrings if it did not hurt. That is not a gambler. That is a fan.

But taste cannot explain the auction. If you love the character, the product line is full of it. You can buy the whole set and skip the suspense; the company sells that option right beside the single sealed box. Taste tells you why you want a monster doll. It does not tell you why you would pay nine hundred yuan for one you could have had for a hundred, or why the sealed box, the one you cannot see into, is the one that sells out in seconds.

The second explanation is childhood. Grown people dress a little plush to look like themselves and say it brings them back. Also real. Also not enough. Nostalgia does not run an auction. It does not make seven thousand people line up behind one store, or turn a reseller's eight-thousand-dollar order into a quarter-million dollars a month in six weeks. Missing your childhood does not have a bid price.

The third explanation, the one that feels smart, is that this is just gambling with a cartoon face on it, and the buyers are marks. I resisted this one hardest, because it is the easy cynicism, and because the buyers who get quoted keep refusing to play the mark. One collector who spent around four thousand dollars before she looked at her statement calls the practice, in her own words, one hundred percent gambling, and warns newcomers off it. She is not fooled. She is describing it accurately and doing it anyway.

That is the tell. When someone can name the trap precisely and still walk into it, the trap is handing them something the name leaves out.

The box sells two things at once, and only one of them is a toy. The other is a short stretch of not knowing, three minutes of it, and the not knowing is the actual product. You are not paying twenty-eight dollars for a doll worth twenty-eight dollars. You are paying it for a sealed guess that probably holds a common one, listed later for six to twenty dollars on resale, and might, rarely, hold the rare one worth many times that.

Researchers who surveyed hundreds of blind-box buyers found this in the plainest possible form: the uncertainty made the toy less useful and more emotional, and the emotion was the strongest reason people bought at all. Not knowing is a bad feature if you want a specific object. It is a great feature if what you actually want is the three minutes before you know.

None of this is new, which is the part that should make us sit up. We have sold packaged uncertainty for a long time, mostly under other names. A lottery ticket is a slip of paper worth nothing that sells you a weekend of maybe. A pack of trading cards is a sealed guess with a picture on the front. A raffle, a claw machine, a seat at a table where the house always wins: in each one the object is half a decoy, and the customer is buying a state of mind, the little suspended moment where you might have called it. The blind box just made that moment cheap, fast, and cute, and set it next to the food court.

But cheap uncertainty is only half of it, and the group chat holds the other half. Go back to the woman who wrote I'm not selling. She meant it, I think. Then nine hundred yuan appeared under her refusal. What the bidding offered her was not really money. It was proof, priced in public, that she had been the one who got it. For one minute, in front of the two hundred who had just failed, she was early.

This is what the buyers themselves keep pointing at. At one Labubu event a man said that seeing someone carry one tells you, in his words, OK you're cool. Another said he loved that they go rare, that it was dope they run out of supply. He is not praising the doll. He is praising the shortage. The thing he values is the part that leaves other people out.

I am guessing here, and I cannot prove it from a toy. But I keep landing in the same place. The feeling those bids were pricing, being early, being ahead, being the one who saw it coming, is a feeling I think the bigger markets have made slow and hard to reach. Being early to a house, a career, an asset takes years to prove out, and those doors can feel further off than they used to. The blind box sells the same sensation on a fourteen-dollar timer. You can be early, or lucky, or a step ahead, by eleven in the morning, and everyone in the chat can watch you do it.

That is why it does not matter that the buyers are not poor. Some of them hang these dolls off designer bags worth far more. The bag is only money, and money is the one thing everyone already knows you can convert. The doll is a different currency: a small public claim that you were quick, or plugged in, or touched by a little luck. You cannot buy that with the bag. You can buy it with the box.

And this is where it stops being charming. The same machine that sells a fun three minutes to a content creator sells something heavier to the person a rung down. That collector who called it gambling has watched people in her community post lines she repeats back. One of them: I don't have gas money for the month. If I run out of gas right now, I wouldn't be able to go to work for the next two weeks. That person was not buying a plush toy either. They were buying the same shot at being early, at the same counter, and the counter does not check what you can afford to feel.

The proof does not keep.

For a while the being-early was durable. You could buy a set for a hundred and sixty-eight dollars and it would resell for closer to two hundred and seventy, and the gap was your receipt, evidence you had timed it. Then the company did the obvious thing and made more. Production went up roughly tenfold in a year, to something like thirty million plush a month, out of new factories in three countries. And the receipts started expiring on arrival. A new series in 2026 still sold out in seconds, then showed up on resale sites at a thirty percent discount within hours. In one city the dolls went from the most searched item to off the list entirely inside a year, and listings fell about seventy percent. A collector who had given up the year before, because she could never win, said the falling prices had finally let her buy one, easily.

She meant it as relief, and it is. It is also the whole machine turning over in your hand. When the doll gets easy to buy, the feeling it was carrying drains out of it. The company, oddly, says this is what it wanted, that if people are only in it to flip, the thing collapses. They are right, and they are describing the trap from the inside: the value was never in the plush, so the plush getting plentiful does not lower the price, it deletes the product. What was for sale was scarcity, and scarcity is the one thing you cannot make more of without destroying it. When the company reported its record year, its own stock fell more than twenty percent, because investors were running the same arithmetic the resale sites were: one doll was carrying nearly forty percent of everything, and nobody could say how long a scarcity premium is supposed to last.

So I end back at 10:57, with two hundred people watching a three-minute window, because that chat is a small clean model of something larger and harder to look at. Strip off the monster face and what those people are buying, fourteen dollars at a time, is a minute of feeling early. Some of them love the little creature, and that love is real; it just cannot explain the auction. Fandom creates the want; scarcity and timing add the premium. They line up anyway, because that minute of being ahead is one of the few versions of the feeling most of them can reach, and because it is over fast enough to buy again.

The woman who got the keychain stopped replying. That is all the record holds: a refusal, a rising number under it, then silence. I do not know what she felt. I know only that the nine hundred yuan was never really for the keychain. It was for being her, for one minute, in front of the two hundred who were not. That is the one thing she could not have handed over even if she had said yes, and it is the one thing that does not keep. You cannot buy it back once the window closes. You can only wait for the next one, and buy the not knowing again.
