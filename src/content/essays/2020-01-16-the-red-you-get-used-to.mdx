At about seven in the morning on the last day of the year, in a small town on the far eastern corner of Victoria, it got dark. Not dim. Dark. The people who live there already had a name for it: the black. A firefighter riding on top of a truck lifted a torch and his own arm above his head, and his hand vanished. He said it was like dipping his hand into a bucket of ink, except the bucket was over his head.

By then the beach was the only place left to be. There was no usable road out. Several thousand people, most of them holidaymakers who had come for the water and the quiet, were packed onto the foreshore and the boat ramp, with three or four hundred cars jammed in behind them like sardines. The plan, if the cars caught and the fire came through, was simple and terrible: get in the water. One family worked out who would carry the two-year-old and who would hold the dog, and which shallow line across the lake would take them furthest from the flames. A shop owner handed out wet bandanas so people could breathe. The sky went from black to a red glow to blood red across the whole dome of it. Then whole gum leaves fell out of it, three and four inches long. Then ash, like sleet. Then embers.

While a man stood in that dark, he was also on his phone. A resident with a bit of signal was posting the sky, the smoke, the embers landing around him, because people overseas had lost contact with a camper park and were asking what was happening. Strangers sent him addresses and asked whether the houses were still standing. He answered what he could. He called it taking breaks a hundred and forty characters at a time. The same sky that was swallowing a firefighter's hand was, at that moment, becoming an image. A thing you could look at. A thing you could scroll.

We tell ourselves a hopeful story about disasters like this one. It goes: people do not act on climate change because they cannot really see it. It is too slow, too abstract, too far off, a graph about the year 2100. So show it to them. Put it over their heads. Make the noon sky go dark and the ash come down like sleet and the smoke reach the next country, and they will finally understand, and understanding, they will change. This fire summer is the closest thing to a clean test of that story we have ever had. I think it fails the test, in a way almost no one is naming. The problem was never that we could not see it. We could. The problem is that seeing it might be how we learn to live with it.

Start with the seeing, because the scale of it is the whole point. This was not a fire you had to be told about. The smoke went up as high as fifteen kilometres, into the stratosphere, and by the eighth of January it had crossed South America, more than halfway around the earth. More than two thousand kilometres from the flames, the skies over New Zealand turned smoky orange in the afternoon and people drove with their lights on at five in the evening; particles carried from Australia tinted the snow on South Island glaciers. It was visible at every height at once: from the water at the foreshore, from a street in a city a thousand miles off, from orbit. If visibility were the missing ingredient, this was a feast.

It was not that people did not care, either. They cared with their wallets and their lungs. One online fundraiser for the fire services became the largest in the history of the platform it ran on: close to fifty million Australian dollars, more than a million donors, seventy-five countries. A poll in January found a majority of Australians, fifty-six percent, said climate change was a major factor in the fires. Whole cities rearranged their bodies around the smoke. In Sydney, ferries that started the morning sounding foghorns stopped at lunchtime because no one could see; buildings were evacuated by their own smoke alarms; a woman on the northern beaches lost her voice for three days. In Canberra, the mail stopped, hospital scanners were affected, the readings ran ten times past hazardous, and people were told to stay indoors. At a tennis tournament, a qualifier who said she had never had breathing trouble dropped to her knees mid-match and could not go on. None of that is looking away. That is a society doing, in the moment, exactly what the hopeful story predicts.

So the hopeful story looks vindicated, and that is the trap. The money, the belief, the masks, the stopped matches: every bit of it happened while the smoke was still in the air, while it was still hard to breathe, while the sky was still the wrong color. It was not a response to seeing. It was a response to being inside it. And being inside ends.

What the seeing left behind, once you were no longer inside it, was images. Images have a strange property. One of the most shared pictures of the disaster, a glowing map of the whole continent lit up like a coal, was not a photograph at all. A graphic designer had built it from nearly eight months of fire data stacked together, with the glow deliberately exaggerated and much of it no longer burning. It went around the world as a NASA satellite photo until it was flagged as false. A scientist had to tell a friend who sent it to him, no, that is not what the earth looks like from space. On another widely shared map, the colors did not mean danger at all; they meant how recently a heat source had been detected. People did not stop to read the legend. They shared the fear at the size of the fear.

A researcher who studies this said the thing worth keeping: people pass on false or out-of-context images because those images feel like the story they are living through. Which means the appetite was real. People wanted a picture big enough to hold what they felt, and when the true pictures were not big enough, a false one would do, and the falseness did not register, because emotionally it was accurate. The feed did not fail to move people. It moved them, and then it kept moving, at the same speed, to the next thing.

One of the most widely shared photographs of the season was a kangaroo in front of a burning house. The photographer drove into a town everyone else was fleeing. He helped neighbors drag bins away from the walls. He shot the burning house. He saw the kangaroos, watched one bolt past the flames to somewhere safer, and then he went to bed. In the morning he sent the picture from his phone to an editor. Within days it was on newspaper covers and on the accounts of famous people, the most impactful image of his fourteen years behind a camera. Look at the gap in that story. At one end, a man with hot ears in fire-retardant clothes, standing in it. At the other end, you, holding the same image on a screen for two seconds, between other images. The disaster and the picture of the disaster are not the same size, and only one of them can be scrolled.

Now the number, because you should have to hold it. An ecologist put out an estimate in the first week of January: more than a billion animals impacted across the country. Read the word carefully. Not killed. Impacted, which is worse in its way, because it takes in the ones that lived through the flames and then came back to no food, no shelter, nothing left to meet them but foxes and cats. A billion is not a number a body can feel. So do not try to feel the billion. Feel this instead.

On an island off the south coast, injured koalas arrived at a makeshift hospital in cat carriers and washing baskets, or clinging to the people who carried them. There was no time to give them names, so they used numbers. Number 64 had burns on all four paws. A vet wrapped a pink bandage around one of them. Many of the ones that came in had to be put down. Some of the ones that could be saved had no habitat left to be returned to. Back near the beach we started on, a thirteen-year-old girl found two koalas burned to death and curled up. Her father put down a kangaroo on a neighbor's land because its paws were burned through and its skin had come away. He said that before the fire, koalas used to walk down the main street and into the shops. He said that by the second of January he could hear a few of them again in the trees.

Something else is happening, underneath the money and the maps and the numbered koalas. The eye adapts. It adapts the way your body adapts to cold water: the first second is unbearable and the sixtieth is just where you are now. A red sky in the middle of the day was a terrifying thing to stand under. It was on every screen for a week. And a thing that is on every screen for a week is on its way to becoming ordinary, not because anyone decided to stop caring, but because that is what looking does over time. It domesticates. The astonishing becomes the familiar becomes the wallpaper.

<PullQuote>
Spectacle is not the cure for not-seeing. Spectacle may be how not-seeing survives being shown the truth.
</PullQuote>

It is the middle of January. The fires are still burning. It is genuinely too soon to say whether any of this lasts, whether the giving and the belief harden into something that outlives the smoke or evaporate with it. Maybe this time is different. But notice that the hopeful story has already quietly moved its own goalposts. It promised that seeing would be enough. Seeing has now happened, at a scale we could not have engineered on purpose. And here we already are, asking whether it will be enough, which is the question you only ask when you have started to suspect it will not.

The smoke from these fires is expected to make one full circuit of the earth and come back to the sky it started from, thinner, higher, but the same smoke. When the teenagers came off the ship that carried them out, one of them said the smallest thing. She said she wanted to put all the clothes in the washing machine. To wash the day out of the fabric. To have things be normal again. And also, if you follow that wish far enough, the whole problem. The people who stood by the water will never get that sky out of their eyes. The rest of us are already learning how to look away. When the smoke comes back around, and it will, the only question that matters is whether we look up and see a warning, or whether by then it just looks like weather.
