In late April 2025, Duolingo posted its chief executive's all-hands email where anyone could read it: the company would be AI-first. Two of its lines were really addressed to managers. AI use would count in performance reviews, and a team could add a person only if it could not automate more of its work. Those are not tool questions. They are judgment calls, handed to whoever stands between the memo and the desk.

There is a real reason to count the clicks. Leave adoption unmeasured and the licenses a company paid for become private experiments, while the drift gets called culture. Counted, the number starts as a flashlight: it finds the team that never opened the tool. It turns into a tax the moment anyone reads it as proof the work got better.

The click is the one thing on the dashboard you can see. Judgment is not. So the manager is left coaching the quiet users the software has already nudged, proving a negative to justify a single new hire, and writing a review line about AI use that cannot tell whether one task came out any good. By the middle of 2026, 47 percent of American workers said their organization had integrated AI tools, up six points in a quarter. Inside the companies that had, only 12 percent strongly agreed it had changed how the work actually gets done. The tool got used. The bill for the gap goes to the manager.

You can require the click. The judgment that made it worth clicking is the whole job, and no one has found the box to check for that.
