I keep trying to decide whether the story is a happy one, and I keep failing.

Here is the story. A college senior, twenty-two, sits at a kitchen table in New Hampshire. She studied economics and environmental science, which is to say she did the assigned things and did them well. Over her last year she applied to hundreds of jobs. Each one took a few hours, because she tailored the resume and the cover letter to the posting, the way you are told to. By February she had heard back from not one company. Not a rejection. Nothing. After the three hundredth application she opened PowerPoint, of all the programs, and rebuilt her resume as a promotional slide deck, with emojis and a joke about a school grant to model nuclear reactors: "I'm, like, super cool. I'm basically Oppenheimer." She filmed herself doing it. Ninety-four seconds. She posted it with a crying emoji and the caption "guys it's just a silly video," under a still frame that read PLEASE HIRE ME above her, prayer-hands emoji beside it, in a room full of hanging plants.

The video passed half a million views. It brought fifty-two coffee chats, twenty referrals, ten interviews, and a full-time job at a company that installs solar power. "Instagram truly is the new LinkedIn," she said afterward, half joking.

That is where the story wants to end, and if it ended there I would like it. Person beats machine. But she said one more thing, and it is the part I cannot put down. She said the video was not enough. She said she was already qualified, and still had to go through a long process to get hired. And when she announced the job, she thanked, by name, the person who had connected her to the company.

So the video did not get her the job. Something else did, and it wore the video like a coat. I have spent a while trying to name the something else, because I think it is the whole point, and I think it is worse than the happy version.

Start with the silence, because the silence is not an accident. When you send an application into a modern portal, you are not dropping a letter in a mailbox. You are feeding a machine that turns a person into fields: a parsed job title, a set of keyword hits, a source label, a yes or a no to a screening question. On one large hiring platform the average opening drew two hundred and twenty-two applications in early 2024, close to three times what it had been at the end of 2021. No hiring team can give two hundred and twenty-two applicants equal attention. So some of the reading is done, or half done, by software that, depending on how it is set up, can reject you, rank you, label you, or hide you before a person weighs in. Answer one screening question wrong and some systems will file you under a tab called Disqualified, in the pile marked Applied, and send you a warm note thanking you for your interest. No person rejected you. No person will. The machine can be working exactly as configured and still produce a silence that feels insane from the inside.

It felt insane to more than one of them. Another student in the same market applied to nearly thirty internships and figured she spent about eight hours on each, two hundred and forty hours, most of a working month, poured into a slot that mostly answered with nothing. A salutatorian from one graduating class, a year out, described the hunt as throwing cooked spaghetti at a wall until your arms get tired. A computer science major kept a spreadsheet and colored her rejections red; a third of the tiles went red and most of the rest never answered at all, and she named the thing the silence teaches everyone eventually: you start to wonder if it's a you problem. It usually is not. In that stretch, more than four in ten recent graduates were underemployed, working jobs that never asked for the degree they had just spent four years earning. In the environmental field the senior had studied, the number was worse than half.

Now the video makes sense, but not as magic. Watch what it actually did. It did not delete the machine or leap over it. It produced fifty-two coffee chats and twenty referrals, so it produced people, and people often walk you back into the same kind of system you were drowning in, only this time wearing a label the software treats as gold. On many systems a reviewer can start with a priority group before touching the rest of the pile, and referrals can sit right at the top of that group. The platform that hosts both the job posting and the map of everyone you know publishes the payoff without embarrassment: you are 3.6 times more likely to be hired by a company if you are already connected with someone who works there. Connections, it says, are becoming an increasingly valuable signal. The same platform that turned your resume into a public profile is telling you the resume is not the thing.

This is the part people cheer, and I understand why. It looks like ingenuity beating bureaucracy. A person the machine could not see made herself impossible to ignore, and it worked. But what she had to become to do it, and what got measured, is where the story turns. By her own account she was qualified, and she still had to go through a long hiring process. What the video changed was the order of things. Being watchable, and being willing to be watched, bought her access before anyone had read whether she was good at economics or at modeling reactors. Those are real skills, though the job had advertised for different ones.

Here I owe the other side its strongest version, because it is strong. Sometimes the performance is the work sample. A laid-off creative in another city built a slide deck that was mostly her own face and her own jokes, sent it up to senior executives, and landed a senior creative job; for a role whose whole product is voice and nerve, the deck was the audition, not a stunt. A veteran recruiter calls these videos extensions of the resume, and she is right that a short clip can show initiative and warmth and quickness where a bulleted page can only claim them. When a comment section fills with strangers chanting hire her, and one of them forwards the clip to a friend who happens to be a recruiter, the crowd has done the routing the portal refused to do. For jobs that are themselves public performance, the public pitch is fair evidence. Even a skeptic at one big job board put it well: a video can be the cherry on top, but only if the ice cream is already the flavor they want. The video is a supplement to the resume, useful only when the qualifications are already there.

Concede all of that, and the problem does not go away. It sharpens. Because the trend is not that a few communications majors now audition on camera. It is that the audition is leaking into everything, including work that has nothing to do with being seen, and an audition measures different things than a job does. It measures whether you read well on video, which one study found cuts against confident women in particular. It measures your public self, which in one study of recruiters rating applicants' profiles did not predict job performance at all, and carried a bias the authors told employers to be wary of. And it measures whether you were willing to hand the interview your face, your voice, your humor, your off hours, before anyone had agreed to pay you.

Then there is the number I cannot stop looking at, the one that turns the feel-good story cold. When people were surveyed about using even dating apps to find work, the ones doing it were not the desperate poor scrambling for any edge. It was the reverse. Among dating-app users earning under fifty thousand dollars, twenty-seven percent worked the apps for career leads. Among those earning over two hundred thousand, forty-seven percent did. The informal route is used most by the people who already have the social pool, the practiced confidence, and the free time to experiment outside the form. It hands them a running start toward the one advantage the whole market now rewards, the referral that a resume alone can no longer buy. The performance looks like hustle. It measures access.

And the wins we repeat are counted without the losses laid beside them. A pitch to a sports company drew more than six hundred thousand views and a chorus of hired ASAP, and no reported job. A video aimed at one employer drew fifty-five thousand views and no reported interview. The student who logged two hundred and forty hours applying posted her own video and got exactly one company to answer, a first-round interview she had not yet turned into work when the reporter found her. Applause is not an offer. Views are not hires. The kitchen-table video that became a solar job is the one that gets retold. The videos that only got watched do not.

A career coach in the reporting said the plain thing, and it has stayed with me: you shouldn't have to become an influencer to get a job. But the quiet argument of the whole system is that increasingly you do, or at least that the ones who will are pulling ahead of the ones who won't, and that this gap has almost nothing to do with who is good at the work.

Here is what I finally think the something else was. The resume was a claim about the past. It said: here is what I have done, check it if you like. What is replacing it is a claim about attention. It says: here is proof I can make someone look. Those are not the same test, and we have quietly switched from grading the first to grading the second, and told ourselves it is the same exam because sometimes the same people pass both.

I still cannot decide if the story is a happy one. She is good at the work, and she got the job, and she also had to prove something the job never needed, and she was lucky enough and comfortable enough and connected enough to prove it. What I keep thinking about is the person on the other side of that engineered silence, the one who is also good at the work, and who reads the caption "guys it's just a silly video" and understands, correctly, that it is not silly at all. It is the toll. And decides they cannot pay it. They do not get a rejection. That was never on offer. They just stay quiet, and to a system that now rewards being watched, quiet and gone have become the same thing.
