The internet does not feel like a place. That is the whole trick of it.

We even chose a word to be sure of it. We call it the cloud, and a cloud has no address. It has no weight you could lift, no door you could stand in front of, no length you could measure. It is just there, the way weather is there, the way a voice is there the second you call someone you love. You no more picture how the voice arrives than you picture the air holding it up.

On January 15, for about 105,000 people, it went dark.

A volcano called Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai erupted in the Pacific and sent a tsunami over the islands of Tonga, and within a couple of hours the whole country fell off the internet. Not slowed. Off. Engineers watching from the other side of the world saw the traffic drop to almost nothing, then a spike of routing messages as Tonga dropped off the map the rest of the internet uses to reach it. It stayed that way for 38 days. And the thing that finally fixed it was not a patch or a server or a line of code. It was a ship.

We experience the internet as wireless, instant, and everywhere. When it broke for an entire nation, the repair had to be hauled up off the seabed by a boat that was, at the moment the volcano blew, more than four thousand kilometers away. How does a thing we feel as weightless come down to a ship hunting for a break in the dark?

To see the answer you have to do something we almost never do, which is picture where the internet actually is.

Start at your hand. Your phone talks to a router. The router talks to a cable in the wall, which meets a cable in the street, which runs to a building on a coast called a landing station. And from that building, the internet leaves the land. It goes into the ocean as a bundle of glass fibers wrapped in armor, a cable about as thick as a garden hose, lying on the seabed and running for hundreds or thousands of kilometers in the cold and the black until it climbs ashore in another country. That is the cloud. It has a shoreline. Roughly ninety-nine percent of the world's communication between countries goes through lines like these, not through satellites in the sky. The sky carries a small fraction. The floor of the ocean carries almost all of it.

Now here is the part that made Tonga different. Cable faults are common, and most of the time nobody feels one, because the traffic simply reroutes to another cable. Reliability is high when there are many paths. Tonga had one. A single line, 827 kilometers long, running to Fiji, where it joined the rest of the world. One road in and out for an entire country. On an ordinary day that one line carried nearly all of Tonga's contact with the world: the internet, the international calls, the card payments, the bank transfers sent home by relatives abroad, and, above all, the sound of family voices across the water. More Tongans live overseas than live in Tonga. For the roughly 150,000 of them abroad, that single glass thread was not "the internet." It was the way you heard your mother.

The volcano did not neatly cut the cable. It shattered it. The crew had expected to find two clean breaks. Instead, the cable company said, the line had been cut into pieces, probably four or five separate cuts, with fragments scattered across the seabed. In the middle of the repair, on February 16, the company gave a sense of the mess: the eruption had torn roughly 80 kilometers of cable into pieces, moved one section 5 kilometers from where it belonged, buried another under 30 centimeters of silt, and left more than 55 kilometers of it lost on the bottom, in water up to two and a half kilometers deep.

Fixing this is not abstract work. A ship drops a hook and drags the bottom to catch the cable. It lifts one end to the deck. A technician strips the armor back to the bare glass and splices the fibers by hand, a job that can take most of a day for a single joint, then wraps it, tests it, and lowers it back down. Tonga ran out of cable partway through and had to borrow lengths from neighbors in the Pacific, some of it from New Caledonia. The ship worked for twenty days and replaced a 92 kilometer section with new cable. Count how many times you check your phone in a single hour. Now stretch the gap between one check and the next reaching anyone across thirty-eight days.

That is what the cloud is made of. But the mechanism is only the setup for the harder question, and that question is about people, not glass.

Because a backup existed. This is the detail everyone skips. It was not total silence for 38 days. As the ash cleared, a thin satellite link came up and carried some international calls again, with a warning: call volume was high and capacity was tiny, so people should try, and try again.

Try again. Hold that ordinary phrase against a real week.

A Tongan man in Australia was on a call with relatives who were showing him the volcano. In his words, they heard the explosion and the big bang, and then everything went dark. The next thing to reach him was a tsunami warning. Then nothing, for days, no direct word at all about whether the people he loved were alive. When the thin satellite line finally opened, it did not open for him. His daughter made one hundred phone calls, through the day and into the night, before a single one connected. The call that got through reached his aunties, his mother's sisters, at three in the morning.

One hundred calls. That number measures how far a family will keep dialing before a line answers. And notice what it proves. The backup was there. It still felt exactly like absence.

An engineer would tell you the cloud is not secretly fragile. What failed was more specific: one remote island, connected by one cable, hit by an exceptional eruption and tsunami near its only route. The lesson, they would say, is boring and practical: build a second cable or a second landing station, keep a real satellite fallback ready, pre-arrange bandwidth, and shorten the time it takes a repair ship to arrive. Undersea fiber was never the mistake. It was the fix. Before that cable went live in 2013, Tonga limped along on satellite links so slow and costly that simple files took hours to download; the cable cut the household price of a gigabyte by about sixty percent. Redundancy, they would add, is not free, and it is especially not free for a nation of a hundred thousand people. Countries pay a premium to be repaired first. All of this is true. Every word of it. If the question is "what should Tonga's telecom planners do next," the boring answer is the right answer, and I will not pretend otherwise.

But watch what the boring answer cannot explain.

Tonga had already run this exact drill. In 2019, the same cable was cut, not by a volcano but by a passing ship's anchor dragged across the seabed, and the country went dark for twelve days. They had seen it. They knew the shape of the risk down to the specific glass line. Afterward they even signed a fifteen-year deal for satellite backup. The rehearsal happened. The plan was made. And when the cable broke again three years later, a daughter still dialed one hundred times to hear her aunties at three in the morning. The engineering answer accounts for why there was one cable. It does not account for why, with the risk fully known, a whole population and its diaspora lived every ordinary day as if the line were not there at all.

That gap is the real subject. The practical failure was redundancy; the human failure was that years of reliability had taught people to mistake reachability for nearness. And the thing standing in the gap is not a cable. It is comfort.

A working system erases itself. This is what smoothness does to us, and it is not a flaw in Tonga; it is a flaw in people. When a video call connects on the first try every day for years, you stop experiencing it as a signal traveling 827 kilometers along a thread on the seafloor. You stop experiencing the cable at all. What you feel instead is your mother, present, a tap away, as available as the air in the room. One young woman in the capital put it exactly right after the connection came back: social media had made everyone feel their families were, in her words, one click away. So when the click stopped, the question underneath was "what now?"

<PullQuote>
We had built a way to feel a person's presence, and we had quietly mistaken it for the presence.
</PullQuote>

That is the weakness of the cloud we never guard against. When you think about keeping your digital life safe, you picture passwords, codes, a padlock icon, threats coming at you through the air. You never picture a ship. The real exposure is not that the line can be cut. The line could always be cut. It is that the comfort of the line trains you, every single frictionless day, to stop believing the line is there. The smoother the system runs, the more completely the dependency disappears from view, and the larger the drop when it finally gives. Comfort is not the reward for a reliable supply line. Comfort is the vulnerability. It is the thing that guarantees you will be standing at three in the morning with a phone in your hand, dialing a number that used to just work, learning for the first time how far away the person on the other end has always been.

On February 22, after 38 days, the cable came back. Traffic climbed toward normal a little after midnight, and the phone company announced that data was restored. But read the announcement closely and the comfort story writes its own ending. Service returned to the main island and to 'Eua, where most people live. The domestic cable to the outer islands, to Ha'apai and Vava'u, was still lying broken on the seabed, with an estimate of six to nine more months before the domestic cable could be repaired. For them the line is still a thing you can see, because it is still gone.

The young woman in the capital said that on the night it came back, not a single Tongan slept early; people stayed up to hear voices they had not heard in weeks. By now, six days on, the email runs fast again and the voices come through clear again. The thread on the ocean floor has gone back to being weather, back to being air, back to being a voice that is simply there when you call. Which means the forgetting has already started. It always does. The cloud is only ever one break away from having a shoreline again, and we are only ever one comfortable week away from forgetting it ever did.
