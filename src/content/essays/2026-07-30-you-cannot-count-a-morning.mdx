I keep returning to one scene from a magazine's tour of the building, and I keep changing my mind about what it proves.

A reporter sits in a poolside lounge chair on a terrace twenty-five floors above Lower Manhattan, looking out at the harbor. The space around the chair used to hold a bank's computers. That is the whole picture: a person at rest, a long view, and under it the ghost of a machine. You are supposed to feel that something dead has been brought back to life, and mostly I do. Then I remember the reporter is only visiting, and I catch myself wondering what I would find if I came back on an ordinary Tuesday and went looking for a home.

The building is real. It went up at the end of the 1960s on Water Street as a bank's operations center: twenty-two floors built to hold computers and the people who fed them paper. Almost half of the ground floor was a truck dock. Minibuses carried checks in from around the city, freight elevators took the heavy loads, a conveyor moved the small ones. The floors were wide and deep, two hundred and seventy feet by a hundred and ten, broken only by columns. It ran all day and all night, every day of the week, because the work never stopped and because the district around it emptied at night and on the weekends. It kept a cafeteria in the basement big enough to feed five thousand people a shift, because there was nowhere else down there to eat.

And it had almost no windows. This was deliberate. An architecture magazine explained the reasoning in 1970, in a sentence I cannot stop reading: since computers cannot look out of windows, and the machine operators could enjoy the view only on their breaks, the building did not need much glass. Windows, it said, were "distinct liabilities." They let in heat the air conditioning had to fight. So the wall became a grid of narrow slits, two thin windows per structural bay, and from the street the whole thing looked like a punch card, which is more or less what it was.

Here is the part I keep circling. To turn that building into apartments, they did not divide the floors into rooms and rent them out. They cut two open shafts straight down through the middle of the tower, so daylight and air could reach the center. They tore off much of the outer wall and hung windows where the punch card had been. They stacked ten new floors on top. Two years, eight hundred million dollars, and at the end of it, 1,320 apartments where the machines had been.

If empty offices were housing waiting to happen, none of that would be necessary. You would move in the beds. A building you have to cut open to make livable was never a house in hiding. It was the wrong animal.

There are easy explanations for all that cutting, and each one stops short of the truth.

The first is that this was one strange building. It was not. The difficulty is the rule. One large architecture firm screened more than thirteen hundred office towers and found only a quarter to a third were even suitable for conversion. A brokerage went looking for the easy candidates, the ones mostly empty with small floors, built before 1980, and found they were barely one percent of the office space on the market. The good building is the exception. Most towers fight back.

The second easy explanation is money. Conversions are expensive, and with the right tax break they pencil out. That much is true. Model most of these projects on paper and they lose money; one careful study of eighteen buildings found sixteen came out negative once the work was priced in. But the cost is a symptom. The reason it runs so high is physical. The floors are too deep for light to reach the middle. The old wall was mostly solid, pierced by slits instead of windows. The plumbing ran to a few big restrooms, not to hundreds of kitchens and baths. You are not paying to decorate. You are paying to overrule the building's body.

Which is the third explanation, and the real one. The tower was not built for living. It was built for arriving and leaving. Its unit of time was the shift. Everything about it assumed a tide: a shift arrives, the machines run, the shift leaves, and the streets go quiet until the next one comes. A home runs on a different unit. Not the shift but the repeat: the same window every morning, the same walk for milk, the same faces, the light you left on before you went out. The office was engineered, on purpose and well, for a kind of life that is very nearly the opposite of the one we now ask it to hold.

I want to give the other side its best case, because it is strong. These towers sit on land the city already built for people. The subways run under them, the water mains are in, the jobs and shops are a short walk away. Reusing the shell instead of knocking it down keeps decades of poured concrete, and the carbon buried in it, out of the landfill; a brand-new building can take decades of use just to work off the carbon it cost to raise. A conversion can reach tenants in about two years, where tearing down and building fresh can take five. And in the right downtown the effect is not marginal. One set of models found conversions could roughly double the number of people living in downtown Pittsburgh. None of that is small, and none of it is wrong.

All of that is real, and the order of things matters here: the units come first. A resident signs the lease before the block fills in, before a store finds it worth staying open past six, before any of the daily life that will, or will not, gather at the door. So counting units is not the error. Stopping at the count is. A unit is not a home, the way a hospital bed is not a recovery: it is where one might happen. We have decided a new downtown apartment is a plain good, so we tally them. Ninety thousand in the national pipeline at the start of this year, up more than a quarter in a year, nearly four times what it was in 2022. The number climbs and we clap. But the conversion that counts is the slower one, the one that turns a work district into a place a person can repeat a life in, and it is invisible to the number we celebrate.

Be exact about the number, because the number is doing the persuading. It comes from the industry, from firms that track construction, and it counts what is "in the pipeline," which is not the same as what people live in. In 2024 that pipeline held about fifty-five thousand apartments. By the end of the year, a little under four thousand had actually been finished. The rest were drawings, permits, gutted floors, intentions. Most of the boom is a waiting room. We are counting plans and reading them as roofs.

Even the finished ones raise the harder question, the one the count cannot ask. On the reported tour, you can watch the building admit where it still cannot make ordinary rooms. The rooms that need daylight get pushed out to the new windows and the light wells. The rooms that don't, the bowling alley and the karaoke room and the children's playroom, go underground, into the dark the building has in surplus. Apartments are sold from inside a model kitchen that doubles as the leasing office, one room playing house while it closes a deal. Studios start around thirty-four hundred dollars a month; the units near the top ask twelve thousand. About a quarter are set aside as affordable, required by the tax break that made the math work. A market-rate tenant can sign a lease very quickly. The people who qualify for the affordable units go through a lottery and a chain of city checks that, in the developer's own words, take a very long time.

I don't think any of that is villainy. It is what happens when you can measure one half of a job and not the other. The half you can measure, cutting the shafts, hanging the glass, stacking the floors, is hard, exacting work, and they did it well. The half you can't measure is whether, on an ordinary Tuesday in February, the block outside is a neighborhood or a lobby.

Because the apartment is the smallest part of a home. The rest is the life it sits inside, and that part cannot be poured or clad. It is a shopkeeper who knows your face. It is somewhere to buy an onion at eight at night. It is other people's lit windows across a courtyard, so that coming home does not feel like boarding a docked ship. The best proof I have seen that a converted building is actually inhabited is not in any brochure. It is in the online reviews of a converted tower in Kansas City, where residents complain that the elevators are too slow, that a gate went up in the alley, that there is nowhere outside to stand and smoke. That is what a home sounds like: friction, small rules, the wear of ordinary use. You cannot render it in advance. You wait for it to build up, or you watch it fail to.

Some of these places will get there. Lower Manhattan passed seventy thousand residents last year for the first time, and ninety new storefronts opened, most of them selling food, which is the humblest and truest sign that people now sleep nearby and get hungry at night. A district built to empty out after the workday is filling in, slowly, after dark. It can be done. But it is the slower conversion, longer and far less photogenic than the first, and no one is counting it.

The one thing that building always had, from the day the first computers arrived, is the view. The harbor was out there in 1969 too. The operators just weren't allowed to want it. Now a person sits on the terrace and looks at the same water, and it is genuinely better than a punch card, and it is also the easiest thing to give. A view comes with the height. A neighbor does not. They cut the building open and let the daylight in, which is the part you can do with money and steel. The harder opening, the one that turns a floor you are permitted to sleep on into a place you would choose to wake up in for years, is not something anyone can carve. It has to grow, out on the street, after the workday, in the hours the building was first designed to keep empty.

So I keep the reporter on the terrace in mind, at rest above the water in a chair set where the machines used to run. A reporter, by definition, is only passing through. The open question is the one behind the chair: whether the people signing leases in the floors below are moving into a home or into a very good view. The daylight is in. The rest is not something you can count yet. I don't think the building knows. I am not sure we do either.
