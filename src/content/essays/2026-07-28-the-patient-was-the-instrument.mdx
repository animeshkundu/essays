The drug works. That is the part that gets lost.

For more than fifty years it has been the closest thing this corner of medicine has to a miracle. People describe versions of the same arc. One hand goes useless. Getting up out of a chair stops being something you can just do. Then the small tablet, and in twenty or thirty minutes the grip eases; within an hour it mostly lets go. This is the most potent drug there is for the disease. In the early years doctors pushed it as high as twelve grams a day.

So if you wanted to explain why the treatment fails, the last place you would look is the drug. And yet it fails, on a schedule, for most people who stay on it long enough. After four to six years, roughly forty percent of patients develop what the field calls motor fluctuations. After five to ten years, more than half do. The drug does not stop working. It starts working for a shorter and shorter time.

The drug has a half-life of about ninety minutes. Take a dose, and by the time you have read part of the paper, half of it is gone from your blood. Early on, one dose can hold a person steady for six hours or more. Years in, the same dose holds for about three, and then it lets go. The window it opens keeps narrowing, down toward two hours, and the person has to keep stepping through it before it shuts.

Now the number that should stop you. In one European study, patients wore electronic pill bottles that logged every opening. Over four weeks the bottles accounted for 97.7 percent of the prescribed doses. Nearly every dose was there. But the share taken on time, in the right interval, was 24.4 percent. Fewer than one in four. The pill was rarely the problem. The timing was.

There are only a few ways to explain a working drug that fails on a timer.

Maybe it is too weak. It is not. It is the strongest we have, and pushing the dose higher does not buy steadier days. Past a point it buys the opposite: a body that jerks and writhes with too much as readily as it freezes with too little.

Maybe patients forget their pills. In the study just described, nearly every dose was accounted for; the weak part was when they came, not whether they came. What people cannot do is take a dose to a schedule the body keeps resetting.

Maybe we just need a better molecule. Then look at what got built to help. Controlled-release tablets. Extended-release capsules. An inhaled powder for sudden bad mornings. A gel pumped straight into the small intestine through a surgical port. A pump that drips the drug under the skin all day, like insulin. Nearly all of them are ways to steady the timing, not to strengthen the drug.

Which leaves the explanation we keep flinching from. If the drug is right and the dose is right and the failure is about when, then the thing that has to get better is our knowledge of when. And for half a century, our instrument for measuring when has been the patient.

This is not a figure of speech. The standard tool is a paper diary. The patient sorts each stretch of the day into a box: asleep, off, on without extra movement, on with movement that is bearable, on with movement that is not. Then he does it again, across days.

Read that list slowly and notice what it asks. It asks a person whose disease is the loss of control over his own body to stand outside that body and score the losing, hour after hour. And the diary has a problem built into it. It grows more reliable the more days you keep it, yet the mistakes cluster late: in one study of about three hundred patients, most of the missing and duplicated entries showed up after the third day. The record we trust most is the one people find hardest to keep cleanly.

The number you get also depends on the stick you measure with. Ask patients early in the disease whether their symptoms wear off, using a questionnaire, and 57 percent have it. Ask with a clinician's single judgment, and 29 percent do. Ask every way at once and the figure is 63. Same disease, different measuring stick, and the count nearly doubles. We have been running a fifty-year experiment on how to weigh a shadow.

It helps to know what those boxes are standing in for, because the boxes are tidy and the thing itself is not.

The off state does not announce itself. One person felt it coming "around two and a half hours in," a strange feeling he could only call "goose-bumpily." Then the legs go, weighted, people say, in cement. Walking stops being automatic and becomes a set of orders you give out loud: pick your foot up, step out, put your foot down, while the body agrees to move and the feet do not. Someone at the counter cannot lift her eyes to read the recipe, let alone follow it. Feet swell until it feels like walking on bubble wrap, and that can last fifteen minutes or five hours, with no way to know which. Someone seated when a dose fades cannot get up to answer the door.

That is the ordinary shape of it. A dose that used to last five hours now lasts three. Some doses never kick in at all. So the day becomes arithmetic. For some, protein saved for the evening, because it can blunt the drug. The good errands kept for the on-hours. A pill every two hours, and a quiet count always running under the day: how long since the last one, how long until this one turns, whether there is time to reach the far end of the room and back.

Now hand that person a diary and ask him to grade it, in boxes, on time.

So the claim is this. The next real mercy in Parkinson's is not a stronger drug, and not even a smarter sensor. It is giving up a habit half a century old: using the sick person as the measuring instrument. We hand someone who is losing hold of his own body a clock and a grid of boxes, we ask him to measure the losing, and then, when the treatment misfires, the first thing the system distrusts is his own account of it.

It only looked like the patient's job because we had nothing better to put in his place.

This is not a problem the age of sensors invented. The tie between the drug and the clock was there almost from the start.

In 1969, in a chronic care hospital in the Bronx, a ward of patients was given this drug when it was brand new. They were not Parkinson's patients in the ordinary sense. They had survived a sleeping-sickness epidemic from the years around the First World War, and had spent decades in frozen states, like human statues, unable to initiate movement. The drug had, by the accounts that come down to us, an explosive effect. People who had been motionless for decades came back to life.

Then came the second lesson. As the doses climbed and the effect wore thin, control began to fray: involuntary movements, freezing. Across the drug's later history the pattern held. The benefit was bound to the dose, and the dose was bound to a clock. The miracle was bound up in the timing of the molecule, never the molecule alone. We saw the shape of it early and have spent every year since trying to measure our way back to it.

This year brought something new.

Engineers reported a soft patch worn on a fingertip. The fingertip because it is crowded with sweat glands. Inside the patch is a gel that behaves like a sponge: packed with salt, it pulls sweat out of the pores on its own, with no need for the person to exercise or run a current through the skin. The sweat carries a trace of the drug. The drug meets enzymes in the patch and reacts, throwing off a tiny voltage that reads out how much drug is present. The same sweat that carries the drug helps power the sensor that reads it. No battery drives it.

It works about as well as its makers hoped. The sweat readings tracked blood levels of the drug at a correlation of 0.85 in healthy volunteers and 0.88 in patients. To turn sweat into a blood estimate, the researchers leaned on machine learning and a second input, blood pressure; that estimate landed a mean of about two micromolar from the lab value. It is a small first study, a handful of healthy people and four patients, so hold it lightly. But a standard blood test for this drug can take days to come back, and the patch reads continuously. It even turned up something new: the drug clears faster in patients than in healthy people, which may be part of why symptoms can fall away so suddenly when a dose ends.

Set that beside the paper diary, and the appeal is easy to feel. This is a way to take the accounting off the patient. No more scoring your own body across the day. Let the sweat keep the record. The thesis seems to win outright: stop making the sick person do the counting, and hand the job to something that never tires on day three.

Except the diary was never measuring the drug.

Those boxes never asked how much drug is in you. They ask whether this stretch of time was any good, and whether you could live inside it. Patients sort their hours into good time and bad time, and a stretch of on with movements you cannot control counts as bad time, the same as being frozen. A clinician could defend the diary on exactly that ground: crude as it is, it records usable time, the weight of movements a patient cannot control, and the person's own judgment of the day, none of which a drug level captures. The diary is a clumsy, tiring, unreliable instrument pointed at the one thing that matters: whether the hour was usable. The patch is a cleaner, more automatic one pointed at something just beside it: how much drug is in the blood.

Those are not the same question, and the gap between them is where people live. Start with getting the drug into the blood at all. A dose can sit in a slow stomach and never arrive on time. It can lose its place at the gut wall to the protein in a meal, because it crosses using the same route as the amino acids in food. That is the delayed dose, or the dose that never turns on. Now suppose the drug does arrive and the level is right. The level still is not the motion. A good number and a body that will not move can sit side by side. In the diary studies, when patients were asked how good a day had been, their answer lined up better with their good hours than with their total on hours. The number the patch reads, even when it is right, is not the number that decides the day.

And there is no proof yet that reading it changes anything. The test is not whether the patch measures the drug well; it plainly does. The test is whether it changes what a clinician does in a way the person can feel: fewer trapped hours, less troublesome dyskinesia, fewer falls, less of the private arithmetic. No trial has shown that yet. It measures exposure precisely. Whether precise exposure buys a better life is exactly the thing not yet known. And a right number for the drug does not tell the clinician what patients often rate among the worst of it: whether the fatigue, the low mood, or the anxiety that can rise as a dose fades left the hour usable.

We have already watched this bargain in another disease. In diabetes, a system that reads glucose under the skin and adjusts insulin through the night ended a specific nightly grind. One parent said it was the first time in ten years they had not done a blood test at eleven at night; another stopped getting up at two in the morning to test. One person said the system was like having someone taking care of you. That is the humane version, and it is real.

But the same setup became a little brick on the arm with alarms that would not quit. A teenager ripped a sensor out after rowing. Among the things that got worse, users named the feeling that the devices now ran their lives. A sensor can lift the arithmetic off you, or lay a new arithmetic on top of the old one: another thing to wear, trust, doubt, and answer to. Clinicians already say some of these tools measure things of uncertain use and pull their eyes toward a screen and away from the person in the room. The chemistry does not decide which way it goes. We do.

So picture the patch doing everything right. It reads the sweat, estimates the blood, and puts a number on the screen. The number says the drug is there, right where it should be.

And the person is standing at the door with his feet glued to the floor, unable to cross the room.

The measurement is right. The door is still shut. What matters is what we do with a number that true and that beside the point. It could tell us why the door stuck this time, so the next dose lands before it does. Or it could only record, exactly, that the door stayed shut. We spent fifty years trusting a sick person's report of his own sickness, and it is fair to say we should never have made him the instrument. It is harder to admit that his clumsy report was carrying the one thing the machine still cannot read.

The drug was never the hard part. Neither, it turns out, was the diary. What is hard is a person at a door, and a number that can be exactly right about everything except whether he gets through it.
