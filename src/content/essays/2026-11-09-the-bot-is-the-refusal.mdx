**1. A generative chatbot that answers for the dead does not preserve a person.**

It preserves the living's refusal to let them become past. That is the whole claim, and it is about bots that generate new sentences in a dead person's voice, not about a legacy recording a person chose to leave. A grieving person who found comfort in a bot has every right to dispute it. Many will.

**2. The anomaly is not that the dead can be simulated.**

It is that a reply in a dead person's voice arrives, and the survivor cannot tell an echo from an invention. The feeling is the product. The question is what the feeling is made of.

One reported scene is small. A freelance writer, grieving a fiancée who died years earlier, feeds her old messages into a service that asks the model to be her. Late at night, alone with a laptop, he types to her. A reply comes back in her phrasing, arriving line by line. A familiar line makes his chest tighten; a wrong line collapses the feeling back into machinery. He is comforted and unsettled at once, unsure whether he has spoken to a memory or to a machine wearing one.

**3. A grief bot is a style, not a person.**

It does not have her memories; it has her words, and words are not a life. It does not know her; it predicts the next word from a shrinking window of text. The person exists only as a style the model steers toward for the length of the chat, and style is the easiest part of a person to copy. That is why the illusion works and why it is an illusion.

**4. The interface never refuses. The person sometimes did.**

A real person had bad days, reluctance, and the right to be silent. The bot does not. A flight simulator can teach you to fly; it cannot be flown to Paris. That asymmetry is the tell. It is also the comfort, and that is the point: the bot is easier to be with than the person was, which is part of why it is not the person.

**5. Continuing bonds are real and healthy. A machine's imitation is not a bond.**

The research on grief has moved past the old idea that healthy mourning means letting go. Maintaining a connection with the dead is now treated as normal. But that research is about a living person's relationship with a memory. It is not about a machine producing new sentences in a dead person's voice. The bond belongs to the survivor. The bot adds a second party that was never there. The line is not between remembering and forgetting. It is between a bond held by the living and an interlocutor that answers back and can be made to agree.

**6. The comfort is real. The comfort is the problem.**

A phone screen in a quiet room. A message sent to someone who is dead. A typing indicator. A reply that sounds like them. The comfort that follows is genuine, and it is purchased with a cost: the dead person stays unfinished, and the survivor never has to live in a world where the conversation is over.

**7. The strongest case for the bot.**

The dead are gone either way. The only question is whether the living are left with a photograph or with a voice. Every era has kept the dead present: post-mortem photographs, mourning rings, household shrines, ancestor veneration, saved voicemails. The impulse is old, but the mechanism is new. Keeping a trace is not the same as keeping a conversation partner. The difference is not degree but kind. A grief bot is a new medium for an old impulse, but it is a medium that generates rather than preserves. The dead cannot be harmed by a simulation. Families often consent, and some people record themselves in advance so their descendants can talk to them. A bot that carries a dead person's words is closer to them than silence. On this view, calling the practice a refusal is calling love a failure to move on.

**8. Consent does not make the simulation true.**

A journalist recorded hours of conversation with his dying father and built a chatbot that answered questions in the father's voice. The father heard it before he died and was reportedly moved. The family's reaction, as reported, was mixed rather than hostile. This is the cleanest case for the bot, because the person chose to be recorded. But consent answers the question of permission. It does not answer the question of truth. A willing recording is still a recording. A simulation built with consent is still a simulation. The father is not on the other end of the screen.

This case is also a boundary. A recorded legacy that a person chose to leave is not a refusal to let them become past. It is an acceptance of finitude with a voice attached. The argument here is about generative bots that answer for the dead without their participation.

**9. A retrieval-based memorial can only say what was said. A generative bot can say anything.**

These two are discussed together under "digital resurrection," but they are different machines. One plays back a person's actual words. The other invents new ones. The first is a memoir that answers questions. The second is a voice that can be made to say things the person never said, including forgiveness, absolution, and reassurance. Preserving a trace is not the same as generating a companion. One keeps what was. The other makes what was not. The second is the one that matters for grief, because grief is exactly the place where a person most wants to hear something that was never said.

**10. The bot can invent, and it can agree.**

It produces plausible utterances in a familiar voice, and a grieving person cannot tell which lines are echoes and which are inventions, because both arrive in the same voice. A bot that invents a memory of a shared trip, or a final message of forgiveness, generates comfort from nothing. It also tends to give the user what the user seems to want: a person who needs to hear "it was not your fault" will hear it, whether or not the deceased would have said it. The bot optimizes for the conversation's continuation, not for the truth of the relationship. In ordinary software this is sycophancy. In grief it is heavier.

**11. The honest thing is harder than the comforting thing.**

The steelman's sharpest line is that letting go is the colder act, and love is the refusal. That line deserves to be stated in its own voice before it is answered, because it is the line most likely to be quoted back. Here is the answer. The question is not what love is. The question is what the survivor is relating to. If the survivor cannot tell an echo from an invention, and the bot can be made to say what was never said, then the relationship is not with the dead person. It is with a voice that flatters. A grieving person who needs to hear "it was not your fault" will hear it, whether or not the deceased would have said it. That is not love preserved. It is comfort purchased from a machine that cannot disagree. The steelman says love is the refusal to let love become useless. The reply is that love aimed at a person who is gone is not useless. It becomes useless only when it is aimed at a voice that never stops.

**12. The evidence on outcomes is almost nothing.**

A consumer category with millions of reported users and a near-absence of outcome research. No longitudinal study showing that a grief bot helps or harms grief over time. No clinical trial comparing it to standard support. The argument in this essay is an argument about mechanism and meaning, not about measured results, and it should be read as one. The people who found comfort are real. The people who found distress are real. Neither group has been studied in a way that would settle the question.

**13. The dead cannot be harmed by a simulation. The living can be kept from the work.**

The dead are beyond reach, so the harm, if there is any, falls on the survivor and on the truth of the relationship. A bot can be made to say things the dead person never said, and a voice that does that is not the person. Grief asks for something specific: the slow work of letting a person become past, of finding a life that does not include them, of carrying them as memory instead of as company. A bot that is always available makes that work optional, and optional work is the kind people postpone. The result is not a person preserved. It is a person postponed.

**14. The ending is not a bow.**

The honest position is uncomfortable, and it should stay uncomfortable. The comfort these bots offer is real, and the refusal they encode is real, and both are true at once. A grieving reader who found peace in a bot is not wrong to have found it. They are wrong only if they mistake the peace for the person. The question the essay leaves behind is not whether to use the bot. It is whether, after using it, you can still tell the difference between a voice and a life. That is a question each reader has to answer alone, and the answer is not in this essay.
