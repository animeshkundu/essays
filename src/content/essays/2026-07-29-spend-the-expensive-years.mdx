A 24-year-old Meta engineer, earning a reported $306,500, sleeps in a room with a bed, a blanket, a pillow, and a filing cabinet for a nightstand, and rebuilds his budget spreadsheet from scratch every month so his money has the most years to grow. The planners are right about him, and not only about the dollars: a dollar skipped at 30 is that dollar plus thirty-plus years of growth it never got to do, and skill, reputation, and the colleagues who would vouch for you all compound on that same early schedule. Now set beside him a woman who, by her own account, saved $1.5 million and left a $390,000 job at 35, not from burnout but because her partner is seventeen years older.

Her spreadsheet could price the salary and the forgone growth to the last franc, and not one hour of what she left for: a healthy year with him now rather than a comfortable one when he is much older. Both are disciplined savers who simply disagree about which asset is scarcest, and that disagreement is theirs to settle only because they hold a surplus most households never will.

So for the few who actually get to choose, always guarding the money is not the safe default it poses as; it is a bet on your own scarcity, placed with the one asset that cannot compound and can only run out, and never once admitted to be a bet.
