In July 2009, a 17-year-old student in Michigan opened his Kindle and found that his purchased copy of *1984* was gone, along with the notes he had typed into it for a school assignment. Amazon had remotely deleted it. He sued. The case settled. That small removal is the whole argument in miniature: a buyer paid for a book, wrote in it, and woke up to find it had been taken back without a knock on the door.

The first thing to notice is what did not happen. There was no break-in, no lost package, no scratched disc. The book did not wear out. It was removed by the same company that sold it, over the network, after the money had changed hands. Treat it as an accident and you miss the point. The deletion was not a malfunction. It was the system working as designed, doing exactly what the contract allowed. The real question is why a system that calls itself a store is built to take back what it sells. The point is not that a company misbehaved. It is that the system is built to take back what it sells, and the word "buy" on the screen is doing work the contract underneath cannot support.

Start from the word "buy." A physical purchase transfers a copy the buyer controls. The seller cannot reach into the house and remove it. The first-sale doctrine applies. A digital "purchase" does not transfer a copy in that sense. It transfers a permission. The file on the device is encrypted; the key lives on the platform's server; the buyer's right to open it is a row in the platform's entitlement database. If that row changes, the buyer's access changes, no matter what the button said.

Decompose "buying" into five rights and the picture sharpens.

- Possession: does not transfer. The buyer holds an encrypted copy; the usable form depends on a key the platform controls.
- Access: transfers conditionally. The buyer gains the right to render the content on authorized devices, subject to account standing, device limits, and server availability. This is the right actually exercised day to day, and it is the right that can be revoked.
- Transfer: does not transfer. Licenses are personal and non-transferable: no mainstream store lets a buyer resell, transfer, or move a purchase to another person's account, and a purchase generally cannot be carried to another ecosystem either.
- Inheritance: does not transfer. Licenses terminate at death in practice. A few platforms offer family sharing or legacy tools, but the license itself does not pass to heirs as property.
- Revocation: retained by the platform. The terms of service keep an explicit right to remove, modify, suspend, or discontinue content and service.

At "purchase" the buyer gains one conditional, revocable right, access, and never receives the other four. The money buys access dressed up as ownership. Call this the permission model: what you buy is not a copy, it is permission to use a copy that someone else keeps. The test of any purchase is simple. Ask who holds the master copy and the key. Where the buyer holds both, ownership holds. Where the platform holds both, the buyer holds a license wearing a buy button.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Four tools do the work.

1. Server-side entitlement check. The content is encrypted; opening it requires a license token or decryption key from the entitlement server. The app asks whether this account, on this device, is entitled to this title. A "no" locks the content without touching the device's storage. This is the default for ebooks, apps, and most streamed media.
2. Device authorization. Licenses are bound to a limited number of devices. A buyer who exceeds the limit must deauthorize one, and the platform can trigger deauthorization, instantly removing access on that device.
3. Remote wipe. The platform pushes a delete command to the device. This was the tool in the 2009 Kindle deletion: the book and the annotations typed into it vanished together.
4. Store or service shutdown. When a platform exits a business, it shuts down the entitlement servers. The encrypted local copies become unreadable because the key stops being issued. Nothing is deleted from the device; the key simply stops coming.

Apply the definition to three cases and a near-miss, and see whether it holds.

**Microsoft ebook store closure, 2019.** Microsoft launched an ebook store in 2017 inside the Microsoft Store. On April 2, 2019 it announced the store would close; by July 2019 the purchased books were removed from libraries and the files stopped opening, because the DRM keys were revoked. Buyers received full refunds, routed to the original payment method or to Microsoft account credit where that method was no longer available. Buyers who had made annotations or highlights received an additional $25 credit, explicitly framed as compensation for the lost notes. The mechanism is tool four: shutdown. The human cost is a library that goes blank and a company pricing a lifetime of marginalia at $25. The gap between the "Buy" button and the help page that explained the books were "licensed" is the whole mechanism in one receipt.

**Ubisoft and *The Crew*, 2024.** Ubisoft shut down the servers for *The Crew* on March 31, 2024. The game required an online connection even for single-player, so owners could no longer launch a game they had paid for, in some cases years earlier. The game was also delisted from stores. No compensation was reported for owners of the base game. The mechanism is tool four again, but aimed at a game: the bytes sat on the drive while the permission to run them expired. The felt moment is an error message where the title screen should be, and the discovery that "owning" the download meant nothing once the server was the thing being bought. The shutdown became the rallying case for the "Stop Killing Games" campaign, which argues that publishers should not be able to render purchased games unplayable.

**Sony and purchased Discovery content, 2023.** In December 2023 Sony announced that purchased Discovery films and shows would be removed from PlayStation libraries because of licensing arrangements between Sony and the content owner, with removal set for December 31, 2023. Subsequent reporting said Sony reached an updated agreement and the content would remain available. The exact final status is unverified here, and that uncertainty is worth keeping in view: a buyer in December 2023 did not know whether movie night would work, and the decision was being made between two companies the buyer was not a party to. The buyer paid; the two companies negotiated; the buyer's library was the bargaining chip.

**Near-miss: where ownership genuinely held.** GOG sells PC games as DRM-free installers. A buyer who downloads the installer keeps the game forever, even if the title is later delisted from the store. The installer runs without a server check, so a store closure does not strand the purchase. Bandcamp delivers purchased music as downloadable files the buyer keeps permanently; even after Bandcamp changed owners, previously purchased downloads remained with buyers. Physical media is the baseline: a DVD or a printed book cannot be recalled by its seller. The disc may scratch and the paper may burn, but no one can reach into the house and take it back. The near-miss proves the withdrawal is not technically inevitable. It is a business choice about who holds the master copy and whether an online check is required. Where the seller gives the buyer the file and removes the check, ownership holds. Where the seller keeps the master copy and gates access, it does not.

Now the strongest version of the other side, stated without hedge. Permanent ownership was always a burden disguised as a right. What a buyer actually wants is not a disc or a file but the experience of the work, and a cloud system delivers that experience more reliably than any object a person can hold. A book on a shelf burns, warps, and goes out of print; a film on a disc scratches, rots, and is locked to one region and one player; a game on a cartridge depends on hardware that stops being manufactured. A license that lives on a server survives fire, flood, theft, and the death of the device it was first opened on. It syncs to wherever the buyer is, remembers where they left off, and updates itself without their effort. The buyer is not losing ownership; they are being freed from managing a thing. The word "buy" on a screen is shorthand for "I will make this available to you for as long as I can," and that is a more honest and more durable promise than any deed to a piece of plastic ever was. Permanent ownership is an industrial-age expectation carried into a network age, and the fragility people feel when a title disappears is the friction of a transition, not a defect of the new model. This position was most influentially stated in a 2009 *Wired* essay titled "Better Than Owning," by Kevin Kelly, which argued that ownership is a poor proxy for use and that access is always current, always available, and never decays.

The steelman is strong on three points, and it should be conceded. Durability against physical loss is a real and large advantage: fire, flood, theft, and decay are risks a cloud library does not carry. Cross-device sync and automatic updates are genuine quality-of-life improvements that physical media cannot match. The breadth of a modern catalog is purchased with revocability; a store that promised forever would be smaller and more expensive.

But the steelman answers the wrong question in three places.

First, durability answers "what if I lose my stuff" and not "what if the store takes it." These are different risks, and access trades the first for the second. Access may well be more reliable for the average title on an average day; the argument here is not that it never is. It is that the withdrawal clause is hidden, unlabeled, and unmeasured, so the buyer cannot weigh the two risks. A buyer who prefers cloud backup still has a stake in whether the cloud can revoke. Counting only the first risk is incomplete.

Second, revealed preference does not establish informed consent. Streaming dominates recorded music revenue, and subscription libraries are popular, especially among younger buyers. But a buyer who prefers a subscription may still expect a title they paid for outright to be permanent. "People like streaming" and "people understand that buy means license" are separate claims, and only the first is well supported.

Third, "buy is shorthand" is a claim about language, and it is contested. The button says "Buy." The receipt says "Purchase." The library view calls the item yours. The price is a purchase price, not a rental fee. None of these say "license" in the place the buyer looks. The terms of service do say it, in fine print almost no one reads. The argument that buyers knowingly accept terms treats the button label as a user-interface convenience and the contract as the real instrument. That is a coherent position, but it is not the same as saying no one was misled. The two claims should be kept separate.

The business-realities argument explains why a store cannot promise forever, and it is right about that. A store does not hold the copyright to the works it sells; it holds a license from the publisher, time-limited and territory-limited. When a publisher withdraws a title or lets a license lapse, the store's right to distribute ends. A store that promised "forever" would be promising something it does not control. Refund programs, like Microsoft's 2019 closure, are the honest settlement of a license that could not be completed. But explaining why a store cannot promise forever is not the same as justifying the word "buy" while making that promise. The explanation and the labeling are separate issues, and the second one is where the buyer's complaint actually lives.

There is a reason this keeps happening, and it is not bad luck. A 2017 study by Perzanowski and Hoofnagle surveyed roughly 1,300 consumers about a hypothetical digital "purchase" and found that a large majority believed clicking "buy" conferred ownership-style rights, keeping the item permanently, transferring it, lending it, reselling it, and were surprised to learn the transaction was in fact a license that could be revoked. Even where terms of service are displayed, consumers do not read them, and the word on the button dominates the word in the fine print. If most buyers misread the button, then every revocation is not an edge case. It is the terms working as written against a buyer who was never told.

One genuine uncertainty should be named plainly. In the 2009 Kindle deletion, the book itself was removed in every account. Whether the annotations were deleted outright along with it, or survived as orphaned notes separated from the text, is not resolved here. The difference matters for the felt detail of the scene, a teenager's own thinking disappearing along with the book, or surviving without the text it belonged to. The essay does not commit to either version.

There is also a cost that no one is counting. Platforms do not publish counts of affected buyers, and no aggregate dollar figure for withdrawn access exists in any source. Refunds, where offered, return money, not the use, the notes, or the years. The absence of a number is itself a finding: the withdrawal of paid access is not tracked anywhere as a loss, which is part of why it keeps happening.

So here is the test, and it is the only thing to take with you. When you click "buy" on a screen, ask one question: if the store vanished tomorrow, would the thing still open? Ask who can still reach in and take it back. If the answer is anyone but you, you did not buy it. You bought permission, and permission is the kind of thing that can be withdrawn after the money changes hands. The question does not tell you whether to click. It tells you what you are clicking on. The word "buy" is doing work it cannot stand behind, and that is the question worth sitting with the next time a library you thought was yours goes quiet.
