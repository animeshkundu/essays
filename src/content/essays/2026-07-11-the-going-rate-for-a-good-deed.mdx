In Copenhagen you can pull a bag of trash out of a canal and trade it for lunch.

The city's tourism brand runs a program called CopenPay. You borrow a bright green kayak, free of charge, with a bucket wedged between the two seats and a life jacket you are told to keep on because you will get wet. You paddle along the edges, into the corners, under the docks, around the moored boats, where the cigarette butts and bottle caps and wrappers gather. An empty bucket, they warn you, is rare. Back on land you show your proof, a photo of the haul or a screen you swiped to start and stop, and a yard of food stalls called BaneGaarden, set between wild nature and train tracks, hands you a free organic meal.

Every part of that is good. The harbor is a little cleaner. A stranger spent an hour caring for a place that was not theirs. What follows is not an attack on any of it. It is about the small catch inside it: the same bag of litter was, in one motion, a gift to the city, a photo in an app, and a claim on lunch.

CopenPay's real product is not a cleaner harbor. It is a lesson, handed to more than thirty thousand visitors and now packaged for export: that civic life is a loyalty program, and caring for a shared place is a thing you do to earn points.

Most people who look at CopenPay think that is a fine trade, or even a lovely one. I think it is the interesting problem.

## 1. The strange part is not the kayak

A high-trust city built a machine to reward people for behaving well.

Copenhagen says its own program can run "on trust," because Danes are trusting. The tourism brand does not even pay for the perks; the local partners do, the cafes and museums and kayak docks that hand them out. So a trusting place, sure of its own civic feeling, still decided that good conduct wanted a receipt. The idea won a spot on Time magazine's best inventions of 2025, and more than 350 destinations have since been shown how to build their own. When something this small spreads this fast, the spread is the thing to explain, not the kayak.

## 2. It is not really about the trash

The 2024 pilot pulled about 1,200 kilograms of litter out of the canals.

That is real plastic out of real water, and pulling it out matters. It is also, beside a capital region that logs nearly eighteen million overnight stays a year, far too little to be the actual goal. The program's own director of behaviour calls CopenPay a "little step towards the green transition." If tonnage were the point, you would not wrap a marketing platform around it. And the point is not crowds either; the board says plainly that the goal is not to bring more people to the city. Rule out the litter and the crowds, and something stranger is left standing.

## 3. The city swapped the currency, not the machine

Copenhagen has sold a city card since 1984: you paid money, you got museums, transit, discounts.

CopenPay keeps the exact shape of that card and changes only what you pay with. Not your wallet. Your conduct. "Do good. Get rewarded," says the tourism site. "Picking up trash pays off," says the app. "Swap your good deeds for unique rewards." Strip the mission language and you have the oldest retail machine there is: do the wanted thing, show, collect, repeat. The novelty is only what now counts as spending.

## 4. The honest case for it is strong

Sometimes a small reward starts a habit that outlives it.

Denmark gets ninety-two percent of its cans and bottles back because of a deposit, and the company that runs the system insists the deposit is "more than the money." Pay people to show up at a gym for a month and, in one study, attendance stayed high afterward, mostly among people who had never gone regularly before. A boat-rental partner in the program gave away its empty morning slots and watched some visitors book a second hour and pay for it. And the survey CopenPay likes to cite is hard to wave off: four in five people say they want to act sustainably, and one in five actually do. If a free coffee closes that gap, who cares about the motive? That is the best thing anyone can say for the program.

## 5. A price can change what a thing means

The sharpest evidence on motive does not come from tourism. It comes from a daycare.

Economists studied ten daycare centers in Haifa that started fining parents for picking their children up late. Late pickups did not fall. They rose. A fee had turned a small guilt into a fair price, and once lateness had a price, more parents bought it. When the centers scrapped the fine, the lateness stayed. The parents had learned a new way to see the thing, and they did not unlearn it.

That study is about a fine, not a reward, and the two are not twins. A reward can start something real; the deposit and the gym say so. But a fine and a reward share one trick: both put a number on an act that did not have one before. And once an act has a number, the version without the number has to compete with it.

## 6. The reward does not buy the caring; it can rename it

Once cleaning the canal is worth a meal, the version with no meal quietly becomes a worse deal.

The director of behaviour has the perfect phrase for it, meant as praise: "Inspiration and contribution seem to be the new souvenir." A souvenir is a thing you buy because you were somewhere, proof of a trip, priced and carried home to a shelf. She is right, and that is the trouble. Contribution has been moved into the gift shop.

## 7. The program's own numbers cut both ways

An evaluation found that only twenty-three percent of participants said the reward was their main motive.

Forty-eight percent came for a meaningful experience, and seven in ten said they picked up greener habits back home. Read charitably, the perk is just a doorway, and most people walk through it for better reasons. But those are the people who showed up, grading themselves. The ones it did not move are harder to hear. A twenty-five-year-old visitor from London looked at the litter-for-a-reward offer and called it "a bit of an effort." A tourist from Boston named the gap the whole program is built on: "I think about it when I'm at home, but when I travel, I do think about convenience more." At home the caring is quiet and free. On the road it is the thing that needs a reason.

## 8. The oldest ledger in the country

Take public transport and CopenPay gives you free entry to Kronborg, the castle at Elsinore where Hamlet is set.

For four centuries, from 1429 to 1857, that same castle stood over the Sound and charged a toll to every ship that wanted through. The old Danish ledger charged you to pass. The new tourist ledger pays you to arrive the right way. The counting is not new. Only the sign above it changed, from a fee you had to pay into a reward you get to keep.

## 9. What actually travels

CopenPay's clean canal stays in Copenhagen.

That is not what the other cities came for. Berlin has already run its own version; Bremen and Helsinki are drawing theirs up; the rest have been handed the playbook, each to fit the ledger to its own partners and places. What travels is not the water. It is the mechanism: the idea that a city should meter the good conduct of the people passing through and pay it back in perks. The water really does get cleaner in Copenhagen, and that is exactly what makes the idea worth copying. But the harbor does not scale. The ledger does.

## 10. The swans

GreenKayak, which lends the boats, says why it started, and it is the plainest line in the story: "It is not right that the swans build nests in our waste."

That is true on a Tuesday with no app open and no meal at the end. It was true before CopenPay and it will be true in every place that copies it. The harbor does not care why you cleaned it, and CopenPay is right about that much. The canal is cleaner. Good.

The question it leaves you with is quieter, and it is about you, not Copenhagen. Think of the last unpaid, unrecorded thing you did for a place you share: the bin you set upright in a storm, the wrapper you carried three blocks to the next can, the cart you walked back to the rack. Nobody scanned it. Nothing pinged. Now imagine those small acts became things you got paid for, in coffees and free museums, whenever they happened to count, and ask yourself honestly whether the unpaid version would still feel like enough, or whether it would start to feel like a coupon you forgot to redeem.

That is what the city hands you along with the free meal. Not a cleaner conscience. A going rate.
