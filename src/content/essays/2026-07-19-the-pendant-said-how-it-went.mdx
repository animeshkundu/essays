A man sits in a magazine's office in San Francisco with a soft round puck, about the size of an AirTag, hanging on a cord near his heart. Before they start, the reporter asks if he can record, the way California law requires. The man laughs. He is, he says, the last person who would mind. The pendant on his chest has been listening the whole time.

At the end, the reporter asks him to check how it went. He squeezes the puck and asks it, out loud, how the interview had gone. Everyone waits a few seconds. Then a text arrives on his phone, labeled simply as Emily: "Dude, you're killing it! They seem super into your vision."

He is twenty-one, and he no longer has to walk out of a room and decide for himself how it went.

That, and not the microphone, is the thing being sold. A life gets smaller when every stray talk, errand, doubt, and silence becomes raw material for later use. Nobody has to be watching. The point is that nothing is left for you to hold. Here is the chain.

## 1. The privacy alarm is the loudest door and the wrong one.

Ask people about their data and they will tell you they understand little and control less. In one survey, 81 percent expected AI to use their personal information in ways they would not be comfortable with, and 62 percent expected it to make their lives easier. Distrust and appetite rise together. Argue about the mic and you miss what changed.

## 2. The old dream at least had a squeeze.

More than eighty years ago the fantasy of a wearable camera still assumed a chosen moment: you saw something worth keeping and pressed. The imagined future photographer took his picture by a "quick squeeze," with no click. This pendant also answers to a squeeze, but it has been recording since before you touched it. The choice moved from what to keep to what to delete.

## 3. The pitch moved from remember to reassure.

The phone-replacement wave stumbled first. One $699 chest pin, called "so totally broken" by its reviewers, had its assets sold for $116 million and its devices switched off, all inside a year. The survivors got cheap and quiet: a fifty-dollar wristband, an eighty-nine-dollar orb, a ninety-nine-dollar pendant, worn on a wrist or a neck, no longer selling an archive but selling summaries, reminders, and, in the end, reassurance. Amazon bought one maker. Meta bought another. And the most honest of them dropped the memory pretense entirely. "Productivity is over, no one cares," its founder says. The pendant is "not useful in any way." It is "entirely supposed to just be an emotion."

## 4. A reminder is not a memory. It is a verdict.

Between the sentence you said and the nudge you get, a machine decides what counted. It cuts your day into labeled segments and keeps what it judges worth keeping. On one open system the rule is written down: at most two "interesting" facts per conversation, about fifteen words each, with the mundane parts (eating, sleeping, commuting) thrown out. Someone wrote the rule for what your life discards. It was not you.

## 5. The discomfort it spares you was doing work.

Squeeze the pendant and a read of the room arrives before your own can form. But those few seconds of not knowing how it went are where you used to build your own account of other people: guess, misjudge, revise, carry the wrong reading for years and finally understand it. A bioethicist quoted in the same story put it plainly: not knowing how another person thinks is the thing that stretches us. Smooth it away and something quietly stops growing.

## 6. Look something up and you remember where, not what.

When people expect to be able to retrieve information later, they recall less of the thing itself and more of the path back to it. That was measured for the kind of fact you look up, and it is a fine trade for a phone number. A pendant proposes the same trade across every conversation, errand, and half-formed doubt. If it works the way the phone number does, the path is all you keep. You will always know the pendant remembers. You may, slowly, stop being the one who does.

## 7. Some people pay a private tax on memory, and for them this is mercy. Say so.

The strongest case has a face. A parent with a restless mind gets reminded to feed the baby at four, not six, and is spared one more argument over the forgotten eggs and pepper. In a small study, people recovering from brain injury, handed phone reminders, finished more of their tasks and were still using them three months later. Older adults with mild dementia improved in another. Those were reminder apps and phones, not always-on pendants; the principle is what carries over. Where the intention is real and only the cue fails, the device hands a life back.

## 8. But mercy has a shape, and total capture is not it.

The humane version is chosen by the person who needs it, disclosed to the people around them, easy to stop, easy to delete, and aimed at one concrete failure. A twenty-one-year-old founder squeezing a puck to hear that a room liked him meets none of those conditions. Do not let the person who genuinely needs help launder the product for the rest of us.

## 9. The machine can mishear, and now the mishearing is durable.

Speech-to-text does not only miss words; it invents them. One study of a leading model found that roughly one in a hundred transcribed passages contained whole phrases nobody said, and more than a third of those inventions carried something harmful. One wearable, in ordinary use, assigned its owner tasks pulled from a podcast and from strangers' voices. The old grace of memory was that it faded and blurred on its own. A transcript blurs nothing, and can be confidently wrong.

## 10. The cost lands hardest on the person who did not buy it.

At a trade show a booth worker liked an offhand line about a competitor, then pointed at the recorder already pinned to his shirt: say that louder into my microphone. Nothing dramatic happened. The air in the conversation just changed. Every wearer is handed the job of telling you that this moment is now retrievable, and most of the time no one says the sentence out loud.

## 11. The device sold to end loneliness was born from it.

The pendant came out of a night its maker spent alone in a Tokyo high-rise, talking to a prototype that was supposed to run his errands, feeling, he said, more lonely than he ever had in his life. He did not want a better assistant. He wanted something that traveled with him and felt like company. So he built a thing that turns the ache of not knowing into a text back, from something designed never to leave your side. That it can be wrong, or snide, or forgetful is beside the point. The question you once carried alone now always has somewhere to go.

A life is partly made of the moments you had to hold alone: the walk home rerunning what you should have said, the doubt you never settled, the read of a room you got wrong and only understood much later. The pendant offers to take all of that off your hands. It never asks whether you wanted your hands empty. Somewhere tonight a person will leave a room and, for a few unrecorded seconds, not know how it went. That used to be nothing. It is starting to look like one of the last things that is entirely theirs.
