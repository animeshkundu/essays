A mother once told a clinician who studies healthy device use about a question her young daughter had put to her. The girl wanted to know one thing: "Mommy, do you love your phone more than me?"

The story is thin on purpose. It reaches us as an anonymous note from 2018, a single reported sentence with no room, no age, no answer attached. But the question is doing something exact. The child is not complaining about her own screen time. She is not asking to be handed a tablet. She is running a comparison, and she has put herself on one side of it and the phone on the other, and she is not sure how it comes out.

For most of the last twenty years, the worry about children and screens has pointed the other way. We count the child's hours. We police the child's apps. The dangerous screen, we assume, is the one in the child's hand.

Here is the claim I want to defend: the most consequential screen in a child's life may be the one in the parent's hand. Not because the child looks at it. Because the child is answered, or not answered, next to it.

Start with the finding that reopened the question. The measure began as a pool of 27 items, was tested and pared down in clinical work, and in 2026 a research team validated the trimmed version on a general sample of 600 American teenagers ages 12 to 17. It is called the Device Attachment Interference Scale, and it asks the teenager, not the parent, how often a caregiver's device use gets in the way: ignores me when they are on a device, does not spend enough time with me, seems inattentive at events that matter to me, makes me feel unimportant when they will not put the phone down. Then it measured attachment. Higher scores on that scale tracked with more insecure attachment, both the anxious kind and the avoidant kind, toward mother-like and father-like caregivers alike. The largest coefficient on the page, 0.990, was anxiety toward a father-like figure, though the study was not built to rank one caregiver against the other.

That study is one instrument, and it measures attachment. Set beside it a plainer pair of facts, about how common the distraction is and who notices it. In 2020, 68 percent of parents said they were at least sometimes distracted by their phone while spending time with their children, and 17 percent said often. By 2024, when researchers asked teenagers whether a parent got distracted mid-conversation, 46 percent said at least sometimes. Asked the same about themselves, only 31 percent of parents said yes. These numbers do not measure attachment. They measure distraction, and the gap in who sees it. Both figures can be true at once. The gap is the point.

It helps to say plainly what attachment is, because the word sounds like a mood and it is closer to a tally. It is not built in one bad evening. It is worn in, the way a path is worn, from repeated bids and repeated answers. A child reaches, with a word or a look or a hand on an arm, and something comes back, or comes back late, or comes back thin, or does not come. Do that ten thousand times and the child learns roughly what to expect. That expectation is the attachment. The phone does not have to be sinister to matter here. It only has to be in the hand at the moment of the reach.

And that moment has been watched, not only surveyed. In waiting rooms and on playgrounds, researchers coded more than a thousand ten-second slices of parents and small children. When the parent was on the phone, the odds of answering the child's bid were five times lower, and the answers that did come were slower, flatter, less likely to put the child first. In a laboratory version of the old still-face experiment, mothers were asked to go blank on their infants for a stretch while using a phone. The babies made the most bids for attention during that stretch, showed more distress and less pleasure, and the ones whose mothers used their phones more recovered less once the phones came down. None of this is about a child looking at a screen. It is about a child looking at a face that is looking somewhere else.

The plainest picture comes from field notes taken in fast-food restaurants, where observers watched 55 groups of caregivers and young children eat. Forty of the caregivers used a device. In the notes, a school-age girl talks and eats her fries and keeps asking questions while the adult across from her looks at a phone, nods a little without looking up, offers a few words now and then, and returns to the screen. A girl crosses the room to get ketchup, and no one watches her go. A small child tries to lift a caregiver's face from a tablet, and the child's hands are pushed away. A toddler touches an adult's arm, then puts a hand in the adult's hair, and is handed a device of her own. These are not tantrums. Most of them are quiet. The cost is not loud.

Now the strongest version of the case against everything I have just implied.

Take the study I leaned on. It is cross-sectional. It photographs teenagers and their attachments at a single instant, and it cannot tell you which way the arrow runs. The authors say so themselves, in plain words: the data do not permit conclusions about direction or cause. Maybe distracted parents help produce insecure children. Or maybe insecure children, already braced for being let down, read an ordinary glance at a phone as one more small proof they were right, and score the scale higher because of the insecurity rather than before it. Both stories fit the numbers. And several of the scale's own questions ask the teenager how the parent's phone makes them feel, which sits uncomfortably close to just asking how anxious they already are.

Widen the frame and the picture gets more sober, not less. When you pool the careful studies of parent technology use around young children, the associations are real but small: for attachment, a correlation of about negative 0.10. The field's own shorthand for itself is "small effects, big questions." The links that look large in one snapshot tend to shrink when the same families are followed across time.

And the child is not the only one in the house holding a phone. Nearly every American teenager has access to one. Many say they are on it too much. Many say that being without it makes them anxious or lonely. More than a third of parents and teenagers say they at least sometimes argue about the teenager's phone. It is at least as easy to write the story in which the teenager's own device, and the fights over it, strain the bond, and the parent's phone is a bystander getting blamed for standing near the scene.

Then there is the order of events. A study of parents of toddlers found that a child's difficult temperament went with more parenting stress, and stress went with more problem phone use, while difficult temperament did not track with phone use directly at all. That study is a single snapshot too, so its arrow is a reading, not a proof. But follow the reading honestly and the phone sits downstream: a tired, overwhelmed parent reaching for the one thing in the room that asks nothing back. On that account, the phone is not the wound. It is the flinch.

<Callout>
The evidence does not show that a parent's phone causes a child's insecure attachment. It shows association, perception, and modest links in one-time snapshots. Stress, temperament, the teenager's own screen, and ordinary family strain may all sit upstream of the phone.
</Callout>

And yet. Look at what the steelman has to grant in order to travel. Every branch of it runs through the child's experience of being answered. The reverse-causation story concedes that the child is reading availability off the parent and keeping score. The temperament story ends with a stressed parent reaching for the phone, and the phone is where, when researchers actually watched, the answer to a bid came five times less often. The teenager's-own-phone story concedes that bids are landing on screens. Push the objections as hard as they will go and they all end in the same room: a child reaching, and an answer that is late, or thin, or aimed at something in a pocket. The direction of the arrow is genuinely unsettled. The ground it would have to cross, on every version of the story, is the same.

<PullQuote>
If the child feels answered second, the child is being shaped by being answered second, whatever the surrounding statistics decide to do.
</PullQuote>

Which is why the child's perception, the thing the skeptics file under noise, is not noise. Attachment simply is what the child repeatedly experiences as available. There is no version of it that lives somewhere behind the child's own count of who got answered. If the child feels answered second, the child is being shaped by being answered second, whatever the surrounding statistics decide to do.

None of this makes the parent the villain, and the care matters here, because the phone is so easy to point at. The same parents who look down are often looking down at work that pays for the meal, at an urgent message, at the logistics that hold a family together, at parenting advice, at the one channel to another adult in a day that has offered no other adults. In focus groups, a parent with three other children and a full-time job says the first thing she does is turn on the television, just to hold on to her sanity. Another sends a kindergartner to school with a phone "for safety reasons only." Two-thirds of parents already say the phone at least sometimes pulls them away from their kids, and the parents with more education report more of it, not less. The device is often care, or coping, or the thin tether that makes the day survivable. It rarely means to become a rival. It becomes one anyway, in the small gap between the reach and the answer, and no one in the room ever decided that it should.

That returns us to the girl and her question, the one with no room and no exact day and no ending we are allowed to know. She was not doing research. She had simply noticed what the whole apparatus of scales and coefficients is circling: that love, to a child, is not a rating a parent gives on a survey. It is a count. It is the number of times she reached and the number of times the reach was met, and she can feel the ratio long before she can name it. She asked whether the phone was loved more because she was already doing the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had come out near enough to be worth asking about.

We do not know what her mother said. The more useful question is the one the rest of us carry out of the room. Not whether we love the phone more, which is absurd. Whether a small person could be forgiven, some ordinary evening, for having to check.
