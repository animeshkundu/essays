On June 3, 2026, a crew went into a room on Orlando's International Drive and began taking a skatepark apart. For a decade it had been a Vans park: 61,000 square feet, a 40-foot wooden vert ramp, street courses, an above-ground concrete pool. It closed in January 2012. The interior sat there for years after, abandoned and still skateable, while the building around it changed hands and names. Now the ramps come down for good.

What goes in is a club. Eleven courts for padel and pickleball, a juice bar, a pro shop, fitness rooms, a members-only coworking lounge, a mezzanine widened into a bar with a warming kitchen. The courts and the classes and the events run on reservations and membership tiers. If the schedule holds, it opens in October.

This is being sold as a cure, and the cure is partly real. Adults socialize less than they did a decade ago and report more loneliness, and here is a bright, staffed, weatherproof room where strangers can meet every Tuesday, going into a building that has spent twenty years being remade, one leisure tenant after another. Look hard, though, at what gets carried out the door while the better lighting is wheeled in. The booked club is not a cheaper commons. It is a different good: belonging with the accident removed. A city that can build only the bookable kind gets healthier, busier, and quietly narrower, meeting more of the people it chose and fewer of the ones it did not.

**A third place is defined by what you do not have to do to get in.** The scholar who named the third place meant neutral ground: somewhere you go with no reason, no role, and no plan, where talk is the point and the regulars half-own the room. A reservation inverts every word of it. It wants a plan, a partner or a rated level, a payment, and a slot, all before you arrive. His own warning fits the trade: where the means of relaxation are not publicly shared, they become objects of private ownership and consumption.

**The format is stranger than the fee.** We keep saying cities need more places to gather, then treat the loneliness of people with nowhere to be by building rooms you cannot enter without already having somewhere to be at 7 p.m. The old skatepark charged too. It ran $170 summer camps, clinics for kids six to sixteen, contests with cash and shoes for prizes, and it split its own days into two-hour slots with rented boards and required pads. It was never a free commons. But it left more room than a booking tier does for the parts nobody pays for: watching from the gallery, lingering after, learning off a stranger, standing near people you did not choose.

**Blaming money misses the axis.** The contrast was never free versus paid, because both sides charge. It is loose time versus scheduled time: watching versus booking, lingering versus turnover, the room you drift into versus the slot you are sorted into. The old room sold you a two-hour slot and let the watching around it stay part of the day. The new club sells you the slot and then the lounge to linger in afterward.

**The building was never a commons.** Run the tenants in order. It opened in 2003 as Festival Bay, an entertainment mall that pitched itself as a friendly place to shop and relax and that visitors described as a near-empty warehouse. The Vans park lived inside it. Then came Artegon, an artisan market of chainlink stalls. Then Dezerland, billed as Florida's largest indoor attraction, with go-karts and a car museum. Now the racket club. Five managed leisure businesses in two decades, each one booked, priced, or ticketed in its own way. It was always managed private leisure, never an unpriced public commons. The honest question is which kind of market leaves the door widest.

**Empty shells explain the where, not the why.** Weak malls and dead anchors make cheap, fast conversions: a shuttered department store in Connecticut became 28 courts with showers and a cafe, and operators say they can turn a vacant box into a club in about 100 days against two years for new construction. That is why a padel court lands inside a former skatepark, and not why a reservation beat every other way of being near other people.

**Call it a fad and you miss the buildout.** One sports-industry survey put American pickleball at 24.3 million players in 2025, up from about four million in 2020. Padel is tiny here and enormous abroad: roughly 30 million players and 63,000 courts worldwide. Operators are signing leases, not testing a season.

**The reservation is a prosthetic.** Strip away the fee and the fad and one thing is left standing. The booking stands in for a skill many adults have lost, which is walking into an unstructured room alone and staying. It also collects the rent that every effortless commons runs on. The software does both at once. It matches you to a partner at your level, sells you the hour, and meters the space so the lights stay on. It works. That is exactly why the cost is easy to miss.

**The software sorts you before you arrive.** The platforms say so plainly: make your match public to find partners, join a match nearby, see the other player's level first. One booking company reports more than 105 million bookings across some 2,300 clubs. Membership tiers open the calendar 14 or 21 days out, hand you guest passes, and, at the top, a concierge. You meet people. You meet the people the filter returns.

**The same city sells belonging on a ladder.** In New York you can play five-dollar community pickleball on a public park rink, paddles thrown in, or you can join a private club whose door will not open to the public without a member and where a year runs $4,800. Both wear the label of the new third place. One of them your neighbor cannot afford to walk into.

**The booked room beats the dark one.** Against a dead anchor store and an evening given to the television, which fills more than half of the average American's leisure hours, a staffed court where strangers turn up every Tuesday is not the villain. It is the bill. These formats are built for the person who cannot arrive alone: sign up solo and get placed on a team, wear a hat backward, no collared shirt required. In one converted mall a father and son played backgammon for hours without touching a paddle, and a man sat on a couch and read a book while the games went on around him. That is a commons doing its oldest work.

**Do not romanticize the room that closed.** The free-feeling old places kept people out at the door long before an app could do it. Black travelers once needed a printed guide to find the gas stations and diners that would serve them. Service clubs wrote the exclusion of women into their charters. The booked club filters by price and comfort, with software; the fondly remembered one could filter by race, sex, class, and who your father knew. Nostalgia is not an argument.

**What survives the steelman is the accident.** Sorting is efficient, and sorting is the loss, and not only by skill. The system also sorts by price, by who booked three weeks out, by who is fluent in the app, by who has a free 7 p.m., by whether lingering is the reason you came or an amenity you paid for. The old concrete kept a beginners' corner for small children under the same roof as riders who traveled to contests, unsorted, because nothing stood between them to do the sorting. You learned off people you could not have booked. Even the backgammon players and the man with his book are lingering inside a club built on memberships and booked courts, where drifting is the amenity and not the door. The evidence shows these rooms produce repeated contact and fill dead buildings, not the lasting friendships the brochures imply. The reservation is very good at returning people like you, on time. It cannot hand you the stranger you did not know you needed.

**What you book, someone else can cancel.** The same walls, two tenants back, held that artisan market its sellers called a way of life. They learned it was closing at a meeting they thought was routine: sixty or seventy of them stood up and asked what, and no, and how, and why, and were given about two weeks to clear out. Belonging you rent by the slot belongs, in the end, to whoever owns the slot.

**The unbooked room is the one nobody pays to keep.** After 2012 the empty skatepark stayed skateable, and skaters kept hopping the fence to ride it until security ran them off. It was open, unscheduled, and free precisely because it was worth nothing to anyone. Those are the quiet terms of the trade. In a private leisure box like this one, free and open lasts only while nobody is paying to keep the room.

The crew will finish. The ramps and the concrete pool will be gone, the courts will go down clean and bright, and if the schedule holds you will be able to reserve one in October. A skater who filmed the goodbye that last winter wrote the plainest thing anyone has said about the place: "Vans is a place many of us grew up. we all made a lot of friends there." He did not book them. The city coming next will be busier and warmer and better lit than the one being carried out, and easy to love. The one thing it cannot sell you is the stranger you would never have chosen.
