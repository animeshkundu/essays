A woman has been asked to keep a two-week diary of her sleep, and she is making it with scissors.

She is twenty-seven. The clinic wanted the ordinary paper kind, a grid of nights to fill in. She does not fill it in from memory. She cuts the printouts from her sleep tracker into strips, trims them to fit the boxes, and pastes them down by hand, so that the record the clinic asked her for turns out to be the record her wristband had already made. The device has taken the place her own account was meant to fill.

She came in because she could not fall asleep and could not stay asleep, and because the wristband kept grading the nights and failing them: frequent restless sleep, a sleep efficiency of sixty percent. She believed the number. It matched how the mornings felt.

What follows is months of a clinic trying to make her feel better while barely moving the number. Her restless legs, once found and treated, quieted down; the sleep still felt broken. A stretch of deliberately shortened time in bed, the standard move to consolidate a night, did nothing after ten days. A home test for sleep apnea came back clear. She lost twenty pounds on purpose. Still light, still unrefreshing. Finally she spends a night in a sleep laboratory and undergoes an in-lab polysomnogram, the test that reads brain waves directly instead of inferring them from movement. It found no trouble with her breathing, a normal arousal rate, and more deep, slow, stage-three sleep, the very kind the wellness world tells you to chase, than the wristband's story of a broken night allowed. She had taken a long time to fall asleep; the night was not perfect. But the instrument built to see sleep did not see the failure her wristband kept reporting.

Her clinician gave her the good news. She had one question. "Then why does my Fitbit say I am sleeping poorly?"

Hold that, because it runs backwards. Offered the more accurate reading, taken from electrodes on her scalp, she reached instead for the guess made by a strip of plastic on her wrist. The wristband was the witness she trusted. The laboratory was the thing she had to argue with.

It is tempting to file this under gadgets being wrong and move on. That would be a mistake, because the strange part is not that the device disagreed with the lab. It is which one she believed.

Something has shifted in the last decade about who gets to say whether you slept. The boom in sleep scores is not really about rest. It is the quiet arrival, in the bedroom, of the logic of the performance review: the body is now asked to prove it recovered before it is allowed to feel recovered. And for a certain kind of person, the proving becomes the thing that ruins the sleep.

That is a claim worth disputing, and plenty of thoughtful people will, because a tracker is also just useful self-knowledge, a mirror you would be foolish to cover. So let me rule out the easy explanations first, the way the woman's doctors had to.

Start with the obvious one: the wristband was simply wrong. It half is. A wristband does not watch your brain. It watches your movement and your pulse and hands the pattern to a private algorithm that returns a verdict about stages it cannot actually see. When researchers set one Fitbit model, the Charge 3, against the lab in a study of thirty-nine adolescents, its sensitivity to deep sleep was about fifty-nine percent: when the electrodes were in a stretch of deep sleep, the wristband agreed a little over half the time. It is better at noticing that you are asleep than that you are awake, which means a person lying dead still in the dark, willing sleep to come, can be scored as sleeping soundly through the worst hour of the night. And the number people chase hardest, deep sleep, is close to the one these devices measure worst.

All true. And all beside the point for the woman making her diary, because the machine that told her she slept deeply was not the wristband. It was the lab. If her problem were only an inaccurate tracker, the accurate reading would have fixed it. It didn't. She heard the better instrument and asked why the worse one still said she was failing. A bad guess helped put the wristband in charge. But being in charge, not being wrong, is what kept her awake. The mechanism is authority.

The second explanation is fairer, and it should be said plainly, once. Nobody knows whether the tracker made these people anxious or only gave an existing anxiety a place to live. The clinicians who first described the pattern, and who coined a name for it in 2017, were careful to admit they could not tell whether their patients had worried about sleep before they ever strapped on a device, or found the worry by scrolling the data. Orthosomnia, they called it, straight or correct sleep, built on the same root as orthorexia, the unhealthy preoccupation with healthy eating. It is a proposed term, not a diagnosis. There is no checklist. The whole idea still rests on a small stack of cases.

Grant every bit of that, and the shape of the trouble still points one way. Across those cases the same things kept happening. The goal stopped being to feel rested and became a number to hit. The evidence people carried into the doctor's office was not their memory of the night but a screen. One woman filled out her paper diary not from what she recalled but from what the device had recorded, which is how a person ends up cutting a wristband's printout into strips and pasting it where her own account was supposed to go. And the fix they reached for, lying in bed longer to pad the total, is close to the worst thing an anxious sleeper can do. Whatever first lit the worry, it had reorganized itself around the score.

None of this stays in the clinic, though what spreads is the tracking, not necessarily the failure. In a survey of two thousand American adults, forty-eight percent said they had used a device to track their sleep, up from thirty-five percent two years before. More than half had changed their behavior because of what a tracker told them. And seventy-six percent, three in four, said they had lost sleep to worry about their sleep. The tracking is ordinary now, and so is the worry. The clinic word marks something narrower than either: not that most people are failing this way, but that the failure now has a shape, the morning the score outranks the body.

Here is what is left standing when the easy explanations are gone. The authority over your night has moved, and it has moved somewhere strange.

For most of the history of sleep advice, the advice was about conditions, not scores. The modern version, sleep hygiene, dates to the nineteen-seventies, and it reads like a list of chores: keep the same hours, cut the coffee earlier, make the room dark and cool, and if you have been lying awake too long, get up, because the bed should not become a place you practice failing. Old sleep advice told you what to do and then left you alone to do it. It did not wait by the bed with a clipboard to grade the result.

The score does. A popular one hands back a single number each morning and sorts it into words: ninety and up is excellent, the low eighties good, the sixties merely fair, and anything under sixty is stamped poor. You wake, before your eyes are fully open, and the first fact you learn about the day is a grade for something you did unconscious and cannot appeal. One writer testing this described a morning when the app gave the previous night a sixty-six, against a monthly average of eighty. The low number looked alarming until it was set against the plain facts of the night, which had been hot, with a loud neighbor. The verdict arrives before the context does.

This is where the measuring turns on the sleeper. Sleep is one of the few things you cannot do by trying. It comes when attention lets go and retreats the instant you watch for it. Clinicians have a name for the loop: the way effort and attention and the fear of not sleeping feed each other until the bed itself becomes a cue for lying there tense and awake. Every good instinct the score installs makes that worse. It teaches you to attend to your sleep, to want a particular result from it, to stay in bed longer to raise the total, to check in the morning to see whether you passed. Trying hard to sleep well for the number is a reliable way to stop sleeping at all. The measurement can manufacture the very failure it reports, and then report that too.

It does not even have to be true to do the work. In one experiment, people were told, at random, that they had gotten an above-average or a below-average night of sleep. The figure was invented. It changed how they performed on tests that morning anyway. A verdict on your sleep can run your day whether or not it describes your sleep. Now put that verdict on your wrist every morning, labeled poor to excellent.

Which is where the performance review comes fully into the bedroom. One of the people the clinicians described was a man of forty whose light, broken sleep had begun years before, during a change of jobs. He worked forty hours a week and then more on side projects in the evenings and on weekends, often right up to bedtime, and he slept with his phone beside him and answered the texts and emails that came in the night. He did not bring the clinic a diary either. He held up his phone and described his nights, in his own words, "according to my data." His symptoms, he said, showed up only on the days the tracker reported under eight hours. His goal for treatment was not to feel less tired. It was, in his phrasing, to achieve at least eight hours of deep sleep every night: a target, a quota, a figure to make. He had carried his job into bed with him, its metrics and its quiet assumption that an unproductive night is a personal failing, and he wanted the device to certify that he had hit his numbers. The trackers oblige that mood. They no longer just log sleep; they issue readiness scores and recovery scores, the vocabulary of athletes and factories, rest reframed as output you are on the hook to maximize.

Now the strongest case against all of this, because it is strong and it is also true. Sometimes the number is not a judge. Sometimes it is a clue, and the clue saves you.

A third patient, a man of sixty-nine, had started wearing a tracker his wife gave him and noticed it kept flagging restless stretches at night. He mentioned this to his neurologist. That led to a real sleep study, which found severe sleep apnea, fifty-four breathing events an hour. The wristband had not diagnosed anything, and it was in no position to; it had flagged restless nights, a clue, and the clue was enough to get him tested by something that could. He went on a breathing machine, used it faithfully, and credited the wristband with the nudge that got him tested. The device did what a good instrument should: it did not pronounce, it pointed him outside itself, toward a doctor and a test and a treatment. Later, tapering off a sleep drug, he found it steadying to watch the tracker show his sleep holding up without the pills.

And he is not a fluke. When researchers have handed trackers to people with insomnia, or paired the devices with a little coaching, the anxious spiral often does not appear; several studies found no rise in sleep worry at all, and some people slept better for the feedback. The makers, at their best, know the risk. One company's own help page, answering a user who feels rested but sees a low score, tells them in so many words to trust their body first. So the honest line is not that these things are poison. It is a distinction. A number that sends you back into your life, toward a pattern, a doctor, a fixable habit, is a clue, and clues are good. A number you have to satisfy before you are allowed to feel rested is a verdict, and the verdict is the thing that keeps you up.

The same wristband can be both. The man with apnea and the woman with the scissors wore, more or less, the same device. It rescued one of them and would not let go of the other.

Go back to her. The night in the lab was supposed to be the happy ending, the proof that her body had been doing its job the whole time. Instead it became one more thing to disbelieve. At the end she was referred to the standard talk therapy that treats exactly this loop, the one good answer the system had for her. She never went. Her insurance would not cover it. So she was left where she began, holding a wristband that told her she had failed at rest, against a laboratory that told her she had not, and trusting the one that made her feel worse.

Many readers will check a number about themselves tomorrow morning, before their eyes are fully open, on a wrist or a phone. If you are one of them, it is worth knowing what you are really about to find out, and whether, on the mornings the number and your body disagree, you can still say which one of them actually slept.
