In April, a 27-year-old in Monterey, California, drove to her post office box and found twenty parcels waiting.

Since the start of the year she had spent four thousand dollars on small furry dolls sold in sealed boxes. You do not pick the doll. You pay, you tear open the box, and only then do you find out which one you got. The dolls are called Labubu, beady eyes and a row of little pointed teeth, small enough to clip onto a handbag. She helps run her husband's business, and for a while she hid from him how much she was spending.

Standing in front of the stack, she said the accounting out loud. "I'm telling myself I want to fly to Europe, I want to travel the world, but how can I do that if I'm spending $4,000 on fur, cotton, vinyl, whatever these things are made of?"

On one side, a trip around the world. On the other, twenty parcels of fur and cotton.

The easy move is to decide she never grew up. A grown woman, thousands of dollars deep in cartoon monsters: what else could it be but a child who got a credit card. The trouble is that the people doing this are grown in every way you can measure. One buyer who ran herself out of gas money on these dolls was 55 and had just passed the California bar exam. Another, a collector in China with thousands of these toys on his shelves, was a practicing surgeon. And the format is old. Coin-operated capsule toys have been a fixture in Japan since the 1960s. Americans spent 1.4 billion dollars on Beanie Babies in 1998 alone, adult collectors lunging for understuffed animals with a heart-shaped tag. Buying small sealed chances is not a new failure. Calling it one explains nothing about why it is happening again, now, at this size.

The next easy move is to say they are being played. That one is partly true. Say it plainly: Pop Mart, the company behind Labubu, wrote in its own 2020 prospectus that its toys are aimed at adults from 15 to 40 and that blind boxes drive repeat buying "due to unpredictability and fun." Popular restocks sell out within seconds. Resellers run bots. New drops arrive in live-shopping streams built to make you scramble to add one more to the cart. All real. But manipulation is a technique, not an appetite. It can sharpen a hunger and cash in on it, but it has to find the hunger already there. The question a bot cannot answer is why this particular bait works so well that the toy line these dolls belong to brought in about 14.2 billion yuan last year, more than the whole company earned the year before.

The last easy move is the kindest: the dolls are cute, and this is just comfort shopping. The buyers themselves talk you out of it. A 28-year-old in Ireland, spending up to 270 dollars a week, bought mystery plush toys from a brand she did not even like, opened one, and thought, "Why do I now have this squishy lobster?" A woman filmed herself buying a box and saying she would be happy with any figure except the Christmas tree, opened the Christmas tree, bought a second box, and got another Christmas tree. The bar-exam woman left some of her packages unopened. If this were love of a cute object, the unwanted lobster would not sting, the duplicate would not deflate, and a box left sealed would make no sense at all. The woman in Monterey said it exactly, staring at her shelf: "The feeling of wanting something you can't have is addicting, rather than the item itself. They're just cotton, they're just vinyl."

So the doll is not the point. What is left, once you clear away the toy, is the thing the toy is wrapped in: the few seconds before you know.

They are not paying for the doll. They are paying for one small unknown they chose on purpose, in a life where nearly every other unknown is either dangerous or has already been predicted for them.

We tell ourselves that adults crave certainty and children crave surprise. An ordinary adult week runs the other way. Your phone guesses your next word and your fastest way home. A feed forecasts the song you will want next. A score decides whether you can be trusted with a loan; an app charts your sleep, your cycle, your risk of the illness you fear. What stays genuinely unknown is mostly the frightening set: the layoff, the diagnosis, the rent you open with one eye closed. Against all of that, a sealed box is a strange kind of luxury. It is an unknown that, on its own, cannot hurt you, that you picked yourself, that resolves in a few seconds. That is not a child's toy. It is close to the only safe surprise a fully optimized adult can still buy.

Then notice what the most optimized people build around that one small unknown. A release time announced to the minute. A restock alert for a drop that lands at half past eight at night. A code scratched off the box and checked against a database to prove the toy is real. A resale chart. Bots fighting other bots for a plush the size of a palm. The full adult apparatus, trained on the one purchase they made precisely because it could not be known in advance.

People go a long way to stretch those seconds. A 33-year-old surgeon in China can guess what is inside a blind box by shaking it and listening to the toy knock against the cardboard; over two years he has put more than 100,000 yuan into them, thousands of little figures from a dozen different lines, and says the joy is not the toy but "the moment you tear open the box." A man trained to keep his hands still under pressure, using that training to draw out the one instant before he knows. A 29-year-old who works in finance put it this way: with salary cuts all around and a trip abroad out of reach, a blind box "brings joy without breaking the bank," "a source of happiness that we can still afford." When the big uncertainties, a house, a pension, a career that goes to plan, have quietly priced themselves out of reach, the small one is what is left on the shelf.

Toys bought for people 15 and older are now nearly a fifth of all toy spending worldwide, and the money spent on them has more than doubled since 2020. The company behind Labubu is turning out something like thirty million plush toys a month, ten times the year before. This is not a quirk of a few lonely people. It is a very large, very fast business, and its core product is a sealed guess.

Then look at what the guess costs. Not the sticker price. The other thing. The 55-year-old with the fresh law license had no money for gas in June; she borrowed it, watched the credit card climb, and let the packages pile up unopened, because by then the wanting was the whole event. "It became like a game," she said. "Most of the time, I didn't want the stuff. I just liked the excitement of waiting, finding and obtaining them." A 34-year-old cashier in Mississippi spent about four hundred dollars in a month on a live-shopping app that did not show him the total until the order reached his door; he stopped feeling glad when the box arrived, too busy adding up "the damage I'd done to my savings and finances." The woman in Monterey had it right at her post office box. The boxes did not cost four thousand dollars. They cost Europe.

Where the safe surprise ends and a compulsion begins is not a line you can always see from the outside, and often not from the inside either. The comfort story cracks in three places, and it is the buyers who point to them: when the thing arrives unwanted, a squishy lobster nobody asked for, a duplicate that only deflates; when the spending has to be hidden from the person you share a bank account with; when the packages stack up unopened because opening them was never the event. The same few seconds that loosen one person's over-scheduled week are the ones another person cannot stop paying for. The wiring underneath is the oldest trick there is: a reward that arrives after an unpredictable number of tries is the one that builds the most stubborn habit, the same reason a slot machine holds a person to a stool. One buyer swore she would never be someone who sat in Vegas feeding the slot machines, then heard herself, and admitted she was doing the exact same thing.

So before you decide these people are foolish, add up your own week and see how much of it you already knew before it happened. The reason a box of cotton can be worth twenty dollars, or four thousand, is that the real unknowns in a grown life have mostly been either automated away or turned into things to dread. What she was buying was never a doll. It was the feeling of not knowing, in the last shape where not knowing still felt safe. Her stack of parcels does not really ask why she bought so many. It asks how many surprises are left in your life that you would actually choose, and what you would pay to feel one.
