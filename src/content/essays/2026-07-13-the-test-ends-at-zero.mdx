A man lights an incense stick in a small room and waits for the air to get bad.

The room is a fixed size, 728 cubic feet, the same room every time so the tests can be compared. Smoke climbs. A laser sensor reads the particles a person cannot see: the fine ones, PM1 and PM2.5, small enough to slip through lung tissue and into the blood, and the larger inhalable particles measured as PM10. He switches an air purifier to its highest speed and watches the numbers on a dashboard fall. The test ends when PM1 reaches zero.

Then the dull part, which is most of it. He measures the sound from three feet away at every fan speed. He reads the watts at each speed with an energy meter, down to standby. He works out what a year of replacement filters will cost, and the filters run fifty to a hundred dollars each. When a machine seems wrong he opens it to see what the manufacturer actually put inside. He has been writing about air purifiers since 2010 and testing them by hand since he started the site. His wife runs the day to day and supports the testing. For years the whole operation was paid for mainly one way: when a reader followed a link and bought a purifier, a retailer sent back about three percent. No display ads. No money for sponsored posts. No payment for a kind review. The room, the sensor, the year of filter math, all of it was funded by people arriving, reading, and clicking through to buy.

For a while, about four thousand of them arrived every day from Google.

Then, in the spring of 2024, Google changed the machinery that decides what a search page shows, and the room kept running while the people stopped coming. The site's own number, which other outlets repeated, was a ninety-one percent drop. Four thousand daily visitors from search became two hundred. Most of those two hundred already knew the site by name. They were not being discovered. They were coming home. At its peak the site had paid fifteen contributors. Afterward it could afford one full-time person. The test still ended when PM1 reached zero. There was almost no one left to read the result.

The easy story about this is that the answer machines came and stole the work. And there is a version of that story arriving now that looks worse than a search update. An answer engine reads the web, digests it, and hands the reader a finished paragraph, so the reader never opens the page, never sees the link, never buys the thing, and the person who lit the incense gets nothing. On July 1, 2026, Cloudflare, which handles traffic for more than a fifth of the web's domains, published a one-year report on what the old arrangement looks like now. By its count, more than half of all internet traffic is now non-human. Most of the crawlers are not there to send anyone back. As of that report, fifty-two percent of crawler requests existed to train models, up from twenty-two percent a year earlier. For every hour a person spends looking something up, Cloudflare says, only fifteen minutes now happens on the open web.

But the theft story does not quite fit the room.

Start with the timing. The thing that gutted the site in 2024 was not an answer engine. It was an ordinary Google core update, the kind that reshuffles rankings, and the site's careful pages sank beneath sponsored posts, a Quora thread from 2016, big-media lists, and sixty-four shopping listings. The machines that write finished answers were barely in the picture yet. So the wound came first, and the robots that skip the visit arrived after, into a site already hurt by the older cut.

Then look at the quality complaint, that the lost pages must have deserved to lose. This is the page that did not. Google's own written guidance for product reviews asks for exactly what the room produces: show the product physically, give real measurements, compare it against rivals, explain the tradeoffs, prove you touched the thing. The room is the ideal made literal. It still lost.

And look at the age of the pattern. Searches that end with no click at all are not new and are not mainly about AI. A majority of Google searches already ended without a click back in 2019. By early 2026, by one clickstream estimate, more than two-thirds of United States searches ended without a click, and in that same window the new AI Mode was still only about a third of one percent of searches. The answer box on the results page, the knowledge panel, the little fact lifted straight from your page and shown before the link: those had been eating the visit for a decade. The newest machines did not invent the leak. They found it.

Which leaves one explanation standing, and it is stranger and colder than theft.

The visit was never the useful part. It was the receipt. Thirty years ago the web decided, without ever quite deciding, that the way to pay for knowledge was to make a person walk to it. In 1994 the web got its first rule for crawlers, a plain text file a site could post to tell robots where not to go. It was never a law, nobody enforced it, and the problems it was written to solve were physical: robots hammering a server too fast, fetching the same file over and over, blundering into a script and voting in a poll a thousand times. It was a note taped to a door. It said nothing about money, because at the time there was no money in the doorway. The money came later, in 2003, when Google let anyone paste a snippet of code and get paid for the ads on their page, and when programs like Amazon's began paying a few percent on whatever a visitor bought. That is the moment the visit became the cash register. Not because the visit was valuable in itself. Because it was the one thing you could count and charge for. The page was paid not when it was useful but when a useful page pulled in a body that could be sold an ad or a purchase.

Traffic was always a proxy. A rough, convenient stand-in for a thing nobody could measure directly, which was whether the page had actually helped somebody. For a long time the proxy held, because the only way to get the help was to go to the page. The answer machines broke that link. Now the help can travel without the person. The room's numbers, the fifty-three minutes to clear the smoke, the sixty-eight decibels at top speed, the two-hundred-dollar-a-year filter bill, can all show up in a paragraph the reader never has to leave to read. Cloudflare, which sells the infrastructure that sits between crawlers and publishers, put a figure on the imbalance: in one week it measured one AI company sending a single visitor back for every seventy thousand pages its crawlers took. The room did not stop being useful. The one thin thread that turned its usefulness into rent got cut.

This is where the honest counterargument lives, and it is stronger than the people losing traffic want to admit. Picture the reader, not the writer. She is standing in her kitchen with batter half mixed, out of eggs, and she wants to know what to use instead, now, without scrolling through ten pages built to trap her. She wants to know if a particular loveseat will fit in the back of her particular minivan. Microsoft, making the case for AI search in 2023, guessed that half of the ten billion questions people ask a search engine every day go badly answered, because search was never built for them. To that woman in the kitchen, the finished answer is not a robbery. It is the search box finally doing the thing it always implied it could do. And the moral word "theft" is doing more work than it can bear. Groups that spend their lives on the open web, the Electronic Frontier Foundation and Creative Commons among them, argue that a machine reading the whole web to learn from it looks less like copying a page and more like a search index or a library, the kind of use the law has long allowed. You can believe the room got a raw deal and still think "they stole it" is the wrong sentence.

There is a harder problem hiding behind the sympathy, which is that the obvious fix might be worse than the wound. If the answer to unpaid use is to make every use paid, you build a web where knowledge has a turnstile, and turnstiles favor the big. The publishers already signing licensing deals with AI companies are the large ones, with lawyers and leverage. Cloudflare, which is building the tollbooth, admits the deals are bespoke and unlikely to replace what small sites lost, and that a small site now faces an ugly choice: stay open and get used for free, or lock the door and disappear from the places people still look. Charging by the actual use may also mean building a system that records who asked what, which is a strange machine to bolt onto a person quietly asking a private question. And an answer is usually stitched from many pages at once, so even if you wanted to pay the room, it is not obvious how you would know its share.

Still, the direction is set, and it points away from the pageview. Cloudflare's move is to stop treating all robots as one thing and split them by what they came to do. A search crawler indexes your page to answer questions later, and is expected to send readers or pay. An agent acts in the moment on one person's behalf. A training crawler feeds a model. Starting September 15, 2026, on pages that carry ads, Cloudflare's default will wave the search crawler through and block the other two unless the owner says otherwise. And it is turning its pay-per-crawl experiment into something it calls pay-per-use, for a blunt reason: a crawl is a terrible measure of value. One page can be fetched once and quoted in a thousand answers, or fetched a thousand times and used in none. More than half of all well-behaved crawling, Cloudflare says, just re-fetches pages that have not changed. So the new idea is to charge closer to where the value actually lands: a fee when your work shows up in an answer, a payment when an agent needs one specific page. A crawl price pays for the door. A price at the answer pays closer to the help. A price on the finished task pays closest of all.

Here is the part nobody has solved. We do not know how to price usefulness, because usefulness was never the thing the web measured. We measured the visit, for thirty years, and got so used to the proxy that we mistook it for the real thing. Now we are trying to build, in a hurry and late, a cash register for a value we only ever tracked by its shadow. Cloudflare's taxonomy is a vendor's architecture, not a market. "Price usefulness" is the direction that remains after the old receipt stopped tracking the work. It is not a solved problem.

The couple who run the room have, by the numbers, come out the other side. By late 2025 their search traffic had recovered, and they had stopped believing in it. Whatever stability they once had from Google, one of them said, they no longer count on at all, and a return of that traffic would not change the plan. They pushed into video, a membership, subscriptions. They still refuse display ads. From the worst of it, when the visitors had gone and the tests had not, one of them put it plainly: they were living as if Google were simply gone for them.

The room has not changed. Someone still lights the incense, still watches the fine particles climb and then fall, still waits for the number to reach zero. The test ends the way it always ended. What is unsettled is everything downstream of it: whether a reader ever learns the room is there, and whether, when the answer arrives already finished, anyone thinks to pay for the room and not for the walk to its door. We spent thirty years paying people to be visited, and told ourselves we were paying for the work. The visit is the part we no longer need.
