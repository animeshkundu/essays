Four days ago in Mexico City, hours before kickoff and before eight in the morning, people in lucha libre masks and mariachi suits were already walking toward an old concrete bowl in a neighborhood called Santa Ursula. They came in green. Some wore the folkloric skirts and pre-Hispanic dress of a country getting ready for company. A convenience store just outside the stadium was doing brisk trade by half past seven. The morning was clear and sunny after rain, the forecast a mild 24 degrees Celsius, the air thin at more than two thousand meters. A man of fifty stood on the concourse in a sombrero, carrying a replica of the trophy, and told a reporter, "This is our temple." He had been a small child the last time this city hosted a World Cup, in 1986.

By kickoff the ground held a capacity crowd of 80,824. When the anthems finished, the fans threw thousands of cardboard sombreros toward the pitch; most of them fluttered back down into the crowd. The smoke from the fireworks, red and green and white, had barely cleared when a Mexican player scored in the ninth minute, and a roar came down off the stands. In the opening minutes, every completed home pass drew a chorus of "Ole." A second goal followed after the break, a header, the scorer in tears. It was not a clean match: South Africa finished with nine men after two red cards, and a Mexican defender was sent off in stoppage time. But the country won its opening night, 2-0, and the party by the arena went on afterward under a light rain.

The stadium had done all this before. It is the first ever to hold matches in three separate World Cups, in 1970, in 1986, and now, and the first to host the opening game three times. It went up in the 1960s, took a first crowd of more than a hundred thousand, and remains the largest ground in Latin America. On this grass a forward lifted his third World Cup trophy in 1970, and his country kept the old cup for good. Here, one afternoon that year, two European sides played a semifinal so wild it is still called the Game of the Century. Sixteen years later, on the same field, an Argentine scored the two most argued-over goals in the tournament's history: one with his hand, the other with a run past half the England team. The finals held here drew 107,412, then 114,600. This was a place that made its name by concentration. It held the very peak of the old World Cup, the scarce and unrepeatable moments, the games nobody could miss.

Now, reopened after nearly two years of work (the crews were still finishing details forty-eight hours before it took its first crowd back in March), the Colossus of Santa Ursula was doing something close to the opposite. It was opening the largest tournament the sport has ever staged.

The numbers are the quarrel. This World Cup has 48 teams, not 32. It runs 104 matches, not 64, across 16 cities in three countries, from that June morning to the middle of July. Forty more games than the last one, spread so wide that a fan trailing the Canadian team from its game in Toronto to its next one in Vancouver would cross more than two thousand miles. To make room, the group stage swelled from 48 matches to 72. And here is the figure the critics keep in their pocket: in 2022, those 48 group games knocked out 16 teams. In 2026, the 72 group games knock out the same 16. Two-thirds of the field, 32 of 48, walk into the knockout round. The gate is wider and the first cut is softer.

To a certain kind of fan, and it is not a small or stupid kind, this is plain dilution. The head of European football called a large share of the matches "completely uninteresting." A historian who has just written a new history of the tournament put the worry precisely: a World Cup game should feel almost must-watch, and nobody is going to watch 90 of 104 games, it is simply too much. A former United States forward said the thing does not really start until the round of 32. The head of England's players' union reached for the plainest image he could find: Christmas is nice because it is not every Tuesday. Their case is not nostalgia, and it does not deserve to be waved off. Scarcity is where a World Cup gets its pressure. Build a group in which one win might be enough to go through, and you drain the jeopardy that once made a single early defeat feel like the end of the world. Stretch the calendar, add a knockout round so the finalists play eight games instead of seven after a long club season, and in the thin mountain air the legs tire faster: on opening night alone, three Mexican players cramped, more than their coach had seen in twenty-five years. One former defender said the best of them are treated a little like cattle. If enough matches carry no stakes, the argument runs, the whole thing sags in the middle and the audience wanders off. That could happen. Four days in, the only honest thing to say is that nobody yet knows.

But the purist argument measures one thing and calls it the whole. It measures the peak: the density of must-watch minutes, the sharpness of the elite contest. By that ruler a bigger tournament can only lose. The trouble is that a World Cup was never mostly its peak. It is closer to the other way around: not a competition that happens to draw a crowd, but a crowd that happens to hold a competition. And a crowd is measured by reach, not by sharpness.

Consider who walked through the wider gate. Cape Verde, a chain of Atlantic islands with about half a million people, the smallest African nation ever to reach a World Cup, qualified for the first time; its goalkeeper, a man of thirty-nine, said he had dreamed of the moment since he was a child, and a fan back in Praia said he had shouted until his voice gave out, and was happy. Curacao made it too, an island of roughly 150,000, smaller than any country that has ever qualified; one of its players called it an impossibility made possible. When Curacao's first match came, it lost to Germany 7-1, which the purist will note and the island will remember for its own reasons. Jordan reached its first World Cup on a night that filled Amman with car horns and a sky lit by drones; a schoolteacher of fifty-five said it was about time, a joy the country had waited a long time for. A midfielder for Haiti, back for the first time since 1974, said the World Cup had been a fantasy he watched as a child and never thought he could touch.

To the man who called those matches uninteresting, they are the low-grade filler stretching out the first round. To the people inside them, there is, in the words of a joint letter from a dozen or so football associations across Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean, "no such thing as an unimportant World Cup match." Both statements are true. That is the whole knot. A single game can be, at the same time, a fixture a neutral forgets by dinner and the most important ninety minutes in a particular country's life. Scarcity is very good at producing the first kind of value and completely blind to the second. Expansion spends a little of the first to make a great deal more of the second. Whether that is a good trade depends entirely on which one you think a World Cup is for. But even that answer has to earn itself. Expansion is worth defending only if the new matches carry real national stakes, and only if the tournament does not spend so much of its jeopardy that the best football thins to nothing. The honest case for the bigger event is not that nothing was lost. It is that the loss was worth the price.

Here the story refuses to stay tidy. The same tournament widening the field on the pitch is narrowing it in the stands. This is the first men's World Cup to price its tickets the way airlines price seats, moving them with demand. They opened at 60 dollars at the low end and 6,730 for the final. By January the whole range had climbed; some face-value final tickets passed 32,000 dollars; in the spring, four seats for the final turned up on the official resale market, which takes a 30 percent cut, at just under 2.3 million dollars each. At the opener, some fans said they had paid 3,000 dollars or more just to be in the building. A man from Tijuana who could not afford that took his girlfriend to a free fan zone instead, and said, "Thank God it all worked out." Another, waiting in a long line, said the governing body cared only about profit, and then, in the same breath, "It's the passion." A large can of beer inside cost about seventeen dollars, and the piles of empties afterward showed the price had stopped no one.

So the commons is being opened and enclosed in a single motion. The door swings wide enough to admit a team from an island of 150,000, while elsewhere in the same tournament a seat at the final can cost more than a car. A ritual that puts a Caribbean side onto the biggest stage in the game can also quietly sort the people in the seats by what they can pay. Both things are the tournament. Neither one cancels the other.

That old ground in Santa Ursula has now asked this question three times. In 1970 and 1986 it answered by concentration: hold the peak, the final, the goals that argue forever, and let the world lean in. This June it answered by expansion: throw the doors open, let more nations in than ever, and bet that reach is worth more than sharpness. Four days in, the tournament is a blur of anthems and red cards and lopsided scores and impossible seats, and no one can honestly tell you yet whether 104 matches will fill the world or bore it.

But the question the argument really turns on is older than the format. It was there in the man in the sombrero calling a concrete bowl his temple, and it was there in a footballer from the smallest nation ever to reach a World Cup, a side already beaten 7-1 by Germany, who had said before the tournament began that his island wanted to show it could play, and that it had earned its place. It is this. Is a World Cup the best football that can be played, or the most people who can be made to feel they belong to the same afternoon? For most of its life the sport did not have to choose, because the two came bundled together. Now, in the largest tournament ever built, it does. When the last of the crowd filed out under the light rain, one fan was still standing by an exit, in no hurry to leave.
