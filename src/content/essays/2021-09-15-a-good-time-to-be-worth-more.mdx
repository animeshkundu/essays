There is a photograph I keep going back to.

A Burger King sign beside a road in Lincoln, Nebraska, the plastic letters set into the grooves by hand: WE ALL QUIT. SORRY FOR THE INCONVENIENCE. Someone put it up before opening this July, took a picture, and it spread on Facebook and then to news sites around the country within days.

I cannot decide what it is. It reads like a revolt and an apology in the same breath. The top line is a fist. The bottom line is a small, almost old-fashioned courtesy, the kind of thing you say to a neighbor whose mail you forgot to bring in. Both are meant. The woman who ran the store said afterward that the apology was real: some of her crew had worked there seven years, eight years, one of them eighteen, and they had been handing food across that counter to the same regulars for most of that time. They were sorry. They were also finished.

What she and her crew were finished with is not mysterious. The kitchen air conditioning had been broken for weeks, and the room ran into the nineties. She worked fifty to sixty hours a week. She got so dehydrated in that heat that she ended up in a hospital on IV fluids, delirious, and afterward her boss told her she was being a baby. Lunch rushes that should have had eight or nine people ran with three or four, all of them cooking and taking orders and working the drive-through at once. None of this was new. The heat was old, the short staffing was old, the rude calls from management were old. Bad jobs have always been bad.

So the question the photograph really asks is not why they were miserable. It is why they walked out now.

The answer we have agreed to give is a good one. This summer, four million Americans quit their jobs in a single month. That is about a hundred and twenty-nine thousand people a day handing in notice, walking off a shift, not coming back. The story we have built around that number is a story about waking up. The pandemic held people still long enough to see what their jobs actually cost them, and they decided the price was too high.

The testimony is real. A middle-school history teacher in California left the classroom and said the pandemic ripped a veil from her eyes: she had believed the work was about the children, and had come to feel it was about money. A software developer outside Washington noticed his hair thinning, did the arithmetic on a forty-five-minute commute done twice a day for years, and said he simply valued his time now. A man who had cooked in restaurants for twenty-six years quit after he caught the virus at work and carried it home to his wife and her father; during his furlough he had built his two small kids a playroom in the garage, and what he wanted, more than a title, was to see their faces on Christmas morning. A woman doing in-home patient care in Missouri described undressing in her garage after shifts and going straight to the shower before she touched anyone, and sometimes sending her son to sleep at his grandmother's so she would not bring the virus into the house. None of these people are lying, and none of them are talking about a raise. They are talking about their lives.

I believe that story. I keep it in one hand. Then I notice the timing, and I cannot get it out of the other.

The same month those four million people quit, the government counted 10.9 million open jobs, the most since it started keeping the number two decades ago. By one rough comparison, that was more open jobs than there were people counted as unemployed. The two numbers come from different surveys and do not line up cleanly, so treat it only as a sense of scale. But the scale points the same way the summer felt from inside a hot kitchen: there was somewhere else to go. Two years earlier, in a labor market everyone called strong, there had been about 7.2 million openings. The heat and the short staffing had not changed. What had changed sat on the other side of the counter, in the number of doors standing open.

And here is the sentence I cannot walk past. The general manager in Lincoln, describing the moment she decided she was done, put it this way: "It's a good time to sit there and say, OK, I am worth more than this. I don't have to be treated like this." She did not say she had finally realized her worth. She said it was a good time to realize it. The dignity is genuine. The clock in the sentence is doing quiet work.

This is the thing I have come around to, and it is not comfortable. Self-respect is usually described as a feeling you either have or lack, something that grows inside a person like a spine straightening. But the government's own definition of a quit points somewhere colder. It calls quits a measure of workers' "willingness or ability to leave jobs." Two words, joined by an or. For years the willingness was there, sitting in every hot kitchen and every closing shift. What the summer of 2021 added was not willingness. It was ability.

We like to think conviction is a fixed trait, like eye color, carried steadily from room to room. It behaves more like a purchase. There is a price at which a person will act on what they already believe, and this summer that price came down. It did not come down the same amount for everyone, and for many it did not come down at all. But for a worker with somewhere to land, walking out of a bad job cost less than it had in years. When it did, a private complaint that people had swallowed for years became a public sign by the road.

You can watch the leverage working inside the same small story. When the crew started to leave, they were not simply shrugged off and replaced. Management sent people in to convince them to stay and offered a dollar-an-hour raise. The workers compared that dollar to what was being paid to hire new managers and read it, correctly, as a measure of how little they had been worth the week before. A dollar was the sound of an employer discovering that these particular exits were now real. Nobody chases a worker they can replace.

I want to be careful not to swing too far, because the market did not make anyone brave for free, and it did not deal the ability out evenly. Even with all that leverage, a labor economist pointed out this summer, these were still jobs paying in the low twenty-thousands a year, with unstable hours and thin benefits. And plenty of people did not get an exit at all. Mothers of young children left work in large numbers over this stretch, but many of them were pushed out by child care that vanished, not pulled out by a better offer waiting across the street. Leverage was handed around the room unequally, the way it always is.

So I am left holding two true things that do not want to sit together. The dignity was real. The leverage was real. And the uncomfortable part, the part I cannot argue my way out of, is that the second is what let the first become something you could say out loud. The awakening was not fake. It was just not free, and we have mostly told it as though it were.

Which leaves a question I do not have the standing to answer, and neither, I suspect, does anyone who put up that sign. If self-respect needs a hot market to be affordable, then it is not only a feeling you have. It is a feeling you can afford to act on, and someone else sets the price. The openings will not stay near this high forever. When they come down, we will learn how much of this summer was a change of heart we get to keep, and how much of it we were renting from a labor shortage.

The plastic letters came down that Saturday, once the picture had done its work. The road is still there. The question is still up.
