Walk into a city council meeting in a town that used to have a daily paper and you will often find the press row empty. Not always. But often enough that it has become the normal thing, and normal things stop being noticed.

The consensus explanation is simple and comforting. The paper was a news-delivery service. The internet delivered news better and cheaper. Readers left. Advertisers left. The paper died. The town is less informed, but information is everywhere now, so the loss is mostly nostalgia.

I think that explanation is wrong in a way that matters. A local newspaper was never mainly a news-delivery service. It was the institution that turned private grievances into public facts. When it disappears, a town does not just get less informed. It loses the ability to agree on what happened.

The anomaly is not that papers closed. Medill counts more than 2,900 newspaper closures since 2005, and its 2023 count put roughly 200 counties with no local news outlet, more than 1,700 counties with one outlet or none, covering roughly 70 million people. The anomaly is that a town can lose the one person paid to sit through its meetings and almost nothing visible changes the next day. No alarm goes off. No headline says the noticing stopped. That is strange, because if the paper's job had been delivering news, the loss should have felt like a missing newspaper on the porch. It usually does not. People still hear about the fire and the festival. They hear about the mayor's arrest, if it is dramatic enough. What they stop hearing about is the budget hearing, the zoning variance, the school board's quiet change to a policy, the contract that went to a cousin. The boring things. The things that only matter because they accumulate.

Part of the loss is that people are less informed. But information is not scarce anymore. A resident can read state budgets, council agendas, and court filings online. Access is not the problem. Almost nobody reads them, and nobody is paid to turn what they would find into a shared account. Advertising moved too, and that is the mechanism, not the loss. Classifieds peaked around 2000 at roughly nineteen to twenty billion dollars and fell to a few billion within about a decade. Newspaper advertising overall fell from roughly forty-nine billion dollars in the mid-2000s to roughly nine or ten billion by 2020. Pew counted a fifty-seven percent drop in newspaper newsroom employment from 2008 to 2020, from 71,640 to 30,820. The money left. But asking why the money left only explains why the paper died. It does not explain what died with it.

What died with it was a bundle. A local paper was never one product. It was legal notices and obituaries and high school scores and the police blotter and the locals column and the classifieds, all in one bundle. The reader who bought the paper for one part paid, through the bundle, for someone to sit in the meeting. Legal notices, which in many states must be published in a paper of general circulation, were another guaranteed revenue stream and another reason a paper had to exist in a county. When the profitable parts were unbundled by the internet, the noticing lost its subsidy even though demand for it did not disappear. A reporter costs roughly seventy-five to one hundred twenty thousand dollars a year, fully loaded. A four-hour budget hearing produces no revenue. So the reporter is the first cost cut, and the meeting continues without a witness.

The phrase "turning private grievances into public facts" is my own, not a quotation from anyone, and it needs defending. A grievance starts private. Someone's water bill spikes. A neighbor's fence moves six inches onto their land. A contractor is passed over. A teacher is not renewed. Left private, these stay rumors, favors, or grudges. A reporter's job is to ask the question in public, check the document, print the answer, and put it where the whole town can see it and cite it later. That is the conversion. The paper was not the only place it could happen, but for most of a town's history it was the place where it reliably did happen, and where the result persisted.

When it stops, the town does not go dark. It goes fragmented. A post in a Facebook group can move a zoning rumor into a packed council chamber in two days. But a post is not a record. It scrolls away. It cannot be cited in a lawsuit, a budget hearing, or a history. It reaches one subgroup rather than the whole town. A newsletter can put a corrupt contract on the record for less than a reporter's salary, but one person cannot cover a school board, a zoning board, a county budget, and a police blotter, and when the founder gets sick the noticing stops. An automated service can turn hundreds of council votes into readable prose, and that is genuinely useful. But it notices what the data already contains. It does not notice the item that was quietly tabled, the mayor's glance, the packed gallery, the absence of a document that should have been there.

The evidence for harm is not as clean as advocates sometimes claim, and I should say so. Newspaper closures are not random, so separating cause from correlation is hard. But the direction of the findings is consistent. Counties that lose a paper tend to vote more straight-ticket and less split-ticket, by a few percentage points in one study. One study found wider yield spreads on municipal bonds, higher taxes, and higher local spending after closure. Another found lower civic engagement: fewer people contacting officials, attending meetings, or working on community projects. A third found mayoral elections becoming less competitive, with fewer candidates and higher incumbent re-election rates. These are not proof that every closed paper was a watchdog. Some were boosterish, captured by local elites, or simply thin. The loss is of reporting capacity and institutional memory, not necessarily of a great newspaper. But the studies point the same way: when no one is paid to notice, officials face less pressure and voters rely more on national party cues.

The strongest case against my thesis is not that social media replaced the paper. It is sharper than that. The paper was never the only way to turn a private grievance into a public fact, and for the events that actually change a town, the new tools are faster, cheaper, more inclusive, and less captured by local elites than a single editor with a printing press ever was. A teenager with a phone filmed a killing that police might otherwise have buried. A resident with a water-testing kit and a scientist ally forced a state and federal response in Flint whose decisive evidence came from a citizen-scientist alliance rather than from the paper alone. A volunteer-run group can move a rumor into a packed chamber in forty-eight hours. A one-person newsletter can put a corrupt contract on the record for less than a reporter's salary. An automated service can do the boring work no human was going to do. The paper's defenders, on this view, confuse the cost of noticing with the act of noticing. The new stack notices more, more cheaply, with more voices. Where the old paper was a bottleneck and a gatekeeper, the new stack is a flood. It is strongest where the old paper was weakest: viral accountability, routine public data at scale, speed and reach, affluent and engaged places, and inclusion. The old paper was often boosterish, sometimes racist, usually male-run, and protective of its advertisers. Nobody should mourn that part.

It goes thin exactly where the thesis lives. Nobody volunteers for the school board, the zoning board, the budget hearing that runs four hours, and nothing viral comes of any of it. A group thread is not a record that can be cited in a lawsuit or a budget hearing five years later. A newsletter has a byline and some accountability, but no editor, no newsroom, and limited institutional backing; when the founder stops, the noticing stops. The replacement models that depend on subscribers, donors, or clean public data skew toward markets that have them, so the poorest counties are the least likely to be reached. And a feed is not a common reference point. It is a contested, algorithmic, subgroup stream, while the paper's "what happened" was at least nominally singular.

I think a replacement actually replaces the paper only if it meets six tests. Was someone in the room when it happened? Is the account checkable against a document or a named source? Is it still findable and citable in five years? Does it cover the unglamorous routine, not just the viral? Is there a named, reachable, legally exposed person responsible for errors? And does it become a common reference point for the whole town? No single replacement has all six. A phone video does not follow up on the zoning variance that passes on a Tuesday night. An automated feed notices only what the data already contains: not the item quietly tabled, not the mayor's glance, not the packed gallery. What the paper had was not any one of those properties. It was the bundle: presence plus verification plus persistence plus routine coverage plus accountability plus sharedness, funded by a two-sided market that no longer exists.

On August 31, 2019, a daily that had published for about a hundred and fifty years printed its last issue, in a city of about sixty-five thousand people that the trade press described as the largest in the country without its own daily newspaper. The owners had announced in mid-2019 that they could not find a buyer. The institution did not disappear in a dramatic way. It stopped. Successor outlets exist, and they matter, but they cover narrower ground: a regional paper from the next county, a digital outlet whose later status is uncertain, television, radio. The loss shows up in small places: obituaries that used to be how a death became a public fact in a town now cost money in distant papers or live on funeral-home websites; high school sports that used to be how a town followed its children now move to school social-media accounts and parent-run streams; reporters who used to cover the meetings leave the field; and residents learn what happened from Facebook groups, television, and word of mouth, faster in some ways and less checkable in all of them. None of this is a catastrophe on any single day. That is the point. The loss is invisible while it is happening, because the value of noticing shows up mainly in what does not happen: a corruption that does not occur, a rumor that does not spread, a dispute that stays private.

A paper can also die as a reporting institution while keeping its masthead, which makes the loss hard to see. Medill tracks these ghost papers separately from closures, and their number has been rising. A site with a local name and no one in town can look like a newspaper without being one. One network alone has been reported to run more than a thousand local-news-branded sites funded by partisan donors, many of them automated. It is the simulation of noticing, used to launder messaging, and it works precisely because readers cannot tell the difference between a person in the room and a machine in another state.

I may be wrong about affluent, engaged towns. A strong Facebook group plus a good newsletter plus an automated feed of council votes may be enough there, especially where money and engaged residents exist. If your town has that, you may never miss the paper. But most towns are not that town, and the ones that are not are the ones that lose the ability to agree on what happened. Ask five people in your town what happened at the last school board meeting. If they give you the same answer, or at least the same document to argue about, the noticing still works. If they give you five different versions, or no version at all, it does not.

The paper sold news. What it made was the shared sentence, "here is what happened," and the town's willingness to fight about it. Whether anything can make that sentence again is still an open question, and it is being answered right now, in meeting rooms with empty chairs.
