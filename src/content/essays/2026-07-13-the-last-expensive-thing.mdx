The fight about machines and science is stuck on the wrong fear. The fear is fake science: fabricated data, invented citations, more plausible junk than anyone can clear. That fear is not baseless. In one audit of 2.5 million papers, nearly 3,000 in biomedicine carried references that trace to nothing, and in a single recent year retractions passed 10,000. But the fear is small. It assumes the danger is that the machines will lie. The harder possibility is that they will tell the truth.

The machines will make more real science, not less. New analyses, new figures, new conclusions, generated at low marginal cost. And the loss will not land where we are looking. It will not be in the science. It will be in the scientist. Discovery is being converted into filtration, and that is a loss even when the public gains.

Watch what the tools already do. Give a chemistry system a plain sentence, "perform multiple Suzuki reactions," and it searches the literature, picks the coupling partners, calculates the volumes, writes the robot's code, hits a wrong instrument command, reads the machine's own documentation, corrects itself, and shows the product in a chromatogram at 9.53 minutes. Leave a materials lab running for 17 days, and it makes 36 of 57 target compounds it had never made, more than two a day, working from a recipe base of 33,343 procedures it read out of 24,304 papers. Let a free-roaming robot work for 8 days, and it runs 688 experiments and finds a catalyst six times as active as the starting formula, at about one-sixtieth of the human time. Point a closed loop at dye-like molecules, and it makes 294 that no one had ever reported. Open one protein-structure database, and more than three million users from 190 countries pull structures that would have cost some 17 million GPU hours to recompute. Hand an unreleased model a longstanding conjecture in discrete geometry, and it disproves it, and human mathematicians read the result and call it valid and publishable.

None of that is a lie. The reactions ran. The materials exist. The proof holds. The strong version of AI-produced science is not counterfeit, it is real, and there is going to be a great deal of it.

<PullQuote>
When making a claim costs almost nothing, the scarce job is no longer making claims. It is deciding which ones to believe.
</PullQuote>

The production side is already unrecognizable. One index counted 6,626,211 journal articles with a 2025 date, about 18,154 a day, up from roughly 1.5 million in 2000. The trust side did not move like that. Reviewers spent more than 100 million hours on peer review in 2020, more than 15,000 years of human attention, and that work rests on very few shoulders: one model found that 20 percent of biomedical researchers did between 69 and 94 percent of the reviews. The number of researchers per million people grew by about half from 2000 to 2020. The output grew by more than 200 percent. The gap between what can be produced and what can be checked is the argument, and it was widening before the machines arrived to widen it faster.

Here is what that gap feels like from inside one person's job. In early May 2026, an associate editor at a neuroscience journal was handed a manuscript on the dynamics of neuronal networks. He did the oldest part of the craft, the part that predates every submission portal: he chose the right readers. He invited about half a dozen referees he judged to have the needed expertise. Before any of them answered, the journal's automated assistant sent its own invitations to other people. Two of them accepted. He thought both were out of their depth on this paper. He flagged it and was told to keep looking on his own. Then one of his experts accepted, and a few hours later the system revoked the invitations he had sent by hand.

"It's just inconceivable," he said, "that a manual invitation to someone who is actually an expert in the field is revoked just a few hours after it is sent." He resigned rather than keep his name on the process.

He was not making a stand against the machines. He said he favored using language models to find experts, as long as the editor is consulted. He said he could even accept a fully automated journal, if it disclosed that the editing was done by AI and stopped putting a human's name on the work. His objection was narrower and colder than technophobia: it was about who certifies the science. The publisher prints the editor's and reviewers' names on the finished article precisely so that someone is answerable for it. He would not be answerable for readers he did not pick.

The publisher tells a different story, and it deserves to be heard. Its chief editor says editors can pause the automated invitations at any stage, that an editor's own expertise and choices always take precedence, that anyone can revoke, replace, or add reviewers at any point, and that this case looks like a breakdown in communication rather than a machine overruling a human. It says automation cut the time to line up willing reviewers by 30 percent in a year, that most of its handling editors find the automation helpful, that the large majority of authors rate the experience good or excellent. Those are the company's own numbers, and they may be right. Then again, a second editor at the same journal tells the first man's story back in miniature: after he decided to reject a paper, he says, the system invited another reviewer without his permission. "Automation is important and often helpful," he said, "but their system has crossed the threshold." Other editors said they had never hit the problem at all.

Follow the gap downstream and filtration stops being quiet craft and turns into a police problem. At a large machine-learning conference this year, organizers hid instructions inside submitted papers, invisible text telling any AI that read the paper to drop certain telltale phrases into its review, so that a reviewer secretly handing the job to a chatbot would be caught by the words that came back. One reviewer, converting the PDFs to Word to read them, saw the hidden text, assumed the authors had planted it, and rejected a paper before he found the same trap in a second one and realized the organizers had set it. "You do not build a healthy reviewing culture," he said, "by treating your reviewers as suspects." Another conference watermarked its PDFs with a dictionary of 170,000 phrases, caught 795 reviews from 506 people who had agreed not to use AI, and desk-rejected 497 papers. This is what trust looks like when it runs low: hidden ink, decoy phrases, everyone checking everyone.

The honest case for the machines is strong, and it deserves better than a caricature. Human review was never the clean gold standard we mourn. When one journal slipped major errors into papers on purpose, some reviewers caught none of them and most caught about a quarter. When reviewers could see the authors' names, they favored the famous ones and the elite institutions, sometimes at twice the odds. The old system leaks. And the best version of the new one does not merely run faster; it keeps a record. An AI that works through every step can, in principle, save the whole messy path, the dead ends and the discarded runs and the reason a number was thrown out, and tie each claim back to the data and code behind it. That is a kind of honesty the polished paper has never offered. The abundance is not abstract either. One group building a drug-repurposing search points out that roughly 3,000 diseases have an approved treatment and about 9,000 do not, and says its machine can score 66 million drug-disease matches in days. The person who started it, the group says, nearly died five times as a medical student, until his team found an overactive immune pathway and tried a 25-year-old drug already sitting in a pharmacy; by its account he has been in remission for more than eight years. Tell that man that discovery should stay slow and human.

That is the case, and it is real, and it does not close the question. Grant every word of it. Grant that the public may get more cures, faster and cheaper. The thing being optimized away is still a specific human act, and there is no line on the ledger for it.

Two things are being confused, and the confusion is doing a lot of work. One is the volume of science, which will rise. The other is the human role inside it, which may hollow. You can measure the first at a glance: papers, materials, structures, matches per day. The second resists counting, the way the success rate of a procedure can hold steady while something the practitioner used to know quietly goes missing. The editor choosing the right readers was making a claim about the map of his field, about who understood this one question well enough to judge it. Push that judgment to the edge of the workflow, turn it into an override you may or may not remember you still have, and the paper still gets reviewed, the queue still moves, the 30 percent time saving still shows up in the report. What does not show up is that the person whose judgment was the point has become a sorter of other people's outputs, a checker of machine work, a hand near a revoke button. The loss is not that scientists become filters; the good ones always were. It is filtration without authority: being asked to certify what you can no longer steer.

The abundance is coming, and most of it will be real. That was never the danger. Abundance does not make trust cheaper; it makes trust the only expensive thing left, and it sends the bill to the shrinking number of people we still ask to stand behind the work. One of them chose about half a dozen readers for a single paper and, in his telling, watched the system move around him, until he set down his title rather than sign his name to readers he had not chosen. He had sent one invitation to a real expert by hand. A few hours later, it had been revoked. Count that, if you can find the column for it.
