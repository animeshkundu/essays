The resale story comes with a number now. One forecast puts the global market for secondhand clothing at $393 billion by 2030. It comes from thredUP, a company that sells resale, so hold it loosely. The direction is not really in question. Secondhand is growing faster than clothing retail as a whole, and it gets sold twice over: as a way to spend less, and as a way to harm less. Buy the used shirt, the story goes, and one fewer new shirt has to be made.

Three facts sit badly against that story.

Thrift got expensive. Prices for used clothing rose about 40 percent over five years, by the count in thredUP's own report. The bargain rack is still cheaper than the mall, but the gap is closing, and in some stores it has already closed.

The heaviest secondhand shoppers are also the heaviest new-clothing shoppers. A 2025 study in the journal Scientific Reports surveyed 1,009 Americans and found secondhand buying moved in step with new buying, a correlation of 0.58. The people most engaged in resale also kept each garment the shortest time. That is a correlation, not a proven cause. The researchers read it through rebound and moral licensing: the used purchase may be granting permission for the new one.

And one of the world's largest reuse hubs still cannot use everything it takes in. In Kantamanto, in Accra, roughly 40 percent of the millions of garments that move through the market each week leave again as waste. That share does not vanish. It is swept into sacks at night, burned in corner fires, or dragged back onto the city's beaches in ropes of tangled cloth more than ten meters long.

Put the three together and the reassuring picture inverts. Secondhand shopping has stopped being a brake on overconsumption and become the machinery that lets overconsumption feel careful. The reflex answer is that this is a young market with growing pains, and scale will fix it. But this is not a flaw that scale removes. It is what scale feeds. Follow one shirt.

A shirt you put in a donation bag is not one thing. It is a bargain, a listing, a margin, a bale, and a waste bill, and which one it is depends entirely on who is holding it.

In your closet, the shirt is guilt, and getting rid of it is relief. A fashion writer who counted her wardrobe for a waste-awareness challenge reached 167 garments, and figured the number she actually wore was closer to 90. She had put off the clear-out for years because she feared her castoffs would end up in landfill. When she finally did it, she handed most of them to friends over WhatsApp, because listing everything would have taken too long. For the closet to keep feeling light, the clothes have to keep leaving.

At the resale platform, the shirt becomes a job. This is the part the word "resale" hides. Someone has to receive it, inspect it, grade it, clean it, photograph it, describe it, price it, store it, find a buyer, pack it, ship it, and eat the cost if it comes back. thredUP built distribution centers that hold more than 7.5 million items and process more than 100,000 of these one-off listings a day. A garment worth $4 and a garment worth $400 move through most of the same steps.

That shared handling creates a floor. A recirculation service that does this work for brands put the floor in pounds: clothing resells for about half its first price, the minimum item worth handling is around 40 pounds, and a decent margin needs 100 or more. Its chief executive called 40 pounds "really scraping the barrel," and said the work is "all quite manual still." Below the floor, the labor costs more than the garment returns.

That floor is only the first of three conditions. Resale stops being a brake on buying and becomes an engine for it wherever three things hold at once.

The first is the floor itself: what falls below it gets pushed downstream. The cheap, flimsy stuff, the fast fashion, mostly falls below. It cannot carry the handling, so managed resale does not want it, thrift stores cannot move all of it, and it flows toward wherever labor is cheap enough to sort it by hand. That is the export bale.

The second is that the money rewards transactions, not longevity. thredUP's revenue grew almost 20 percent in 2025, on 25 percent more orders, while the average order barely moved. The growth came from more sales, not bigger ones. The luxury consignor The RealReal reports that more than 80 percent of its GMV comes from repeat buyers and more than 80 percent from repeat consignors; 16 percent of its buyers also sell, and half its sellers also buy. It calls this a flywheel, which is the right word, because it spins. A business that lives on transaction count has no reason to want you to own your clothes longer. It has every reason to make secondhand feel like shopping, which is why thredUP now offers image search, plain-language search, and a chatbot that will build you an outfit.

The third is that the moral label converts resale into permission. This is the licensing effect the survey caught. If buying used counts as virtue, then buying more used counts as more virtue, and the closet fills faster than it empties. The 2026 industry report is candid about the behavior it is selling: 46 percent of shoppers say they browse resale before buying anything new, 41 percent look secondhand first for value, and 57 percent now resell their own things for income, more than double the year before. Read one way, that is a market maturing. Read another, it is a permanent shopping habit that has learned to feel like stewardship.

A definition has to survive a hard case, or it is only a mood. At a secondhand shop in Boulder, a family spent about an hour choosing bedding and pillowcases for a child's birthday bedroom, checking with grandparents seated in donated chairs along the wall, speaking in hushed voices, holding pieces up. At the register they learned each item was no more than $7. The owner said the news brought tears of joy. The shop sells donated clothing for a dollar to three, uses online auctions to stay in the black, and sends its small surplus to an animal rescue. "We actually lose money being here," the owner said. Nearby, a college student browsed and left, because she would not pay, in her words, "$15 for a damn pair of jeans." This is thrift doing exactly what thrift promised: a shirt with real life left in it, sold cheap to someone who needs it, no floor to clear and no permission being bought. In that moment, thrift was meeting a need.

Then watch the same rack turn. A reseller who quit a car museum job in 2019 now works the unsorted donation bins full time, and says the regulars picking those bins have multiplied. Some days, he says, 50 to 80 high-school kids are in there, hunting the same items. Some people who would have donated their clothes six years ago now check their closets for anything worth money and sell it themselves. The charity rack has become a supply depot. Same shirt, same store, and the line between thrift and inventory runs straight down the middle of it.

Everything that falls below the floor has to land somewhere. One place it lands is Accra.

A retailer in Kantamanto buys a bale sight unseen, a compressed block well over a hundred pounds, wrapped in plastic and wire, and cuts it open on the market floor. Within about an hour they know whether they will make the money back. The top grade, the pieces with tags still on and the items worth reselling, averages under a fifth of a bale by volume but has to earn back most of its cost. The rest is worn, wrong, damaged, or dead. The local name for the trade translates as "dead white man's clothes," from an old idea that a person would have to die to give away this much.

One bale, opened for a reporter before the fire, held 180 light summer jackets. Eighty-five could not be sold: sweat-ringed collars, missing buttons, a bloodstain on one sleeve. Familiar labels went by as the importer sorted: Target, Zara, Billabong. The bale had cost him $90. He sold about $20 of it. The rest went out with the rubbish. He had one word for it: "It's an insult."

The clothes move through the market on the backs of women, most of them from the north, because the paths are too narrow for machines. A single bale runs over a hundred pounds. One head porter had started the work at twelve; she carried her young son with her and made around four and a half dollars a day. Some women are hurt badly enough that they cannot carry again, and are sent home. About 40 percent of everything that comes through the market leaves as waste.

Then, in the opening days of 2025, fire tore through much of Kantamanto. By the careful count of the foundation that has studied the market for years, it destroyed about 8,890 stalls, some 60 percent of the market by area, and affected more than 10,000 people. Two of them died. A stallholder who stitched his own designs from secondhand scraps got the call in the early hours and reached the market to find parts of it still burning. "I couldn't salvage a thing. Everything has gone," he said. "Now my daily bread has been cut. I used my stall at Kantamanto to sustain myself."

After the fire, bale prices rose. According to that foundation's early census, most retailers now need to charge at least $3 an item just to break even, and because the good grade is such a thin slice of each bale, they often have to price the best usable pieces closer to $5. The lowest daily wage in the city can be as little as $2.

A light closet and a full donation bag at one end. At the other, a person pricing a used shirt at more than a day's wage, because that is what it costs to survive the volume the story keeps producing. The market spends more than $300 million a year buying these bales, over half of it paid back to exporters in the Global North. The shirt was free when you let it go. Somebody downstream is still paying for it.

None of this makes reuse a lie. When a used garment truly replaces a new one and stays in use, the savings are real, and the studies that measure it find most of the benefit sitting in the new garment that never got made. That is the whole case for thrift, and it still stands. The trouble is that the word "secondhand" no longer tells you whether any of that happened. It has been stretched to cover the family buying bedding for $7, the app that files your castoffs as inventory, and the bale that loses money on a market floor in Accra, and it flatters all three the same.

So here is a test you can run, the next time you feel good dropping off a bag or clicking buy on something used. Follow the two items. Did the used thing you bought replace a thing you would have bought new, or was it extra? And the thing you gave away: is it in use somewhere, or is it now someone else's problem to sort, sell, or burn? When resale replaces a purchase, it is thrift, and it earns every kind word it gets. When it adds one, it is shopping with a better alibi.
