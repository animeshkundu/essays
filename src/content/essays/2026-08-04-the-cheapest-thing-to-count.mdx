A badge reader knows one thing. A credential was presented at a door, at a time, and the lock either opened or it didn't. That is the whole of what it holds: who, where, when, granted or denied. It does not know whether you sat down. It does not know whether you taught anyone, learned anything, fixed a problem, or said a single word to another human being. It knows the door.

For most of the badge's life this was fine, because nobody asked the door to mean very much. It was a security tool. It kept strangers out and let staff in, and it left behind a quiet log that almost no one read. Then companies decided that the door should stand for the workday. In one 2025 Owl Labs survey of 2,000 full-time U.S. workers, 43 percent said they were already "coffee badging," which the report defines with unusual honesty as showing up at the office just long enough to be seen. Another 12 percent said they planned to try it. Being seen is the unit. People are supplying the unit.

The easy story about this is that workers are lazy and a little dishonest, and that a plastic card has made it easy for them to cheat. That story is popular, it fits what a lot of managers already believe, and I think it is mostly wrong. Not because the workers are saints. Because the behavior is too widespread, too patterned, and too well tolerated to be a moral failing of individuals. When a behavior turns up in survey after survey, sorts itself the same way across companies, and gets quietly tolerated by the very people paid to stop it, you are not looking at character. You are looking at design.

Rule out the boring explanations first, because that is the honest way to reach a claim worth defending.

It is not simple laziness, because the people doing it are not the people the lazy story predicts. In Owl Labs' 2024 numbers, managers coffee badged more than the workers they supervised: 47 percent of U.S. managers against 34 percent of individual contributors. The bosses enforcing presence were gaming presence at a higher rate than their reports. Laziness does not sort itself so neatly by rank. Incentives do.

It is not that people hate the office and want it gone. The same surveys show workers happily choosing the office for specific things: 55 percent said it suited meeting new people, 51 percent said mentoring. They are not rejecting the building. They are rejecting the trip on the days the trip buys them nothing.

And it is not that enforcement is too soft. Enforcement is heavy. In a December 2023 ResumeBuilder survey of 800 business leaders, 79 percent said their companies were already tracking office attendance, and of those, 58 percent used badge swipes. A third said they would fire people who didn't come in when required. The stick is real, and the behavior persists in front of it. When a strong penalty fails to move a common behavior, the penalty is usually aimed at the wrong thing.

One thing is left standing once the easy answers are gone. The company wanted something it could not see: collaboration, mentoring, fast coordination, the hallway question, the reason the commute was supposed to be worth it. Those things are expensive to measure and slow to prove. So the company reached for a cheap stand-in that it could count without effort, a body at a door, and then it made the stand-in the target, with consequences attached. Measure the wrong thing and people will give you exactly that thing, and nothing else.

An economist named this failure in the 1970s: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Walk the steps slowly, because the argument lives in them. First you pick a proxy that is easy to observe, because the thing you actually care about is hard to observe, and the two usually drift along together, which is why the proxy looked trustworthy at all. Then you attach stakes: a bonus, a rating, a firing. Then people do the rational thing and supply the proxy at the lowest possible cost, stripping away everything it was standing in for. A swipe used to hint at a day of work. Once it is the target, it hints at nothing but a swipe.

This pattern is old, and it helps to watch it run across cases, because it looks the same every time.

Start with the oldest version, a machine from 1888. The inventor built a clock that made each worker carry a key of a distinct shape, insert it, and turn it, so the machine could print that person's number and the hour and the minute onto a paper ribbon. A companion patent three years later sold the point plainly: let each worker "by his own act accurately record the time of his arrival or departure," and so prevent "all disputes." Read that promise again. A threshold ritual, performed by the worker, stands in for the day, and it is sold as the end of arguments about time. The employer still never sees the work. It sees the key turn.

More than a century on, the same trade is made with a card and a sensor. In 2025, PwC in the UK tracked office attendance with work-pass swipes and Wi-Fi connections, cross-referenced against timesheets, and turned into a colored status: amber below a 60 percent threshold, red below 40, with breaches able to touch bonus and rating. The revealing part is the denial. The firm's chief people officer told a House of Lords committee that the monitoring was "not clocking people in and out" and "not an old-school manufacturing world." Set that next to the 1888 machine, sold to make the timekeeper unnecessary. The mechanism got quieter, a badge and a spreadsheet instead of a key and an ink ribbon, but the wish is identical, and so is the flinch from naming it.

Then the case where the proxy stops being a metaphor and becomes an accusation. In 2026, Bell fired a group of corporate workers for what it called "swipe and go": entering the office, swiping a key card to record attendance, and leaving. By June, roughly fifty former employees had sued over the terminations, with more than thirty represented by a single employment lawyer earlier in the spring. Their tenures ran from two to thirty-two years. Some, according to their lawyer, had barely worked in an office in their lives; one had been hired more than a decade earlier and, the lawyer said, never stepped foot in it. Bell says a small number were let go, that the majority admitted to deliberate and repeated falsification, and that in its account some workers did vivid things, one swiping a card just before midnight and again after to fake two days, another coming in only to use the gym. Those are the company's characterizations, not proven facts, and they are the strongest version of its case.

But look at what the workers say, because it is the hinge. Their lawyer says some managers waved them through: "as long as you come in and swipe your fob, you don't have to stay the whole day." Workers got, in his words, "different and contradictory messages." And here is Bell's own tell: the company says the managers who condoned the practice were investigated and fired too. That concession is quiet but real. It means the nod existed somewhere. In the workers' account, the swipe was a courtesy a manager had blessed, a small measurable yes that stood in for the day because someone with authority said it could. In their telling it was a habit, not a scheme, right up until the company decided to read the log a different way. Bell tells it differently, and both accounts sit in the record, untested in court. But notice what is not in dispute. A gesture that some managers had treated as permission became, later, the evidence for firing with just cause, which lets a company let you go with no severance, and which, as one employment lawyer put it, courts have called the capital punishment of employment law.

Give the company its strongest case here, because it has one. An office rule is a promise made to everyone who keeps it: the parent who booked daycare around three days downtown, the new hire who came in to be taught, the manager who reserved the room and the desks. Every honest exception path, the sick child, the house emergency, the approved remote day, works only because it is scarce and above board. A worker who taps a badge and leaves has not merely optimized a bad metric. He has minted a private exception no one signed off on, and made the shared record a little less true for the people who actually showed up. The colleague who stayed all day is not foolish to resent that. Some of what an office is for, teaching a new hire, absorbing how a team works, being in the room when a decision gets made, does need bodies in the same place, and a rule about that needs a record it can enforce. If the rule was clear, the worker knew it, and no manager blessed the workaround, then calling it misrepresentation is fair. The trouble is that last condition, and at Bell it is exactly what is in dispute.

To see why the swipe is such thin evidence, look at the case where the measurement actually held. In a 2023 British Columbia tribunal decision, an accounting firm fired a remote worker and proved time theft, and it won. What it took was not a door. The firm installed tracking software and produced 50.76 specific hours that appeared on her timesheets but not in her recorded activity, including time she had booked to client files she never touched and stretches the software logged against a streaming service. At the meeting, she said, "you can't fight the time," and acknowledged entering time to files she had not worked. That is what a real case looks like: named hours, specific dates, the exact files, the service the software caught, an admission. None of that is the work itself either; it is still a record standing in for the work. But it is a record built to fit the accusation, hour by named hour. A badge tap is not. The distance between the two cases is the distance between evidence assembled against a specific claim and a door that only ever knew it opened.

None of this proves every worker acts in good faith. Some coffee badging is just cheating in the costume of protest, and the case above is what it looks like when a company troubles itself to prove one. That changes the larger problem very little. Even a full, honest, all-day badge record may not be the thing it implies. A worker can commute in, swipe, and spend eight hours on the same video calls she would have taken at home, because her actual team is in other cities. That office day, by the 2025 Owl Labs tally, cost her about $55 in commute, parking, coffee, and lunch, and bought the company nothing it didn't already have. Meanwhile a randomized trial of 1,612 employees found that letting people work from home two days a week cut quit rates by about a third and did not dent their performance grades, their promotions, or, for the engineers, their lines of code over the next two years. The managers in that study had guessed hybrid would cost them output; surveyed afterward, they revised their estimate from minus 2.6 percent to plus 1.0. Presence was never performance. It can feed performance, a morning in the same room as the right person is worth something, but it is not the thing itself, and the badge counted presence while quietly billing it as the thing itself. It was just the cheapest thing on the shelf to count, and counting it taught the company almost nothing it wanted to know.

So here is a test you can carry with you. The next time a company announces that it is cracking down on attendance, ask one question: what did it stop being able to see the moment it started counting doors? A number is usually cheap to collect for a reason: it measures the easy thing instead of the real one, and the gap between the two is exactly the space where people learn to give you the number and keep the rest. The badge tells you a door opened. It was never going to tell you whether the day behind the door was worth the trip.

Go back to the worker at the reader, card in hand, making the smallest gesture the building would accept as a yes. In the workers' account that gesture was once enough, blessed by a manager or at least tolerated, and the company built its whole record of the workday on it because it was cheap to count and easy to file. The number told the company, over and over, that a tapped card equaled a day of work, and the company believed the number, because believing it meant never having to ask the harder thing: whether the office day it was selling back was worth showing up for. Then one morning it read the same taps as fraud and let the people go with nothing. Maybe some of them had earned it. But the gesture it fired them over was, in their telling, the one it had spent years asking them to make. Standing at that door: who was actually cheating whom.
