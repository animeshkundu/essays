The promise comes packaged with the device, and by now you have heard it so often it sounds like plain sense. Wear the ring, or the watch, or the band. Sleep. In the morning, read the number. The number knows your night better than you do, because your body is a biased witness and the sensors are not. More data about yourself is more knowledge of yourself. On sleep, that last part runs exactly backward.

Here is the claim, flat: the sleep score is the rare measurement that can make the thing it measures worse. Not because it lies. Because it grades a state you are not allowed to perform.

Look at what walked into sleep clinics once the scoring got good. A woman in her late twenties came to a sleep center because she could not sleep well, and she brought proof. She had cut her tracker's printouts apart by hand and pasted them, to scale, into her sleep diary. The device reported restless nights and an average sleep efficiency of sixty percent, and that matched how she felt, so she believed it. Her doctors went looking for the cause. They checked her iron and treated her restless legs until the symptoms were gone. They shrank her time in bed. They ran a home apnea test, and it came back clean. Finally they brought her into the lab for a full overnight recording, electrodes reading her brain directly, the measurement every consumer device is only an approximation of. It found no disordered breathing. It found that once she was asleep, which took her a long ninety-seven minutes, she slept deeply, with more of the deepest stage than is typical for her age. The lab did not crown her a perfect sleeper. What it did not find was the wrecked night her tracker kept reporting.

She heard all of this, and she asked one question.

"Then why does my Fitbit say I am sleeping poorly?"

There is no test result that answers that sentence. She was referred for the therapy that treats it and did not go; her insurance would not cover it. She left the way she came, trusting the wrist over the lab.

The clinicians who wrote up her case, and two others like it, named the pattern in 2017. They called it orthosomnia: ortho for straight or correct, the same root as orthodontics, bolted onto sleep. The straightening impulse had turned on the night itself. Now slow down on her question, because every boring explanation for it is available, and each one explains a little and leaves the rest standing.

The first boring explanation is that the tracker was simply wrong, bad sensor, bad number. It is the comforting story, and it is out of date. A modern ring, tested against a full clinical recording, now labels sleep correctly better than nine times in ten and identifies REM sleep right about ninety percent of the time. These devices are good, and getting better, at the one thing they most need to do: telling that you are asleep. But look at where the accuracy actually lives. The older wrist trackers caught genuine wakefulness as seldom as one time in ten, and in many models did not beat a coin flip. A tracker is good at proving you slept and bad at knowing when you lay still and awake. Which means it is weakest at the exact border a worried sleeper stares at: the line between lying there and being gone. Nobody in her file can say precisely what produced that sixty percent. What we can say is that the number was not simply false. It was crude in the one place it needed to be exact.

The second boring explanation is that she was fragile, a rare anxious outlier, and normal people are fine. But the habit is not rare. Wearable use among American adults reached forty-one percent in 2024; nearly half of adults say they have used a device to track their sleep, up from a third two years earlier. And the mechanism underneath her is in all of us. In one experiment, a hundred and sixty-four people were told, at random, that they had gotten above-average or below-average dream sleep the night before. The assigned number, not their own sense of the night, predicted how well they did on cognitive tests that day. A sleep number you believe changes your day whether or not it is true. Nervous people are not the exception here. This is how the wiring works.

The third boring explanation is that this is a literacy problem, and she only needed the numbers explained. Her clinicians explained them. Then they handed her the machine's own better cousin, the lab recording, and in her case the objective proof came twice, the clean apnea test and the full overnight study, and neither one moved her. A man of forty in the same series was fluent in his own numbers, and the fluency only sharpened the trap. His stated goal was eight hours of deep sleep every night, a target no clinician would set, because there is no ideal number of deep-sleep hours. And the deep-sleep figure he chased was handed to him each morning by a device that cannot reliably tell deep sleep from light. He did not bring a diary to his first visit. He brought his phone, and gave his own life back to the room "according to my data." More numbers were never going to help him. The watching was the problem, not the shortage of it.

So take them together. The device is crude at exactly the border she was watching. The worry is real, and shared by millions. The numbers are hard to argue a person out of. Each is a piece of her, and none of them is the whole. What they sit on top of is structural, and it is the same for everyone who wakes and reaches for the score before reaching for the feeling of being rested.

Sleep is the one thing you cannot try to do. Everything else you might set as a goal, you can push: take more steps, lift more, breathe slower on command. Effort helps. Sleep is the state effort destroys. The harder you reach for it, the further off it moves, and the therapy that actually works on insomnia is built partly on getting you out of the bed you are straining in. Now hand a person a nightly grade on that state, and watch what a low grade tells them to do. Try harder. Go to bed earlier, lie there longer, guard the number. Every patient in that first study was doing exactly this, spending extra hours in bed to make the tracker report more sleep, which is the precise behavior that worsens insomnia. The score does not just describe a bad night. It issues the instruction that arranges the next one.

A step count rewards the effort it measures: move more, the number climbs. A sleep score does the opposite. It grades a state that effort spoils, and then it follows you into the one room where being watched is the whole problem. It hands you a verdict on the night, and the body, it turns out, will take the verdict's word over its own.

The people who named orthosomnia are careful about what they cannot claim. They did not know how these patients slept before the device arrived, or whether the worry came first and the tracker only gave it numbers. Fair enough. You do not need proof that the score caused the ruin to see the loop it feeds.

And none of this makes the sensor the enemy. The honest version of the argument has to sit with the man it helped. He was sixty-nine. His wife had given him the tracker, and it kept showing him restless stretches in the night. He took that worry to his neurologist, who sent him for a real overnight study, which found severe sleep apnea: fifty-four breathing events an hour. He started treatment and stuck to it, and he credited the device with pointing him there. He was right to. That is not vanity. Sleep apnea is common: by one global estimate, close to a billion adults between thirty and sixty-nine have it to some degree. His was found because a run of restless nights on a small screen sent him looking. A wrist that does that, that turns a vague worry into a real test, is doing something one lab visit cannot. Trackers are good at what plays out over weeks and hides in the moment: the timing that drifts on travel, mapped across a million and a half nights of real trips; the schedule quietly wrecking a shift worker; the pattern under the noise. Read that way, as a trend a person with a real question brings to someone who can interpret it, the device earns its keep.

But stay with that same man a little longer, because his case does not resolve. It doubles. The tracker that steered him toward a real diagnosis became a voice he could not stop citing. He kept turning his own care back toward it. "I know you don't like this," he told his clinicians, "but my Fitbit tells me." The device that had pointed him toward a genuine diagnosis had become a narrator he trusted over the room, over his doctors, over the morning he was actually having. The help and the harm were not two different devices. They were one device, read two different ways.

<PullQuote>
A trend a clinician reads is a tool. A grade you wake into is a verdict.
</PullQuote>

That is the line, and it is a narrow one. A trend a clinician reads is a tool. A grade you wake into is a verdict. The first answers a question you brought to it. The second asks one you did not: how did you do. And sleep is not something you do. It is what arrives when you finally stop doing, which is why "how did you do" is the single question with the power to undo it.

So here is the small, unfashionable instruction, and it costs you nothing the sensor was ever really giving you. Keep the data if it answers a real question. Hand it to a doctor if it flags something across weeks. Then turn off the morning score. Not the ring. The verdict. Stop grading the night.

Because tomorrow you will wake up, and for one second, before the number, you will know something. You will know whether you feel rested. It is a rough, unglamorous, unquantified thing, and it is yours. The little light on the nightstand is already lit. It has already decided how you slept. You are still allowed to disagree.
