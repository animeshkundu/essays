A boy would sit through his Zoom classes, and when the last one ended, the school day was not over. He went into the bathroom and started on his jaw.

He recited the alphabet while he stretched the muscles, the way a video had shown him. Some days it took about an hour. He did it after class, every day, and he took photos to check whether it was working. He was in seventh grade. Later, at nineteen, he described it to the Child Mind Institute: "I didn't feel like I was done with school until I finished my jawline routine."

Read that again. It moves the end of the day. Class was over. Whatever counted as school was over. But the day could not close until the jaw was done.

This was not the extreme end of it. There are boys who take testosterone for this, or hit their own facial bones trying to reshape them. He had looked at the other boys on the screen, decided their jawlines were sharper, and gone looking for a way to catch up. He found rubber chewing blocks and chin exercises being sold on TikTok. He called the whole thing "a self-care, self-improvement session," and he meant it kindly. "I felt more satisfied with myself," he said, "a little more confident." That is the part to sit with. It was sensible. It felt good. And it still would not let him go.

There is a word for what he was doing. He had been maxxing.

The word comes out of games. People who study slang trace it to min-maxing in role-playing games, where you pour points into some traits and starve others to win. By 2026 it has left the games far behind and fastened itself to nearly everything. People sleepmax with tart cherry juice and mouth tape. They proteinmax by stirring powder into water. They fibermax by dropping chia seeds into soda. They booksmax, and they spermmax with raw garlic. There is relaxmaxxing, which means doing the bare minimum, and there is whimsy-maxxing, which a Better Homes source defines as "intentionally adding joy and playfulness to everyday life," and which in practice means heart-shaped ice cubes, butter pressed into hearts, googly eyes stuck on the coffee maker. There is PTO-maxxing: laying your vacation days against the public holidays so that ten of them buy forty days off, so that rest becomes a spreadsheet and the question turns from when do I need a break into what is the return on this particular Tuesday.

Most people saying these words are joking, and most of the habits under them are fine. Sleep is good. Fiber is good. Reading is good. A day off is good. A heart-shaped ice cube has never hurt anyone. That is the reason to take the frame seriously instead of waving it off as a funny suffix. The joke is doing real work while everyone agrees it is only a joke.

Here is what the word carries in with it. In a game your character has a fixed pool of points, and every point you spend on strength is one you did not spend on speed, so you pick a trait and make it win. Maxxing lifts that rule out of the game and lays it over a life. Pick a trait. Pick a number that stands in for it: a photo, a sleep score, grams of protein, a rank. Push the number up. Check it against other people. Then do it again, because a number can always be higher.

The trap is not the effort but the place the number lives. You can sleep a full night and not know whether it counted until the ring on your wrist grades it in the morning. You can eat a balanced dinner and go to bed feeling behind on protein. The boy could finish every class and still not be done with school. The thing that decides whether you have done enough is no longer the fed, rested, tired feeling in your own body. It is a metric outside the body, and a metric does not get tired, and it never says enough.

It is easy to mistake this for older things. It looks like vanity, which is old. It looks like teenage insecurity, which is old. Given where looksmaxxing began, in incel forums almost a decade ago, it looks like one bitter corner of the internet, and it did begin there. But vanity never used to arrive with a scale that sorts human faces into "subhuman," "normie," "Chadlite," and "Chad." Insecurity never used to hand a teenager the exact words to look at his friend and see, as one of them put it, "these new things that supposedly define how good you look that no one would ever really think about before," and then rate the friend's eyes as a bad angle. The wish to be better is old. What is new is that it now comes with a number attached, and someone to lose to.

You can watch the arithmetic happen out loud. On one site, two strangers put their faces on a split screen and green dots crawl across their features while software compares the tilt of the eye, the ratio of the eyelid opening, the width of the nose against the face. It hands out points, win or lose, and gives each player a rank on a ladder borrowed from chess. One nineteen-year-old ran hundreds of these matches in a single week. "I don't think anyone takes it seriously," he said, which is what people say about the things that have already gotten inside. A psychologist watching the trend was blunter: some people move to numbers, some people move to religion.

Now the strongest objection, and it is strong. A number can help. A schedule can help. Another boy the same age told the same interviewers that lifting weights got him through puberty, that a body changing on its own felt frightening and the gym gave him, in his words, "some agency as a man." He is right, and the evidence backs him. Most Americans really do fall short on fiber, more than nine in ten women and nearly all men. Exercise is a real treatment for depression, not a slogan. A goal you can measure is one thing that actually gets a person off the couch. Peel the suffix off and most of these routines are just the dull, sound advice a doctor would give you. To call all of it a sickness would be its own kind of lie.

So the line is not effort against no effort. Wanting to improve is not the problem. Neither are routines, or even the wish for proof. The problem opens up one step later, at the moment proof becomes the price of being allowed to stop.

It reaches the most wholesome habit there is. Sleep researchers have a name for what happens when people start chasing the number their sleep tracker gives them: orthosomnia, a perfectionistic quest for the ideal sleep. One of them describes a twenty-seven-year-old whose tracker kept telling her she was barely sleeping. An overnight lab study showed she was sleeping. She still did not believe the tracker was wrong. In a 2025 survey, more than half of adults said they had tracked their sleep with a device, and among those who owned one, about one in eight reported sleep-related worry, the kind researchers file under orthosomnia. The wholesome version of maxxing does exactly what the ugly version does. It just does it quietly, to people who would never touch a chewing block, and it looks like health the entire way down.

There is a number for the size of the comparison all of this runs on. In a 2026 analysis of a national survey of American high schoolers, the students who used social media hourly or more often were 8.1 percentage points likelier than non-users to think of themselves as overweight. It is a link, not a cause. But it is the shape of the thing: the more hours a person lives inside the feed, the more an ordinary body reads as one falling short. Nearly half of American teenagers now say they are online almost constantly. A group of them, tracked for a week, got a median of 237 phone notifications a day. One described the feed studying him until, in his words, "you just scroll without really looking."

It would be dishonest to say the feed only wounds. On the same apps where about a third of teenage girls say they feel bad about their bodies in a given week, closer to six in ten say they feel good about or at ease with their bodies just as often. The scorecard does not break everyone. It does not need to. It only needs to move the place where enough gets decided, out of the person and into a number they can keep almost reaching.

The boy is nineteen now. The bathroom, the alphabet, the hour, the photographs, all of it is years behind him, and he can lay it out plainly, which not everyone who lived it can. It would be easy to file him under teenage boys and jawlines and move on. That would be the mistake. His story was never about a jaw. It was about a day that used to have a door at the end of it, the plain door you walked through when class let out, and the door quietly moved, so that finishing school was no longer enough to reach it.

The door has been moving for a lot of us. The night you slept does not count until the app scores it. The dinner you ate leaves you owing protein. The week off has to justify its return. The small joy on the counter has to be shaped like a heart to prove it happened. None of it is extreme. That is the whole of the problem. Somewhere inside all this reasonable, mild, well-meant self-care, a great many people can no longer quite say the plainest word there is, the one word a twelve-year-old could not reach at the end of a normal school day. Done.
