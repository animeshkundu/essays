A woman in Ohio was being treated for breast cancer. Her family had thirteen doctors in it. One of them, married in, was a physician far off in Southern California, nowhere near the room where she sat and heard what her own doctors told her. After the visits she passed along what she had heard. Later they learned that some of it had come through wrong, and some of the most important parts had not come through at all.

Thirteen doctors in one family. And the visit still did not survive the retelling.

Here is the number that should end the argument about whose fault that is. Between forty and eighty percent of what a doctor tells you is forgotten immediately, and almost half of what you do keep, you keep wrong. That is not a figure about careless people. In one study of emergency room patients, most walked out having misunderstood at least one part of their own care, and they noticed something was off only about a fifth of the time. You do not just forget. You forget without knowing you forgot.

So run the boring explanations first. Maybe the family did not care enough. Thirteen doctors in one family say otherwise. Maybe the doctors were careless. Maybe the patient could not follow the words. But a family with thirteen physicians in it does not lack for people who speak the language. None of those are enough, and one explanation is left standing that is not a flattering one for medicine: the spoken visit is a bad container. It leaks on the way home. Memory quietly edits it down and hands you the edit as if it were the whole thing.

This is why I think the most useful medical AI of the next few years is not the clever one we keep being promised, the genius that sits beside your doctor and never misses a diagnosis. It is the dull one in your pocket that records what was said and gives it back to you afterward, in plain words, so the family across the country can hear the actual sentence instead of your memory of it. One of that family's thirteen doctors went and built exactly that.

The case is not as settled as it sounds. The old part of this idea has a real record. Give a cancer patient a recording of the visit where the news broke, and she remembers more of it later, and plays it back for the people who could not be in the room. That is decades of study. The new part, the AI that rewrites the visit into a tidy summary, has almost none. No trial yet shows that a consumer app's summary makes you remember more, take your pills, worry less, stay safer, or trust anyone. It is a promising layer laid over proven ground, not proven itself.

The cost is real too, and the objectors are not wrong about the price. Three things change the moment the phone goes on the counter.

The room changes first. About a third of oncologists think being recorded can steer a visit away from the hardest subjects, the prognosis, the odds; about a quarter think it makes the conversation less detailed. The private question a good doctor is trained to ask, whether someone at home is hurting you, is meant to be asked with no one else in the room. It is harder to ask into a microphone the family also holds.

The family boundary changes next. The recorder that lets your daughter three states away hear the exact words is the same recorder running when the visit turns to a teenager's secret, or to a diagnosis the patient is not ready to hand to anyone yet. A memory aid you share is one you cannot fully take back. Once it is sent, whoever holds it can copy it, forward it, keep it.

Then the holder changes. Your own recording was never in your medical chart; it starts outside it, and where it goes next depends on the app. It might stay as text on your phone. It might become audio in a company's cloud, a summary in a shared family room, a prompt fed to somebody else's model. One of these apps reserves the right to strip the names off your recordings and transcripts and license them to drug makers, analytics firms, and other companies; another makes its free tier the price of letting your anonymized visits train its AI. And the model that writes the plain-words summary can, by the app's own admission, drop a dose, miss the point where a symptom means call us now, or sand the caution off an instruction until "stop if you feel dizzy" reads like an ordinary line. In 2023 the FTC fined a popular prescription-savings app for handing users' medication data to advertisers after promising it never would.

That is the deal. To stop losing your own care on the way home, you make a stranger the keeper of it.

Both things are true at once, and neither cancels the other. A recorder in that Ohio room would have caught the exact words the family lost. It would also have carried her cancer, in her own voice, onto a server none of them would ever see.

We keep waiting for the machine that thinks like a doctor. The one that matters just remembers what yours said, and now it owns the memory too.
