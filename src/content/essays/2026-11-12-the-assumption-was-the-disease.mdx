She could not afford the bus fare. A young woman in her twenties was a patient at a clinic in Harare, expected back for follow-up. She died by suicide.

The psychiatrist who had been treating her did not decide that she needed a better therapist. He decided that the distance between a patient and help was the disease. Around 2006, in Mbare, a crowded township in Harare, he trained older women from the neighborhood, grandmothers, to sit with patients on ordinary benches in the clinic yard. The bench became the clinic. No fee. No appointment. No travel beyond the yard. No diagnostic label. Just a greeting, a seat, and a conversation.

That is the story that built the Friendship Bench, and it is a story about access. It does not prove the bench works. It proves that a bus fare can be fatal.

The mental health crisis is not a shortage of therapists. It is the assumption that healing requires one. For common mental disorders, a trained grandmother on a bench is better than the care people actually get, because the care they actually get is a clinic they cannot reach. And if that is true, then for that condition the credential is not the active ingredient. Proximity is.

What happens on the bench is not a chat. A person is screened with a fourteen-item questionnaire developed in Shona, the local language. The distress has a local name, kufungisisa, which means thinking too much. It covers worry, rumination, and the cluster of symptoms we group as common mental disorders. The grandmother does not diagnose. She follows a structured problem-solving conversation: name the problem, break it into pieces, choose one piece, plan one small action for the week, follow up. She does not prescribe. She does not manage a crisis. If a person is suicidal, psychotic, or severely depressed, the case goes up to the clinic and, if needed, to a psychiatrist. The bench is the front door, not the whole house.

In a cluster-randomized trial in Harare primary care, adults with common mental disorders were assigned either to the bench or to enhanced usual care. Those who sat with the grandmothers had lower symptoms at six months. The women delivering the help had no clinical degrees. That is the anomaly. Why would that work?

Maybe the patients were not really sick. They were. They were screened, and they met the criteria for common mental disorders.

It might be only kindness. Kindness is part of it, but kindness alone does not mean patients leave with a small plan, and the grandmother follows up. The grandmother follows steps. She helps name a problem, breaks it into pieces, and agrees on one small action for the week. That is the mechanism.

Attention could be the whole story. The control group also got screening and referral, so attention alone does not explain the difference. The trial also reported improved functioning alongside mood.

Cultural fit could be the whole explanation. Partly. The conversation happens in the patient's own words and idioms. But cultural fit is not an alternative to the method. It is the method working.

The grandmother's authority might explain it. Partly. An older woman with standing in the community carries weight a clinician without that standing does not. But that authority is not magic. It is earned by training and supervision, and it fails when those are removed.

Cost could explain it. Cost explains why people came. It does not explain why they got better. Once seated, the difference is the conversation.

The trial measured outcomes, not mechanisms. What follows is my reading, not its finding. For common mental disorders, the active ingredient is not the degree. It is a nearby person who can name the problem, break it down, and agree on one small action, inside a relationship that does not require money, travel, or a label. If that reading holds, healing, for that condition, is logistics plus structure plus presence. The professional is not useless. The professional is often unreachable, and for this condition unreachable is the same as absent.

Now the hard part. The strongest case against this is not that grandmothers are bad. It is that suffering is not one thing. Psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe depression with suicidal intent, trauma, substance use: these are not thinking-too-much problems. They need medication, risk assessment, sometimes hospitalization, and accountability when things go wrong. A bench in a public yard has no prescription pad, no crisis protocol, no power to hold someone through the night. The trial did not measure suicide outcomes. It did not compare the bench to professional therapy. It compared the bench to enhanced usual care, which in that setting meant screening results fed back to clinic staff with referral as usual. That is a low bar. Clearing it proves the bench is better than nothing. It does not prove the bench is better than a good clinician.

The trial was also run with trained, supervised grandmothers. Remove the supervision and you do not have the trial. You have a bench. The grandmothers are not archetypes. They are older women, often widowed, often carrying their own poverty and grief, sitting with other people's pain for long hours, sometimes for little or no pay. Treating them as an inexhaustible source of warmth converts scarcity into virtue and lets systems off the hook. There is also a real risk of a two-tier system: a bench for the poor, a clinic for the rich, both called care.

The woman who could not afford the bus fare was already in psychiatric care. The failure was not the absence of a bench. It was the distance. She did not die because no one could help her. She died because help was a bus ride away.

So the bench is not a cure and not a replacement. It is a door. The question is not whether grandmothers can do therapy. It is whether healing is a credential or a distance. If healing is what happens when a person is heard, named, and given one small thing to do, then the grandmother is doing it. If healing requires a credential, then most of the world is untreated by definition, and we should stop pretending the word describes anything real. I do not know which answer we should use.
