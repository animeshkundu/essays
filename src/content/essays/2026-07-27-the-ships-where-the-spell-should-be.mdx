In a Roman tomb at Oxyrhynchus, in Middle Egypt about a hundred and ninety kilometers south of Cairo, someone laid a small folded and sealed papyrus on a dead man's body, over the space between his chest and his abdomen. It is written in Greek. Read across the centuries by a papyrologist and checked by a linguist, the lines turn out to be part of Book II of the Iliad: the Catalogue of Ships, the long roll call of the fleet that sailed against Troy.

We know almost nothing else about him. He was an adult man. He was found in 2025, in Tomb 65, in a part of the necropolis that ancient robbers had already worked over, among Roman mummies and painted coffins broken open long ago. The packet that reached us was folded, sealed, and by then cracked into fragments. That is the whole of the scene: a prepared body, a sealed Greek packet where a protective charm would be expected, and modern hands able to read the words but not the reason.

Because this is not where such a text belongs. Papyri turn up with the mummies of Roman Egypt often enough; that part is ordinary. But when they are sealed into a burial like this, their content is mainly magical: charms, spells, protective words meant to guard the dead on the far side. The mission that lifted it calls this the first time a Greek literary text has been found deliberately made part of a mummification. And of every text one might expect to do the work of a charm, this is among the least likely. The Catalogue of Ships is hundreds of lines of bookkeeping: which town sent which captain, and how many black ships each one launched. A list of ships was lying where a spell should be.

It is tempting to fill the blank. Maybe the man loved the poem. Maybe he was a soldier, or a Greek far from home, and the ships stood for the voyage out or the voyage back. Maybe the list was chosen for a meaning we have lost. The honest answer is that we do not know, and the specialists who lifted the papyrus are careful to say so. They do not have his name, his trade, whether he could read, whether he ever heard a line of it. They say plainly that they cannot even explain why papyri were thought to protect the dead at all. So set the guesses aside, and ask only what the object itself can prove.

It can prove less than we want and more than it looks. This was not scrap stuffing. The specialists say it was not part of the plastered casing and not packing shoved in to fill a cavity, but a sealed packet placed on the body on purpose, probably at the last stage of embalming, one of a group closed with clay seals. So a hand chose it and set it down. Whether that hand thought it precious or handy, chosen or spare, we cannot say, and nothing in the tomb will tell us. But for a hand to lay a copy of Book II on a dead man, a copy of Book II had to exist, and be present at that burial, and be foldable, and be laid into the grave. That is the one part of the story the tomb keeps: not the reason, only the act. The awe, if there was awe, left no mark. The hand did.

Which is a smaller fact than we wanted, and a stranger one. We like to think a great work lasts because each age holds it sacred and keeps it above the ordinary. But reverence preserves nothing while it stays reverence. It saves a text only when it turns into handling: a copy made, a roll stored, a passage drilled into a child, a packet laid on a body. Schooling is reverence with ink on its fingers. Scholarship is reverence that argues over a word. Even this burial, whatever it meant, was a pair of hands at work.

Set the tomb aside and look at how old writing reaches us at all. Start with the city the man was buried in. We can read half a million scraps of Oxyrhynchus because the city carted its used paper out to mounds at the desert's edge, and it never rained there, and the heaps sat above the water table and did not rot. Two excavators from Oxford dug that ancient rubbish from 1896 to 1907. What they pulled out, thirty feet down in garbage, has been called the closest thing we have to finding the Library of Alexandria in a landfill: tax returns and shopping lists tangled with poetry no one had read in a thousand years, including verses by a poet of Lesbos whose nine books had otherwise shrunk to a single complete poem. The fragments were shipped to Oxford in whatever was to hand, including an empty biscuit tin. The heap honored none of it. It only kept it dry.

The poem itself was no stranger to this city. In a modern digital catalogue of literary papyri, Book II of the Iliad shows up in eighty-six records from Oxyrhynchus alone, and Homeric material is heavily represented across the whole catalogue. A large part of why is schooling. Homer was a central school text, and students met him through copying: teacher models written out again, short passages and dictations, lines split into pieces with glosses set between them, on the shaded porch of a temple or on bare ground under a tree. That is how a great deal of poetry stayed in the world at all: not sealed away, but handed to beginners and written out again. Whether the sheet in Tomb 65 came from a school, a library, a shop, or somewhere else, we do not know. The record shows only that in a city like this, Book II lived in many copies and many hands.

Handling reached even into how the dead were dressed. For centuries, Egyptian undertakers built mummy cases out of old paper, layering and plastering used papyrus into masks and breast pieces. By one old account, an undertaker would buy up a neighbor's waste-paper basket and press the contents into the shell for the body in front of him. Whole private archives, one man's letters and accounts from around 250 BC, reached us because they had been built into the casing of a coffin. The writing was saved by being demoted, from something read to something used.

This is not only a trick of the dry Egyptian sand. Two mathematical treatises that survive nowhere else on earth came down to us inside a medieval prayer book. Parchment was scarce after the sack of Constantinople in 1204, and that is one likely reason a scribe took an old copy of the mathematics, scraped the ink away, turned the pages, and wrote his prayers across them. What he needed was the parchment. The proofs survived as a side effect of that need, buried under the prayers, legible again only when modern scholars lit the erased pages with ultraviolet and blew single characters up to the size of a screen, reading beneath the prayers and beneath four saints later painted on top. The reuse wounded the text and saved it in the same act.

Reverence did save the Iliad. It was no accident that this was the poem in that tomb and not some forgotten one. Homer sat at the center of Greek schooling; scholars in Alexandria argued over his lines and fixed his text; he was in print in Florence within decades of the first presses. Without that long chain of people who thought him worth copying, there would be no Book II for anyone to fold into a burial. Reverence built the copies, and that is not small.

But look again at what that reverence was. None of it was a hush around an untouchable thing. It was schoolrooms and copyists and editors and a poem worn smooth by use. The regard for Homer never preserved a single line by itself. It preserved lines by turning, over and over, into the plain work of hands: writing him out, teaching him, correcting him, and, once, in a tomb at Oxyrhynchus, laying him on a dead man for a reason no one now can give. Reverence is real. But it only saves what it is willing to handle.

There is a quiet restraint running under this whole find. The specialists took papyri only from bodies already in poor enough condition that the packets lay exposed. The intact ones they left in place. The sealed packets they left sealed, to be scanned rather than torn open. Careful hands, many centuries on, reading the Greek and leaving the man alone, still unable to say why a list of ships was the last thing laid on him.

So we are left with the list of ships where the charm should be, and no way to read the reason behind it. Maybe the poem meant everything to him. It may have been a thing of power to whoever placed it, or only the material that was available. The tomb will not say, and it does not pretend to. It keeps only what a tomb can keep: a folded packet, a sealed edge, the fact of a hand that laid the ships of a war down on a dead man's body. We keep asking what the poem meant to the man. The tomb keeps answering with a list of ships.
