Around noon, an infrared thermometer read the pavement in one Sacramento neighborhood: 86 degrees. Six miles south, an infrared thermometer at the same hour read the ground at 137.

Nothing you own can feel that difference the way your own hand can. There is a test dog owners use before a summer walk. Hold your hand to the pavement and keep it there while you count to ten. If you cannot last the whole ten, the ground is too hot for a paw. Your hand is the instrument. At 86 it rests there, bored. At 137 you will not reach ten.

Same city. Same afternoon. Six miles apart. One of those sidewalks you can stand on. The other you plan your day around.

We have argued for a long time about which streets are walkable, and we have mostly meant one thing by it: how far. How far to the store, the school, the train. The two scores that rank a walkable street, Walk Score and the federal walkability index, both measure it that way, and neither would have caught what set those two Sacramento streets apart, because neither asks whether anything stands between the ground and the sun. That, more than distance, is starting to decide whether a street can be used on foot at all.

Start with what it was not. It was not two different climates. Six miles is one city under one heat wave, covered by a single line on the same weather report. And that line describes the air, in the shade, a few feet up; it is not the 137 the pavement was doing to a hand. The heat those two streets threw off came from more than one thing: the pavement, the roads, the buildings, all of it warmed through the morning. But the thing that moves most, at the height of a standing body, is whether something blocks the sun before it lands. One street had cover. The other had far less.

Here is the part the weather app cannot tell you. The number it shows is measured in the shade, away from the pavement and the walls. Step into direct sun and what you feel can run up to 15 degrees past that number, and the app has no way to know. A body on a sidewalk is heated twice: from above by the sun, and from below and beside by pavement and walls that soaked it up all morning and now hand it back. Shade changes that load of radiation far more than it changes the air temperature, and the load is most of what your skin is actually reporting.

You can put numbers on both halves of that. In one Arizona study, on a record-hot afternoon, the air in a street canyon peaked near 119 degrees, while the radiant heat pressing on a body standing there reached about 169. Of everything that fed that load, shade was the single thing that moved it most. A separate study in the same desert city surveyed 1,284 people, across four seasons, about how hot they felt. The reading that tracked their answers best was not the air temperature. It was the globe temperature: the heat inside a black globe that soaks up the same radiation pressing on a body at that spot, in sun or shade. That one reading explained about half of how hot people said they felt. In summer, stepping into shade dropped what they reported by roughly a step, from hot to merely warm, and it did that without moving the air thermometer much at all.

Which is why those two scores are half blind. Walk Score, the number stapled to millions of listings, counts how many useful things sit within a short walk and stops counting after thirty minutes on foot. The federal walkability index counts intersections, transit stops, and the mix of what is nearby. Neither counts shade, canopy, pavement heat, or the hour of the day. Two sidewalks can earn the same score and be two different climates: one you can cross in July, one you cannot.

At first the cost of the hot street is small and easy to miss. You cross to the shady side without deciding to. You skip the bench in the sun and take the one under the awning. You cut the errand short, or move it to the evening, or drive the six blocks you would have walked in May.

Then you reach the people who cannot move the errand to the evening.

The bus stop is where this stops being about preference. You cannot walk faster to escape it; the rule of the place is that you wait. More than two thirds of transit riders walk to their stop, so the heat is not separate from the trip. It is the trip. In Los Angeles, only about a quarter of Metro bus stops have a shelter that casts real shade, and some of those shelters, poorly aimed, shade the waiting rider on just 8 percent of sunny days. The shade exists. It simply falls behind the waiting area instead of over the people standing in it.

Phoenix is the hardest case. Over three years, county medical examiners tied at least 40 heat deaths to people at bus stops, and dozens more to people on benches, in parking lots, on sidewalks. One man was found at a stop and reached the emergency room with a body temperature of 109.4.

And there are the children. About half of California's public school students go to schools with little to no tree cover. On the campuses one study measured, blacktop ran from 40 percent to nearly 60 percent of the ground. A scientist whose work is measuring how much of that heat the blacktop throws back at a child's body said it plainly: not suitable for kids. The child does not get to move recess to the evening either.

It would be easy to run too far with this, so here is the strongest case against me. Shade does not make a street worth walking. It only makes it bearable. A canopy over an empty six-lane road is just a cooler place to have nowhere to go. Distance still matters; a store you can reach, a crossing that does not put you in front of turning traffic, a bus that comes, all of it still does the real work, and a tree over an arterial does not make the crossing humane. Shade can even turn on you. Pack a still, narrow street with dense growth and it can trap the wet air and hold you in it; the same crown that blocks the noon sun can seal the day's heat against the ground after dark. Shade is directional. It is not free, and it is not always good.

All true. Shade cannot shorten a mile, open a store, or summon a bus. But once those things exist, heat decides whether you can use them in the hours around noon. The old sidewalk gets you there. Shade decides whether there is reachable at noon.

So there is a border running through every hot city, and it is not the one on the walkability maps. It is the edge of the shade: the line on the pavement where cover stops and the sun starts. On one side of it you can stand still. Step across, and you begin, without meaning to, to count. A woman in Buffalo, out in a heat wave, needed only a few minutes before she said she felt uncomfortable, and then named shade as the thing that decides whether people can stay outside.

The line is real, and it does not hold still. It slides across the sidewalk as the afternoon turns. It gives out where the trees were never planted, and where one block got canopy and the next got left to bare pavement. Older cities built the cover in: one of them still carries more than 60 kilometers of covered walkway, raised over centuries so a body could cross the town in any weather. Then we learned to score a walk by how far it was, and the shade fell out of the arithmetic. The ground has been keeping its own score all along, in how long you can bear to stand still on it, and the answer changes at a line the walkability maps still do not draw.
