The most realistic experiment anyone had yet run on whether AI helps experienced developers worked exactly once. The people it measured broke it the second time, and how they broke it is the part worth understanding.

In early 2025 a research group ran the first version. They took sixteen seasoned open-source developers, people who had maintained their own large projects for years, and gave them 246 real issues from those projects. Each issue was assigned by coin flip: on this one you may use AI, on that one you may not. The developers recorded their screens and logged their hours. They were paid $150 an hour to be honest about the clock.

The measured result cut against every group that had been asked to predict it. The issues done with AI took nineteen percent longer. The developers themselves had expected AI to speed them up by about a quarter, and even after finishing slower, they came away still believing it had. The economics and machine-learning experts polled beforehand had guessed the tasks would come in around forty percent shorter. Three sets of forecasts, all pointing the same way, all wrong.

That was a real finding for that setting and that moment. Later that year the same group tried to run it again: more developers, newer tools, the agentic kind you hand a whole task to. By the time they reported back, the experiment had come apart in their hands.

The second study did not fail because the answer flipped. It failed because a growing number of developers would not take the deal. When you asked them to submit their real tasks into a randomizer that might forbid AI on half of them, they started quietly leaving out the ones they would hate to do by hand. Between a third and a half of them said, when surveyed, that they held tasks back for exactly that reason. Recruiting got harder, because more people did not want to spend half their paid hours working the old way. Some who did get an issue marked no-AI simply never finished it. One completed none of them. And for the developers running several agents at once, the stopwatch stopped meaning anything, because while one agent worked they were off doing something else.

The group that ran the study was blunt about what it had left them with: an unreliable signal, they wrote, likely a bad proxy for the real effect of these tools. The instrument that had produced a clean, surprising, credible number a year earlier now produced noise, and it produced noise for a specific reason. The thing it was trying to measure had changed the people it was measuring.

Listen to one of them, choosing which issues to submit. "I avoid issues like AI can finish things in just 2 hours, but I have to spend 20 hours. I will feel so painful if the task is decided as AI-disallowed." Another: "my head's going to explode if I try to do too much the old fashioned way because it's like trying to get across the city walking when all of a sudden I was more used to taking an Uber." A third, asked simply to come back for the second round: "I'm torn. I'd like to help provide updated data on this question but also I really like using AI!"

In none of these lines is anyone worried about losing a job. None is talking about whether the code is any good. Each is describing something smaller and stranger: a skill that is still entirely theirs, a way of working they were fluent in a year ago, that now feels like a waste to actually use. The walk is still walkable. They just cannot make themselves take it.

That refusal is the finding. The strongest evidence that these tools have become real is not a number showing they save time; the best realistic test we had of that setting, experienced maintainers timed on their own real issues, can no longer produce that number. The evidence is that skilled people have quietly changed which work they will agree to do without them, and that change is precisely what broke the instrument built to clock them. Speed and willingness have come apart. You can no longer measure the first, because the second will not hold still.

Look at what it takes for a test like this to work, and you can watch each support give way. It needs developers to hand over a fair sample of their real tasks: they stopped, keeping back the ones with the most to gain. It needs them to actually do a task when the coin says no-AI: some would not, and one never did. It needs the hour spent to belong to the task being timed: with agents running in parallel, it no longer did. Every one of those failures is the same event seen three ways. A tool that used to sit beside the work had become the ground the work stood on, and the old task-level design could no longer clock it the way it once had.

A tool is a convenience for as long as people will still do the job without it and merely grumble. It becomes infrastructure the moment doing the job without it feels like a different and worse job, so much worse that people would rather not do the job at all. Call that crossing the refusal threshold: the point where a tool stops being something you reach for and becomes something you stand on. The signal of reaching it is not the stopwatch. It is the flinch.

The crossing shows up cleanly when you set the two studies side by side. The first one still worked, and it is worth asking why. It worked because in early 2025 nobody refused. Compliance was clean; people did their no-AI tasks and reported honest times; the same freedom that made the study realistic, letting developers pick their own real work, sat there harmlessly. A year later that same freedom was the exact hole the study drained out of. The core method had not changed: developers still picked real issues, submitted them for randomization, and logged their hours. The pay had dropped, the tools were newer, the pool was larger. But the thing that actually broke it was none of those. It was that the tool had crossed from convenience to baseline, and a design built on people picking their own real work had quietly assumed it never would.

Set beside that the older, tidier experiment people still cite: ninety-five professional developers asked to write a small web server, the ones with an assistant finishing about fifty-five percent faster. That number is clean because the task was assigned and bounded. It was not pulled from anyone's own backlog, and no one was asked which work they would refuse to do without AI. The more assigned and self-contained the task, the less a person's willingness to take it on enters the measurement, and the easier it is to get a crisp figure. The more the task is the developer's own real work, the more that willingness floods in, and the less any crisp figure survives. Cleanliness and realism trade against each other, and only the realistic side ever draws the flinch.

Then set beside both a task that changed shape. A developer rewrote a widely used JavaScript runtime, more than half a million lines, from one language into another, in about eleven days, using dozens of agent workflows and roughly $165,000 of computer time. By his own account, a small team doing it the traditional way would have needed a full year. That is not the old task done faster. It is a task whose economics changed enough to make a different decision possible: a full year of a small team is one kind of choice, eleven days of one developer steering agents is another. The clearest proof these agents matter is sitting right there, and it is a decision the falling cost of trying finally made reasonable.

The honest objection to all of this is strong, and I do not want to wave it off. A developer who avoids the no-AI condition is telling you what they prefer, not what is true. Maybe they refuse because they enjoy the tool. Maybe fifty dollars an hour is not enough to sit through twenty hours of tedium. Maybe the tasks they withhold are exactly the ones where AI helps most, so the study is now blind in the one spot it most needs to see. Self-selection is not a productivity measurement.

And the time it does capture quietly ignores the rest of the bill: the review, the security holes, the cleanup, the shared understanding of a system that erodes when people can change parts of it they no longer have to understand. We already know feelings here run ahead of facts. In the first study people felt faster while measuring slower. In lab tests, people given an assistant have written less secure code while growing more confident it was secure. Most developers now use AI coding tools of some kind, yet more of them distrust the accuracy of what those tools produce than trust it; adoption has run well ahead of trust. A tool can feel indispensable long before it is, on net, worth it, because relief is easy to feel and cleanup is hard to count.

All of that is true, and it leaves the claim untouched. Every one of those objections is an argument about whether the tool makes people better off. None of them reaches the colder, narrower claim. To refuse the old way, a person has to have already been changed by the new one. You cannot flinch at twenty hours of hand-work unless two hours of assisted work has quietly become your baseline. The refusal does not prove the tool is good for you. It proves the tool is load-bearing: the work around it has been rebuilt to assume it, which is a different thing from proven good. Those are different facts, and we keep folding the second into the first because the first is the one we like to fight about.

So carry the test out with you, because it is not really about coding. When you want to know whether some tool has become real, do not wait on the productivity dashboard; it will be late, and it may lie. Watch instead for the job people now refuse to do without it. The spreadsheet nobody will rebuild by hand. The route nobody will retrace from memory. The colleague who would rather not take the ticket at all if they cannot use the thing. Treat that refusal as the signal to watch: it tends to arrive before the numbers, sometimes years before them, sometimes while the numbers, once they finally come, are still saying the opposite.

Which leaves the developer back at the issue list, deciding what to submit. The skill is still theirs. The old way still works; the code would still compile; the walk would still get them there. They just do not want it, and want has quietly hardened into something closer to cannot. Maybe that is wisdom, the correct read of a tool that really has changed the work. Or maybe it is only what habit feels like from the inside, once the easy way has become the only way you can stand. From where they sit, there is no way to tell the two apart. And the people who built the experiment to answer exactly that are standing beside them, holding the old task-level ruler, the one that no longer measures what it was built to measure, watching the same change take hold in themselves.
