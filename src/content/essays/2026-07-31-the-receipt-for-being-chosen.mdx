In May of 2025, in Britain, a company stopped selling a toy because too many people wanted to buy it.

The toy is a small plush monster called a Labubu. It fits in your palm. It has a bunny's body, an elf's face, and a row of sharp little teeth, and it comes with a loop on top so you can clip it to a bag. For months, people had been lining up for it. Outside one London shop, a woman arrived to find people who had been there since three in the morning, and others who had slept on the pavement overnight. The crowd pressed around the door. Someone was shouting that the toys were gone. She had come for a bag charm. She said she watched people in balaclavas rush the front, felt afraid, and left. Later she said the whole thing was probably better shut down.

So the maker, a company called Pop Mart, shut it down in person. It paused sales of the plush in all sixteen of its UK shops and in its vending-machine kiosks, and said it was doing this for "the safety and comfort of everyone," that no staff had been hurt, that it wanted to act early, and that it was working on "a fairer chance to purchase." The online drops kept running the whole time. The halt was not a total stop. They stopped handing it to you across a counter.

Sit with how strange that is. A business had a product people were desperate to own, at a price they were glad to pay, and its problem was that it could not hand the thing over fast enough without risking someone getting hurt. That is not a problem most businesses ever have. Ask how you build one on purpose.

Because it was built on purpose. Start with the obvious explanation, which is a plain shortage: too many buyers, not enough toys. The sellouts were real. Lines formed hours early and shelves emptied. But a shortage is a story about what you can make, and this was never only that. Within months, after complaints about scalpers, the company said it was producing around thirty million of these plush toys a month, ten times the year before. A company that can make thirty million of a thing in thirty days is not being held back by its factories. The tighter constraint sat upstream of manufacturing, in how the toy was sold: timed drops, sealed identity, a fixed set of outcomes. The scarcity people were fighting through was mostly a design choice.

You do not buy the toy. You buy a sealed box, and you do not know which toy is inside. A Labubu plush set runs to six boxes, each a different character; across Pop Mart's toys the general format adds a rarer hidden edition that can replace a regular one at random. Every box looks identical from the outside, so the only way to find out what you got is to open it. The company prints this on the packaging in plain words: no one knows what's in the box before you open it. None of this was invented for Labubu. Pop Mart was founded in 2010, and by 2019, before this particular monster was famous, sealed mystery boxes already made up more than eighty percent of what it sold. The character itself came from somewhere else, a Hong Kong illustrator's picture books from 2015, licensed to the company around 2019. The machine was there first. The toy just fed it.

Now look at what the machine actually sells, because it is not a plush. A single box costs about twenty-eight dollars. That is the important number, and not because it is high. It is low. It is low enough to do again. Some drops come at a set minute, seven in the evening, sharp, and on the screen you get a counter that says how many chances you have left today, a button that invites you to test your luck just for fun, and an instruction to pick a box and shake it before it opens. You are not buying a toy at seven o'clock. You are buying a maybe.

You already know what a maybe costs, because you have bought one before. It is the lottery ticket, the scratch card, the pull of a slot machine. And the research points somewhere you might not expect: it is the not-knowing, more than the prize, that does the work on you. Uncertain rewards light up the brain more than sure ones do. A separate study found people would pay half again as much for a watch when they were told it was scarce. The box stacks both of those on top of each other. It is a maybe, and it is running out. You are paying one premium to not know, and a second premium to hurry.

Be fair about the line this does not cross. It is not gambling, and the difference is real. A marketing psychologist who studies this put it cleanly: the feeling is very close to gambling, but the risk is not the same, because you always walk away with a product. You are not left holding nothing. It is closer to a lottery ticket that always pays out a small prize, or to flavored toothpaste, a little pleasure folded into a thing you were going to buy anyway. If the spending stays within reason, she said, it is perfectly fine. Hold on to that, because it is true, and the argument only works if you keep it.

The next wrong answer is that people just love the character. They do. It is genuinely well made, a mischievous-looking creature that is supposed to be kind-hearted underneath. But design explains only why you would like the thing. It stops well short of sleeping on a sidewalk for it, fighting over it, or paying hundreds for the exact right one. Plenty of toys are beautifully designed. They sit quietly on shelves. This one does not, and the reason is a detail so small it is easy to miss.

The company lists the height of the plush two ways: with the loop, and without. That loop is the whole point. The toy is built to leave the house. It is made to hang off a bag, in public, where other people can see it. The reveal is only half of it. The wearing is the point. On the street, the reaction shifted from "oh, that's cute" to "is that a Labubu?", and the stranger who asked might pull out their own. A private purchase was engineered into a public signal.

The scarcity is manufactured. The maybe is the product. The object is designed to be seen. Put those three together and you can finally say what is actually being sold, which was never the plush.

What is being sold is the feeling of being chosen. Getting through the drop before it sold out. Pulling the rare one out of the box. Wearing, on your bag, the thing other people are still standing in line for. Scarcity, here, was less a fact about supply than a feeling the selling produced: an emotional product, and a cheap one to make. That is the whole trick: scarcity has become the cheapest way there is to make a grown adult feel chosen.

If it stopped there, you could call it harmless, and mostly you would be right. But there is a receipt.

If this were only play, the story would end when you opened the box. It doesn't. At the front of that London line, one shopper was sure the people ahead of her were resellers: as soon as they had a ticket, she said, apparently they were turning it around for a hundred and fifty pounds, and the ticket was the thing that let you buy a single doll. Nobody had opened anything yet, and the wanting already had a price. A standard doll that retails for about seventeen pounds fifty resells for up to thirty. One rare edition reportedly sold, once, for $10,585, around three hundred and seventy-eight times its retail price. Be honest about the fog here: there is no complete public record of what these actually sell for, and plenty of the jaw-dropping numbers are asking prices, listings nobody bought. But the receipt does not need the outliers. The company's own books tell the same story from above: The Monsters, the line Labubu belongs to, went from about three billion yuan in 2024 to about fourteen billion in 2025, more than a third of everything the company sold.

That resale market is the tell. The moment a feeling has a going rate, it has stopped being only a feeling. The people in that line had not just bought a toy. They had learned to hold wanting as an asset: to pay for the feeling of being chosen, and, if the price was right, to sell it on to the next person who wanted to feel the same way. The receipt is the proof the wanting was never only play.

For most people this really is small and really is fun. A social-media manager in the Philippines described opening a box after a long week as unwrapping a gift to herself. A woman in Singapore thought the toy was ugly until a colleague gave her one, and now it hangs on her bag and starts conversations with strangers, which she called a warm feeling. People dress them in tiny outfits. People get them tattooed. One man with more than a hundred of them says plainly that they are not an investment, that he keeps them for the memories. None of that is a con. None of it is sick. If the money stays in reason, it is fine.

But that is not the objection to the argument. It is the engine of it. The fun is real, and the fun is exactly what got priced. Scarcity did not replace the play. It waited for the play to become visible enough, on a bag, in a feed, in a line, and then it attached a number to it: the price of that joy, proof the joy was valuable, and that somebody worked out how to sell the same feeling twice.

Which is also why you should not trust it too far, because the feeling does not hold its price. Once the company flooded the shelves, thirty million a month, its store count in the Americas nearly tripling in a year, the doll turning up through big retailers for as little as twenty dollars, the resale premiums fell by half or more. Worldwide search interest dropped about ninety percent from its peak. On the day in March 2026 that the company reported a record-breaking year, its stock fell twenty-two and a half percent in a single session, because investors looked at a single character line, the one Labubu came from, supplying more than a third of the revenue, and got nervous. A signal stops being a signal the week your aunt is wearing one too.

We have watched this exact shape before. In the 1990s, Beanie Babies became an adult spreadsheet. People bought twenty-five-dollar software that tracked prices for their stuffed animals and printed out tax summaries and insurance reports. The maker drove up resale by retiring certain toys every few months, so there would always be a scarce one to chase. At the peak, some sold for thousands. Then most of those collections fell below what they cost new. The lesson is durable: a manufactured feeling, once it is priced and traded, holds its value for exactly as long as other people keep wanting to feel it, and not one day more.

So the real story is bigger than the toy or the company. We have gotten very good at buying feelings and calling them objects. It is the midnight drop for almost anything. The sneaker you will never wear. The membership that makes you one of the few. The pre-order for a thing that does not exist yet. Each one is a small purchase of the feeling of being chosen, sold back to you at a markup you agreed to pay.

The plush was more honest than most of them. It came with a loop, so you could show people you had been chosen. And it came with a resale price, so that anyone paying attention could read, to the dollar, what that feeling was worth to the next person in line. Most of what we buy to feel chosen does not print the number that plainly. This one did. The question it leaves behind is not whether a toy was worth it. It is how many of the other things you own came with the same two features, and you only ever noticed the loop.
