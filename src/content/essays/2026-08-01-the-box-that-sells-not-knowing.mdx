She had already tried to stop.

In July of 2025, a 28-year-old in Ireland set herself a rule: no more boxes. Then she broke it and ordered twenty-five. While the parcels moved somewhere through the postal system, she found she still wanted to go out and buy more by hand, right then, in a shop. One of the things she had bought was a mystery plush from a brand she did not even like, bought purely for the feeling of opening something sealed. She opened it. Out came a squishy lobster, and she asked the only sensible question: "Why do I now have this squishy lobster?"

She is getting married. The money going into sealed boxes was money that could have gone toward the wedding. "The buyer's guilt that I get nearly every time after I've spent is insane," she said, and then she asked who could "pop off" £1,000, about $1,300, in a couple of months "on literal shit."

Stop on that scene, because the spending is the ordinary part. The strange part is that she can see all of it. The guilt lands nearly every time she buys. The toys pile up with nowhere to put them. She tried to resell the figures she disliked and found no one else wanted them either; she was ready to let them go for half of what she paid. She is the one who fears the habit has become a gambling problem. None of it is hidden from her. And she keeps reaching for the next box.

The easy verdict writes itself: a grown woman failing at self-control, childish waste with a hobby's paperwork. I think that verdict is lazy, and I think it walks right past what she is actually buying.

Rule out the boring explanations. It is not the object she is after: she bought a plush from a brand she dislikes and came away holding a lobster she never wanted. It is not investment: she cannot sell the ones she has, and she knows it. It is not ignorance: she narrates the whole trap out loud while walking further into it. Take those away, and something is left over, something she keeps paying for that the receipt never names.

She is not buying a toy. She is buying a small, sealed, cheap bet on luck, and that may be one of the more honest purchases a stretched adult can make.

A blind box is a sealed package with identical outsides and a different figure hidden in each one. Pop Mart, the Chinese company that turned this into an empire, is plain about the design: some figures are common, a few are rare, and one is "secret," marked on the box by nothing but a silhouette. You do not really buy the figure. You buy the few seconds before you know the figure. The toy is what is left over afterward, the receipt for a feeling that has already happened.

Priced one at a time, that feeling looks almost free. A single box runs about the cost of lunch, $20 to $28. The arithmetic changes the moment you want a particular figure. Buy a sealed case and Pop Mart guarantees no repeats. Buy loose boxes one at a time, outside that no-duplicate case, and you are paying for the chance of pulling the same figure twice: completing a set of six that way takes, on average, closer to fifteen boxes than six, about $411 before shipping. Chase a rarer secret figure, one Pin for Love Labubu pendant published at one in 168, and the expected cost of landing it is near $3,862. Buy fifty of those boxes and you are still more likely than not to walk away without it. The company is not hiding any of this. Its own investor prospectus told the market that around seven in ten buyers would buy the same blind-box line three or more times to get the design they wanted. Repeat buying is not a flaw in the product. It is the product.

This is not a fringe habit. In 2025, Pop Mart's revenue rose 184.7 percent in a single year; its plush line alone grew more than 560 percent and became the company's biggest category, and its Labubu family of characters cleared RMB14.2 billion on its own. And the buyers are not mainly children. In 2024, for the first time, American adults bought more toys for themselves than for preschoolers, and adult toy buying kept climbing through the first half of 2025. Grown people are doing this on purpose, with their own money, in enormous numbers.

People have always paid for that few-second window. A lottery ticket is a terrible investment and a cheap daydream, and most people who buy one know exactly which of the two they are getting. The dollar buys a weekend of picturing the house, the resignation letter, the flight out. The sealed box sells the same product at close range: not a fortune, not a fantasy that lasts all weekend, but a jolt you can trigger tonight. And unlike the ticket, it leaves something in your hand once the moment is gone.

A lottery ticket that loses is trash. A blind box that loses is still a small object you can clip to a bag, photograph, or stand on a desk. On a single box, the risk has a floor: the worst you do is get a duplicate, and the loss stops at the price of lunch. One box, bought and finished, is about as clean a small bet as an adult can make. But "one box, bought and finished" is the one thing the product is built to prevent. The cap is real for the first box and starting to fail by the fifth, because the whole design is pointed at the next one.

This is a guess, not a law: the big bets an adult is told to make barely register while you are making them. The woman planning the wedding was choosing, without quite saying so, between more sealed boxes and the wedding itself; the money for one is the money for the other, and only one of them pays anything back tonight. Another buyer kept telling herself she wanted to fly to Europe, to see the world, while $4,000 went into "fur, cotton, vinyl, whatever these things are made of." The wedding, the trip abroad, the money set aside: those are bets too, but they are slow, and the body never registers a win, only a long quiet drain. A box is a bet you can watch resolve. You pay, you wait, you open, you know. It is the one wager in a careful adult's week that pays out an answer the same night.

It pays a little status, too. Clip the right figure to your bag and you have joined something. A collector at a Los Angeles meetup called the toys "social currency": "If you know about it, you're in and if you don't, you're outside of it." That is not childish. That is how the expensive adult status markers already work, the watch, the car, the school district, run here at a scale a normal person can clear in an afternoon.

None of this lets the box off the hook.

The strongest case against everything I just said is that the whole thing is engineered, and engineered precisely to keep the buyer chasing that best moment before ownership. The reward arrives on a variable schedule, the same on-and-off pattern that keeps a slot machine profitable. In a survey of more than 7,000 players, money spent on sealed digital "loot boxes," the nearest thing with hard data, tracked with the severity of problem gambling. One researcher who studies these products put the worry flatly: "We are concerned about vulnerable people spending more money and experiencing harm."

And the casualties are real, not hypothetical. In the same reporting that gave us the woman and her lobster, another buyer, 55, who had just passed the California bar exam, had no money left for gas one month and borrowed it, credit-card debt climbing, packages sitting unopened at home because by then the hunt, not the object, was the point. A cashier spent about $400 in a month and felt "sick to my stomach," partly because the livestream app he bought through never showed him a running total until the boxes landed at his door. A third buyer called it "straight gambling" and compared herself to someone who swears off Vegas while doing the exact same thing. The harm here is not that adults buy toys. The harm is that the toy becomes the alibi for buying one more chance.

What nobody can yet say is which way the arrow runs. Do these boxes pull steady people into compulsion, or are the people already wired for it simply the ones who spend? The study that linked this kind of spending to problem gambling was honest enough to admit it could not tell.

That uncertainty cuts against the tidy "it is all just exploitation" verdict as hard as it cuts against mine. Not every buyer is the woman without gas money. Collecting has a stopping rule: you want the set, you finish it, you are done. Compulsion has no stopping rule; it keeps moving the finish line so the next box is always the necessary one. The box itself does not decide which of those two people you are; the buyer brings the vulnerability, and the product supplies the tempo. The mechanics that make one buyer feel briefly, cheaply lucky are the same mechanics that make another hide the spending from the person they share a bank account with. The object is identical. Whether you have a stopping rule is the whole story.

Which brings it back to the woman planning the wedding. Asked whether things like this should be regulated, her first reaction was fast and total: "No! Please don't take these away from me." Then, almost in the same breath, she said they should carry "greater warnings about what you could be sucked into." She wants to keep the bet. She also wants to be warned about it. Both are true at once, and she is not confused about either.

That doubleness is the honest part, and it is the part her sensible purchases never offer her. The little box tells you what it is. It is a bet you will probably lose, for a feeling that lasts about as long as the tape around it, and it says so by telling you nothing about the inside. The wedding, the trip abroad, the money set aside: those arrive wrapped as certainties, as things a responsible person simply does. The sealed box is the rare purchase that admits, right on the outside, that you are paying to not know.

She can see exactly what it costs her. She is asking to keep it anyway. The strange thing is not that she cannot stop. The strange thing is how much more honest her worst purchase is than most of the ones the rest of us file under sensible.
