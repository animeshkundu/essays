Your map knows an unreasonable amount about your walk. It knows the traffic, the roads that are closed, the number of turns each way would cost you. Ask it to take you six blocks and it will weigh all of that, draw one line in blue, and call that line best. It will not mention that half of it is an oven.

Best, on the phone in your pocket, usually means fastest. The word sits inside the blue line so quietly that it stops looking like a choice. It looks like arithmetic. You asked for a route, the machine did the math, here is the answer, and the answer happens to be the one that spends the fewest minutes of your day.

Here is the argument. In a hot city, the humane map is not the one that saves you three minutes. It is the one that admits your body is not a stopwatch.

Start with what the thermometer leaves out. The number on your phone, the air temperature, is one thin slice of what your body is doing on a sidewalk. A body does not just sit in warm air. It takes in radiant heat from six directions at once: the sun overhead, the sky, and then the ground, the walls, and the pavement giving back the heat it soaked up all morning. Researchers have a name for the sum of it, mean radiant temperature, and in a place like Phoenix the gap it opens is not subtle. In full sun the mean radiant temperature can pass 150 degrees Fahrenheit. Step into shade on the same block, in the same air, and it can fall below 100. Same street, same minute, two different worlds for the thing that has to walk it.

To find out whether a map could route by that second world instead of the clock, a lab at a university in Tempe, Arizona built one. Then, to check it, they did the plain thing: pushed a cart of weather instruments across campus in the heat. Over twelve days across different seasons they rolled the sensors along the routes the tool suggested and compared what the model predicted against what the instruments read on the pavement. One of them put the thing they were measuring in ordinary terms: above about 105 degrees of air temperature, you can feel "the intense longwave radiation radiating off the asphalt and building facades like an open oven." Buildings, sidewalks, and roads drink the sun all day and hand it back to whoever walks past.

The results were small in a way that ought to embarrass the clock. Over those twelve days, the tool found a cooler route for more than seven trips in ten. The added distance ran from about 25 feet to less than a city block. On average the walker gave up almost nothing and came out under an experienced heat load about four and a half degrees Fahrenheit lower. One of the researchers put the cost in even plainer terms: a detour of about two extra minutes can buy you a well-shaded way through.

Set that beside the heat it spares you. On one side, a body standing in radiant heat above 150 degrees. On the other, a detour no longer than a block, often only a few dozen feet. The shortest way was quietly charging the body a great deal to save the clock almost nothing.

None of this is exotic knowledge. People already walk this way when they can. In one set of experiments in a hot city, walkers treated distance spent in shade as if it were only 0.86 as long as the same distance in sun. They went out of their way, and their feet did the accounting. The tool only puts a number on what those feet already knew.

Cities knew this before software did. Nearly 62 kilometers of covered walkway thread through Bologna, a tradition traced to a statute from 1288, built to carry public life along in any weather at all. That is a routing algorithm made of stone. It decided, centuries ago, that a walk is a thing a body does in the weather. The phone in your pocket inherited a colder idea. It learned the city as a graph of edges and travel times, layered traffic and tolls on top, and kept calling the quickest path best. A decade ago, other researchers built a prototype that scored city routes by beauty and quiet instead of speed. It never touched heat. But it showed that a route could be judged by something other than the clock.

It would be easy to stop here and say: make shade the default, hand everyone the cooler line. Easy, and too fast. The honest objection to that is stronger than the people who love the idea tend to admit.

A default is a promise, and a promise has to hold at every hour, for every body, without a lab attached. Time and distance are crude, but they are stable. A minute is a minute at noon and at dusk. Distance does not change when a cloud passes. Shade is nothing like that. At noon a tall building may throw almost no shadow onto the sidewalk, because the sun is nearly overhead, while a tree still holds its canopy over your head. And the tree is not a clean good either: the same canopy that cuts the afternoon radiant load by tens of degrees can raise it again after sunset. A map that swears the left side of the street is cool may be reading the street's centerline instead of the sidewalk you are actually on. The satellite images that feed these models can be out of date. Trees grow, a path is closed for construction, the shade shifts by season, and the model may be reading last year's shadows.

And shade, chased blindly, runs over things that matter more than shade. A cooler detour with no curb ramp is not a cooler route for the roughly 30 million Americans whose disabilities affect how they move down a sidewalk, nearly half of them using a wheelchair, a cane, crutches, or a walker. A street picked for its noon shadow can be the wrong street to walk after dark. In one Australian town, more than half the routes people actually walked had poor shade or none, and dozens of the shaded stretches that did exist ran along heavy traffic, where a cool sidewalk can still leave the street feeling unsafe to cross. The Tempe tool is honest about its own edges: it covers one campus and the downtown blocks beside it, its makers say plainly it is not built to serve much traffic, and modeling the heat of a whole city in real time would take hours of computing it does not yet have. Sometimes you are simply late. The train is leaving, the meeting has started, the battery is dying and the phone is begging for the fewest turns home. Fastest is not a moral failure. It is often exactly right.

Put it at full strength: duration and distance are blunt, but they are trustworthy, and shade is neither, not yet. Make it a summer option, a prompt on the hot days, fine. But not the thing the map hands you by default, not until a map knows the body, the sidewalk, and the street as well as it already knows the clock.

Every line of that is true. It defeats one claim: that shade should be the map's default. It leaves a harder one standing.

Because the claim was never "route everyone through shade, always." What the shade map exposes is smaller and harder to shake: fastest equals best was never neutral either. It is a preference that learned to pass for a measurement. It decided, on your behalf and without telling you, that the minutes you save are worth more than the heat your body takes on. That is a defensible choice. It is one choice among several, and it was made for you before you asked. The moment a second map exists that scores the same walk a different way, the first map's "best" stops being the truth and goes back to being an opinion, which is all it ever was.

A lead researcher on the tool learned this on foot, long before the software. Years of walking that heat made him shade-conscious, so out walking he hunts shade in real time, by instinct. The tool was an attempt to hand that instinct to people who never learned it. He is blunt about a different number, the temperature on your phone: a low reading, he warns, can make a hot street more dangerous than it looks, because that low reading is the one you trust. He means the weather app, not the map. But the map keeps a low number of its own. It shows you the minutes you saved and says nothing about the heat the body paid to save them.

Not every body has the same margin. For a walker with less heat to spare, the exposed cut the map calls best is not a small inconvenience; it is a risk. And the margins are thinning. Around the world, the share of people in their late sixties and older who live where the hottest days push past 37.5 degrees Celsius is on track to climb from about one in seven today to nearly one in four by mid-century.

None of which means shade wins. It means best was a choice before you ever asked.

So the next time your map draws the quick blue line and calls it best, ask the one thing it will not: best for whom, and measured on what. The clock does not walk home. You do.
