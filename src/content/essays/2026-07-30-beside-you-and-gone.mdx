**A parent can be gone without leaving the room.**
In a lab, fifty-two mothers placed their babies, five and six months old, on a mat and sat beside them. For eight minutes they played as usual. For eight minutes they answered texts on a study phone, told to reply as if a close friend were asking for a recommendation. They stayed beside their babies, close enough to touch, and many kept touching, talking, holding on. Nothing you would call leaving happened. The babies' positive affect still fell to about a third of what it had been.

**One answer in five stopped coming, and she never left.**
In free play, the mothers answered 95.6 percent of the sounds their babies made. While texting, 74.6 percent. A reply that had taken nine-tenths of a second now took nearly two, and sometimes almost six. About one bid in five that she would have answered, she now let pass. That is the whole event: a mother who stayed, and let one reach in five fall through her.

**Minutes come back. The lesson does not.**
The usual worry about parents and phones is lost time, as if attention were a bank you could top back up at bedtime. But a child does not bill you for the minutes. What a child cannot get back is what those minutes teach: that a face can be here and gone at once, that the person you reach for can be within arm's length and not reach back, that love is a thing which can withdraw without standing up. The harm is the model of love quietly installed while everything looks fine.

**This is the ordinary weather now.**
Forty-six percent of American teenagers say a parent is at least sometimes distracted by a phone when they are trying to talk. Sixty-eight percent of parents say the same about themselves. This is the background condition of childhood now, not the failing of a few bad parents.

**The old still face went blank. This one stays busy.**
Decades ago, researchers had mothers hold a still, unresponsive face at their babies after a stretch of play. The babies met it with intense wariness and eventual withdrawal. A texting mother is doing something stranger. She is still there. She touches, she talks, she holds on. What slips is quieter than a blank face: she answers fewer of the sounds, a beat later than before, and coders watching the tape rate her as less finely tuned to the baby while she does it. Timing is only the cleanest of these to measure. And the baby, who cannot read a screen, reads the timing exactly.

**Parents have always looked away. The phone looks away differently.**
To a pot, a letter, a window, another child. Distraction is as old as parenting, and the objection writes itself. But watch a playground. In one study of caregivers at seven of them, a child's bid to an adult with no phone in hand went unanswered about eleven percent of the time, even while the adult was busy with another kid or lost in thought. When the adult was on a phone, the child got nothing back fifty-six percent of the time. The distraction is old. What the phone changes is how often a child gets nothing back.

**Attachment is not made of glass, and one miss is not a wound.**
Here is the strongest case against everything here, and much of it is right. Bonds are tougher than a bad afternoon. Follow a group of children for twenty years and about seventy percent hold the same secure or insecure pattern into adulthood; the patterns that shifted were roughly twice as common among people who had lived through hard events, loss, divorce, serious illness, than among those who had not. A single miss does no damage. The healthiest bonds are the ones where the gap gets repaired. A parent who drifts and comes back teaches something a child needs: the thread holds.

**The child's own phone is the louder machine.**
If you are hunting for what harms a teenager, the parent's glance is a strange place to start. Ninety-five percent of teenagers have access to a smartphone, and the measured link between their own screen time and their own unhappiness is real and tiny, a fraction of a percent of the variance. The device in the child's hand runs hotter than the one in the parent's. All of this is true.

**Frequency was the whole complaint.**
Then notice what none of that touches. Put two adults in a ten-minute conversation and break in on one of them three times with a phone, and they feel more excluded, and trust the other less, than if you break in once, though each interruption lasts about eleven seconds. Repair is the whole defense, and repair depends on the parent fully coming back. A phone competes for her in a way a pot or a window does not: reaching for it runs on habit more than choice, and the pull does not stop the moment she sets it down.

<PullQuote>
Looking up is not the same as coming back.
</PullQuote>

When you switch tasks, part of your attention stays snagged on the one you left. So looking up and being back are two different events, and the second runs late. A baby cannot know what is in your inbox. It can feel the answer that arrives a beat after it used to, or does not arrive at all. It is reading the timing, and the timing does not lie.

**A child keeps score without meaning to.**
It is keeping track of a pattern: I reach, and sometimes the reach lands and sometimes it drops through a person sitting right here. Repeat that often enough and a nervous system draws a conclusion it will carry into rooms this parent will never enter: being close is not the same as being answered. Ask six hundred teenagers about a caregiver pulled into a device, and the more of it they report, the more anxious and avoidant their attachment reads. That is one study, at one moment, in one country, and it cannot tell you which way the arrow runs. But the shape is there.

**The arrow may not point the way I have drawn it.**
Follow children across years and it is sometimes the already-anxious child who later reports a parent as more absorbed, not the absorbed parent who made the child anxious. Maybe a worried child watches harder. Maybe both run at once, a loop with no clean start. The honest version of this is smaller than the frightening one: the lab shows a real effect in the moment, and most of what comes after is a pattern we can measure and cannot yet prove.

**The data will not hand me a villain.**
In that same infant lab, the babies whose mothers used the phone longest were, if anything, the least upset, probably because those mothers cut it short the moment the baby needed them. The distress did not climb with the minutes; it seemed to move instead with the switching, the leaving and returning and leaving. And the object is no villain. It is a paycheck, a map, a school message, the only line to a doctor, sometimes the one thing between a worn-out parent and a worse moment. The trouble was never the phone, and never even the looking. It was the looking away that does not fully come back, again and again, in the exact place a child is learning what closeness is worth.

**Children have no word for it, so they ask the question underneath it.**
A mother told researchers what her young daughter had once asked her. Not why are you busy. Not will you play. The girl asked whether her mother loved the phone more than her. That is what intermittent attention sounds like from the inside, before you have the words to soften it.

**What a child measures is the return.**
A child is learning, from the shape of a thousand small departures, what it means to be loved by someone who is right there. The baby on the mat could not ask the older girl's question yet. It was already working out the answer, one late reply at a time. It reaches. It waits to see what comes back.
