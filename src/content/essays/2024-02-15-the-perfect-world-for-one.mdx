The verdict on the Vision Pro is already written, and it reads like a receipt. $3,499 to start, before the prescription lenses, the travel case, and the care plan carry it past four thousand. 600 grams on your face, another 350 in a battery tethered to your pocket. More than 600 apps at launch, and none built by Netflix, Spotify, or YouTube. Headaches after ten minutes. Eye strain. This week some first buyers are packing it back into the box, because the fourteen-day return window closes on Friday, and the reasons they give all come off that receipt: too heavy, too dear, too little to do.

Every reason is true. Every reason also describes a first version. This is the case the machine's defenders make, and it is a fair one: the hardware gets lighter, the price comes down, the catalog fills in. One analyst already pencils in a cheaper model and millions of buyers a few years out. None of that is promised, but it is the ordinary path, and this company has walked it before. Argue only about the receipt and you may be arguing about the part that changes.

So set the receipt down. The deepest flaw in this machine is not that it costs too much, weighs too much, or does too little. It is that the better it gets at building a perfect world for one person, the worse it gets as a technology for anyone else in the room. Each improvement makes it more solitary, not more social. The wonder and the loneliness are one feature, seen from two sides.

The tell is in the reviews, if you read them for it. The people who admired this device most are the ones who sound loneliest. One reviewer called it the best wearable display he had ever put on, then wrote that he could sit in his own world, inches from his own family. Another put "it's pretty lonely in there" in his own list of faults. He filled a room with enormous windows and found his private gallery had exactly one patron: himself. He replayed a spatial video of his young daughter and called it bittersweet, because he was alone in the headset and could not hand the moment to anyone. These are not complaints from skeptics. They are the sound of people getting what was promised.

Notice which reason for returning the thing does not appear in the reports gathered so far. Buyers name the weight, the headaches, the price, the missing use case. In those reports, nobody sends it back for what it did to the room. The honest reading is that the returns are about the body and the wallet, and the loneliness is quieter, the thing the careful reviewers felt and the ordinary buyer never got far enough to name. The reasons people can put into words are the fixable ones. The one that is hard to say out loud may be harder to fix, and a lighter, cheaper second version might leave it exactly where it is. Or it might not, if that version learns to let two people share a room. The receipt does not tell you which.

Here is what it looks like when the machine works. A father sits on his couch with the headset on and AirPods in, and the living room becomes a movie theater sized to the wall. His son walks over, taps him on the shoulder, and asks if he is asleep. He is not. His wife says she does not like it, that he is "so removed from everything." His son already has a name for the device: "a phone for my face." Nothing in the room has broken. The passthrough cameras, the best a headset has shipped, are showing him the living room in full color, good enough to read a watch. He can see his family. His son can put a hand on his shoulder. What neither the son nor the wife can do is tell whether he is with them.

That gap is not a defect in the passthrough. It is the product. The theater is built for one. The giant Mac screen floating in the air is for one. The windows scattered around the room, the environment that draws in like a curtain, the video of the daughter: all for one, none of it shareable, because two people in two headsets in the same room cannot see the same thing hanging in the air. The company understood this before it shipped. That is why the front of the device carries a screen that projects a picture of the wearer's eyes, and why a video call shows your face as a scanned avatar. Both are confessions.

And both are the least finished parts of the whole machine. The outward eyes, the reviewers agreed, might as well not be there: dim, low resolution, ghostly behind reflective glass. Real eye contact through them, one wrote flatly, is a fantasy. The avatar fares worse. Shown the wearer's digital face on a call, people recoiled. "You look awful," a sister said. "Like Botox from hell," said a friend. "Frightening," said a father; one man's wife told him never to call her that way again. The parts built to give the wearer back to the room are the ones the room rejects on sight. The passthrough is the best a headset has managed, and it is still only cameras: blur when you turn your head, a phone screen it cannot quite read after dark. The company came near to letting the wearer see out. It came nowhere near letting the room see in.

Walk it outside and the same gap opens into spectacle. A filmmaker wore one through New York this month, onto an uptown subway, into the middle of Times Square, a place already built entirely of screens. He had a keyboard hanging in the air, a show playing, messages open, and he said the thing that gives the whole device away: "It's impossible for me to imagine that you can't see what I can see. Everything seems so real." To him, a full world. To the crowd, a man in a glass mask with a cable, pinching at the air. Later he called it the future interface for all computing, and also said no one should wear one in public.

This is an old pattern wearing new hardware. An earlier face computer, a decade ago, got itself banned from a Seattle bar because the customers came, in the owner's words, "to be not known," and could not tell whether the glasses across the table were filming them. The headset after that was so absorbing that an office had to convert a storage closet into a private booth to use it. The one after that let each person in a room see everyone else's little floating avatar, but never their own. Each generation fixed one problem of the body and uncovered a fresh one in the room. This device brings the best passthrough anyone has made and the first serious outward attempt to hand the face back, and the room still cannot read the person wearing it.

Now the strongest case against all of this, stated plainly, because it might be right. Every device that ends up in every pocket looked ridiculous first. A decade ago only about one person in eight thought it was fine to take out a phone at family dinner, and yet nearly nine in ten pulled one out at their last gathering anyway. The thing was judged rude and used constantly, and the rudeness wore into a rule: fine on the street, fine on a train, still frowned on at dinner. Wireless earbuds got called "goofy plastic earrings" the year they launched, and the look faded fast. Norms are slow, then sudden. Make the headset lighter, drop the price, fix the avatar, let two people in one room finally share a window, and the lonely living room is just what a first draft looks like. That argument is honest, and right now it is very much alive.

But first, be exact about the quarrel. A face computer may still reach someone a country away. The trouble is the shared room, where the wearer keeps the whole space and the person beside him is left a mask. Now look at what the analogy quietly asks for. Both those devices left the face alone. The phone took your attention and your hands; you could still meet the eyes of the person who resented it. The earbuds took your ears; your eyes stayed your own. The new etiquette these people are counting on does not have to cover the hands, or the ears. It has to cover the eyes, the part we use to check whether another person is still with us. And the two features built to give the eyes back are the two the careful reviewers trust least. Maybe that is a problem for next year's model. Maybe it is the nature of putting a private computer on a human face. The reviews cannot settle it. They prove only that thoughtful people found the thing strange to sit beside. They do not prove why.

So go back to the living room. The son has a hand on his father's shoulder. Is he asleep? No. He is wide awake, deep inside a world built to fit one person, looking at the room around him in full color. From two feet away there is no way to tell the difference, and that is not the machine failing. That is the machine working. The boy's question is the one the reviews never ran, and the device, for all it can do, has no answer for it. He is still standing there, hand on his father's shoulder, waiting to find out.
