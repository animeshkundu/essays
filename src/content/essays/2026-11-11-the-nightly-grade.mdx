**1. As sleep tracking moved from niche to default, the nightly score became less a health tool than a new way to fail at resting.**

Measuring rest turned rest into work, and the body is now asked to perform for a dashboard. Not every tracker harms everyone. For some people, the measuring is what broke the sleep, and the score has quietly turned rest into a performance with a grade.

**2. Rest used to be the one thing you could not fail at.**

A bad night was a bad night. It passed. There was no number to miss, no streak to break, no morning verdict. The score added a finish line to a thing that had none, and a finish line turns a state into a performance.

**3. The anomaly is small and strange.**

A person can spend more time in bed, move less, and end up sleeping less, because of a number. It is not a failure of will. It is a failure of the metric.

**4. The harm does not need the device to be wrong.**

Wearables are fairly good at telling sleep from wake, and weaker at naming the stages, deep and REM. But a perfectly accurate sleep-efficiency percentage would still be chased by the person lying still in the dark, and chasing it would still keep them in bed, awake, watching the number.

**5. The tool's own definition rewards the wrong move.**

Sleep efficiency is time asleep divided by time in bed, expressed as a percentage. To raise it, a person stays in bed longer and moves less. Both are the opposite of what insomnia treatment prescribes. The metric itself asks for the behavior that breaks sleep; the user is following its instructions.

**6. Orthosomnia is a case series, not a population.**

In 2017 a clinical psychologist and sleep researcher coined "orthosomnia" for an obsession with perfect sleep mediated by a tracker, in a case series of three patients. The paper described patients who arrived at the clinic more worried about what the device said than about how they felt. Whether the same worry extends beyond the clinic is not established; the published evidence is three patients.

**7. The score became the job.**

There is an old rule: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Sleep efficiency is the measure. Rest is the thing. Once the score is the target, the person optimizes the score, and the score and the rest part company. This is that rule, applied to a bed.

**8. The mechanism is insomnia treatment run backward.**

Insomnia treatment restricts time in bed to match time asleep, so the bed relearns its job. The score rewards the opposite: more time in bed, less movement, a better percentage. The bed becomes a workplace for a number.

**9. The scene that carries this.**

A woman in that 2017 case series spent excessive time in bed to push her sleep-efficiency percentage up. Patients in her position lie still, because movement registers as wakefulness and drags the percentage down. The bed was dark, the glow of the phone on the nightstand, the number waiting for morning, and the faint awareness of a device worn to bed. She traded the sleep she was having for the sleep the device would record, and her insomnia deepened.

**10. The score became a verdict on the day.**

About one in five U.S. adults wears a smart watch or fitness tracker. Only some of those devices issue a single nightly score; Apple Watch, for instance, reports duration and stages without a composite number. Across the reported accounts, the morning ritual recurs: reach for the phone before sitting up, the app loading, the number appearing, the small pause while the day's verdict lands, all before reading the body. A poor score becomes a verdict on the day. A good score becomes permission to push harder. Plans get canceled, workouts skipped, mood set, all before the feet touch the floor. The number is not describing the night. It is deciding the day.

**11. The single eight-hour night is itself a modern invention.**

Before industrialization the night in Europe was commonly split in two, "first sleep" and "second sleep," with a waking hour between that was not treated as insomnia. The consolidated block of sleep arrived with artificial light and fixed work hours. So the "perfect night" being chased is a cultural target, not a biological constant. The score is grading a performance of a modern ideal.

**12. The strongest case for the other side.**

The strongest case for the other side is not that trackers are fine. It is that the alternative to an imperfect tracker is usually no measurement at all, and for the most common real problem, chronic short sleep and undiagnosed apnea, the measuring is the intervention, not the destruction of rest. Trackers can make chronic short sleep visible to people who believed they slept fine. They can give feedback that nudges an earlier bedtime or a skipped late drink. For sleep apnea, a disorder commonly cited as roughly eighty percent undiagnosed in its moderate-to-severe cases, though its source is not verified here, a watch that flags breathing disturbances over thirty days can send a person to a clinic who would never have gone. In a clinician's hands, objective sleep data supports treatment for insomnia. And sleep-state misperception cuts both ways: some people feel they slept terribly when objective sleep was normal, and a number that says you slept fine can be a relief. The same device that distresses one user reassures another. On this view, the tool is fine; the harm came from how one person related to a number.

**13. The score and the sleep can coexist.**

A proxy can be gamed and still be useful; step counts are gamed, yet awareness of them has been associated with more walking. A single night's score is noise, but a months-long trend in duration and timing is hard to fake and genuinely informative. The right response to a gamed metric is a better metric, not no metric.

**14. The honest limits.**

There is no population figure for how often tracking worsens sleep. The evidence of harm is case reports and clinical observation, not a survey. The evidence of benefit is mostly self-reported and correlational, with few trials showing that consumer trackers improve objective sleep on their own. Device stage estimates remain modest, so stage-level obsession is built on the noisiest number. The "rare" claim is unmeasured. Exposure grows with adoption; whether harm grows with it is unmeasured. On harm, there is no number to be had.

**15. Back to the dark bedroom.**

The woman is still in bed. The number is still on the nightstand. She did everything the score asked and slept less for it. The question is not whether the tracker is accurate. It is whether the number was ever rest, or whether rest was the thing the number replaced. You will check your own score tomorrow morning. Notice which one you are answering to.
