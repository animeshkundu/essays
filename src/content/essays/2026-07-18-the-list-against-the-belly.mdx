A man had been dead about sixteen hundred years, and on his belly lay a list of soldiers.

He was found this way at Al Bahnasa, a town on a branch of the Nile roughly a hundred and twenty miles south of Cairo, in a limestone chamber cut into the ground. He was of the Roman age. Looters had been through the complex long before the archaeologists, and much of what they left was broken. This body kept one small thing. Resting on its abdomen, laid there by a human hand as part of the embalming, was a scrap of Greek papyrus.

When a papyrus turns up in that exact place, resting on the body, put there on purpose as part of the embalming, the people who work this site have learned what to expect. It should be a spell. They had found Greek papyri in that role before, bundled and sealed into the wrappings, and the content had run to magic and ritual. This one did not. Read under careful hands that winter, it turned out to be a passage from the Iliad: the Catalogue of Ships, the long roll of the Greek army sailing for Troy, town by town, captain by captain, so many ships and so many men. A dead stranger had gone into the ground with a war list laid against the body.

The philologist who identified it put the oddness plainly. Greek papyri had been worked into embalming here before, he said, "but until now, their content was mainly magical." A spell in that place made sense. A poem did not. So the question sits there, waiting: why this, and why here?

There is a warm answer, and most of us reach for it first. Someone loved the poem. In a Greek-speaking town where children learned their letters by copying its lines, a family honored its dead with the greatest thing the culture had made, and laid the Iliad against him like a blessing. It is a good story. The evidence keeps declining to confirm it. And the poem was not rare here: more than eight hundred pieces of the Iliad have come up from this ground over two centuries of digging. That number describes the record we hold now, not the price of any one sheet in an embalming room. Still, of the whole poem, this is the least exalted part: the shipping manifest, the stretch that reads most like a school drill, a register of places and leaders and numbers. And nobody knows whether the dead man could read Greek, or whether the embalmer could, or whether a single soul in that room knew what the marks said.

Set the warm answer down and pick up a colder one. Things do not mostly last because people treasure them. They last because someone, later, stopped treating them as untouchable and gave them a job. Whether that is what happened in this tomb, no one can prove. The motive for the papyrus is genuinely unknown, and ritual, status, habit, an embalmer's mark, and plain reuse all remain open. But the find lets you see the harder possibility clearly, the one reverence would rather skip. Maybe the placement did not need the text to be the Iliad. Maybe it only needed a written object with a use, laid where a body needed something laid.

The town has been teaching this lesson for a century. Its old name was Oxyrhynchus, and almost everything we know of it we know from its garbage. The great libraries of the ancient world, the one at Alexandria, the twenty-eight public libraries of imperial Rome, kept their treasures on purpose, and not one book came down from any of them. What came down came from the rubbish mounds of a dry provincial town, heaped thirty feet deep and drifted over with sand, where it almost never rains and the Nile flood never reached. Out of that trash came lyric poems, comedies, whole plays no library had bothered to copy forward, along with tax returns and private letters and shopping lists, maybe fifty thousand scraps of ordinary paper. The men who dug it in the years around 1900 pulled it up by the basketful. One roll rode back to Oxford in an empty biscuit tin.

The same mounds kept the small records of the town's days. A note that a slave boy of eight, leaning from a window to watch street musicians, had fallen and been killed. An order for a thousand roses and four thousand narcissi for a wedding. The most intimate things we have of these people reached us as refuse, kept by no one on purpose. What we call our inheritance from the classical world is, in large part, what one town threw out and the desert forgot to destroy.

The same thing keeps happening wherever old writing survives, and reverence is seldom the hand behind it. Egyptian workshops built mummy casings out of old paper, cutting sheets to shape, layering and plastering them, molding them wet against the body; the method preserves what it buries. A roll of Hellenistic poetry surfaced exactly this way, as part of a mummy's wrapping, and it greatly enlarged the surviving poems of one Greek poet. It reached a museum by an ugly road, dug up by tomb robbers and sold on the grey market, so where it lay and against whom we cannot say. The poems came back to us as packing. The cleanest case is a Christian prayer book finished in the thirteenth century. To make it, scribes took apart older volumes, scraped the ink off the skins, split the leaves down the middle, turned them, folded them small, and wrote their prayers across the erased pages. One of the books they scraped was Greek mathematics. It holds proofs that survive in no other copy in any language, saved only because the parchment was worth more to someone than the theorems. Open the book today and you see prayers. The mathematics shows faintly, sideways, running underneath.

Here the reverence story deserves its due, because it is not empty, and the deliberate hand on the body pulls the other way. Sometimes people did save things on purpose, and even then the saving looks strange. In a synagogue in old Cairo, a room was kept where no worn-out writing that might carry the name of God could be thrown away, only stored; a thousand years of refusing to destroy things preserved not only prayer books but marriage contracts, business notes, and children's spelling drills, nearly two hundred thousand fragments in the end. And text laid on a body could carry real weight. In other graves around the Mediterranean, thin gold leaves the size of a thumbnail were set on the chests of the dead, telling them what to say on the road through the underworld; one such pair lay on a woman with her lips closed by a coin. Put that beside the deliberate placement here and you have an honest case that the papyrus meant something. The people who found it are themselves split. One says plainly it was there to protect the dead. The philologist who read it will not go that far; he calls a poem doing a spell's work "much stranger," and says he cannot yet explain why the Iliad is there at all.

Notice, though, what even reverence does when it preserves. The Cairo room saved by taking things out of use and letting them lie. Whether by thrift or by holiness, the object stops being what it was and becomes a thing that is kept. And notice what our own wish is doing. We want the poem on the body to be honor, because honor flatters us: it says the great things last because they earned it. The record says otherwise. One Athenian wrote about ninety plays, and seven survive whole. The rest are gone. Chance decided which would last, not merit: a play survived if someone happened to bury or scrape or recopy it at the hour it would have mattered. The only library to reach us from the ancient Greco-Roman world reached us because a volcano buried it, carbonized: hundreds of scrolls like black lumps, most of them still unopened and unread. The first whole word machines have lifted from inside one, after imaging it in slices, was the Greek for purple.

Go back, then, to the winter room, where three people bent over the torn thing and read enough broken Greek to know the army was sailing again. "We've done all we can without destroying the papyrus," the philologist said, and stopped there. Of everything the sheet could have carried, it carried a roll call, a passage whose whole errand in the poem is to keep names from going dark. The Catalogue as a whole opens with the poet asking the Muses for the captains, because he cannot hold them all on hearsay and will not lose them. Which lines of it lay on the body, we do not know. We do not know whether a hand chose this text or simply took the nearest sheet. We would like to believe we keep what we love. The tomb will not confirm it either way. It only sets the question on the abdomen and hands it back to you: of everything you would want remembered, how much will last because someone treasured it, and how much only if, long after you are past caring, it turns out to be good for something?
