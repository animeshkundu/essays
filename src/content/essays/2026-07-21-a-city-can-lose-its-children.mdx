A school can be dying on a street that has never been busier. Hold that against your intuitions about what a dying school looks like, because it breaks most of them. You picture boarded shops, a bus that stopped running, weeds in the yard. You do not picture a queue for artisanal gelato running the length of the pavement across the road.

The last primary school in Soho stands on Great Windmill Street, wedged between a strip club, a West End theatre, and a pub. It is the last of what were once sixteen schools in that patch of central London. Before Covid it had close to 180 children and was oversubscribed. This September it expects close to 65. On the afternoon a reporter came by, parents waited in fierce heat for pickup while the gelato queue built across the street. One of them looked at the queue and said: without this school it's all just tourists, isn't it.

It sounds like a complaint about tourists. It is really a claim about what a school is for.

Start with the sober view, because it is the strong one and it is not cruel. A building made for 170 children is heading toward 65, and its costs do not shrink child by child. It runs catering, energy, site costs, and a safeguarding duty whether it teaches 65 children or 165, because those costs follow the building, not the register. Money, meanwhile, follows the children: schools here are funded largely per pupil, and staff pay alone is usually more than seventy percent of the budget. So when the roll falls, the income falls with it, and it falls faster than the costs can. The predictable result at Soho is a widening deficit. A shortfall of about a million pounds had accrued keeping the doors open, on top of roughly a million more raised from outside. The responsible thing, on this reading, is obvious. Close the small school, move the children to a fuller one, and stop paying to light empty corridors. Fewer buildings, more children per building, healthier books. This is not a spreadsheet being heartless. It is a spreadsheet being right about what it can see.

The question is what it cannot see. And to get there you have to answer the puzzle first: why is this school emptying while its street fills up?

Rule out the boring answers first.

It is not that the school is bad. It was rated good, and before Covid it was oversubscribed, turning families away. A former parent remembers walking past when her son was nearly five, assuming from the outside that it was a drop-in play place, then learning it was a real school and thinking herself lucky he could get in. Parents there use the museums and theatres around the corner as extended classrooms. Whatever is killing it, quality is not.

It is not that the place was abandoned. Parents are campaigning to revive it, the local MP calls it a crucial part of the community, and a planned merger with another church school a mile away was called off only last month. People are fighting for this school, not walking past its decay.

It is not simply that the country stopped having babies, though that current runs underneath everything. In 2023, England and Wales recorded the fewest live births since 1977, and official figures put 2025 as the second fewest in that stretch. The national projection now shows state nursery and primary rolls dropping by 473,000 children by 2031, which one report put as the equivalent of more than fifteen hundred primary schools standing empty. That is real. But births alone cannot explain a place like this, because the children were born; they were taken elsewhere. Look at the wider city and the movement is stark: in one recent reception cohort, about 9,900 children left London for the rest of England while only 1,800 arrived, more than five leaving for every one who came. The babies existed. The neighborhoods stopped holding them.

Which leaves the explanation that actually fits. Resident children are only one of the populations a street can carry, and they are the easiest one to lose. A street can swap families for tourists, offices, single adults, restaurant shifts, and commuters, and look fuller and richer every year while the number of children who sleep nearby drains toward zero. Soho's school lived partly on that churn: its open catchment drew families who worked in the area rather than lived in it, restaurant staff commuting in from the outer boroughs with their kids. When the restaurants and offices shut during Covid, those parents stopped bringing the children, and many never came back. The crowd stayed. The crowd was never the point. Foot traffic and childhood are different systems that happen to share a pavement, and only one of them pays the school's bills.

So the school breaks before the street looks empty. That is the trap, and it is not local to Soho. It is the same mechanism in every system now hitting the same wall from a different angle. South Korea, with the lowest birth rate in the world, has already closed more than four thousand schools for good. In China the official count of kindergartens and ordinary primary schools fell by close to thirty thousand in a single year, 2024 to 2025. Hong Kong shows the timing plainly: its youngest cohorts hollow out first, pre-primary enrollment down by nearly a third over five years, while across those same years the number of primary schools barely moves, from 589 to 591. The children vanish years before the institution does. California is losing students too, hundreds of thousands over the coming decade, and yet fourteen of its counties are projected to grow, which tells you this is not fate falling evenly; it is geography sorting families toward the places that still have room for them.

And then there are the places where you can watch it happen inside a single room. On a Greek island, a reporter found a four-year-old alone in her kindergarten one morning. The class has two children. The other was sick that day. One jacket hung on the rack. The teacher tries, when the weather is kind, to walk her two small pupils out during the older children's break so they can be around other children at all. On a nearby island the primary school is down to two pupils, and a mother sends her son by boat to a different island's kindergarten so that he will not be the only child in the room; many days she rides the ferry across with him. When winter makes the crossing too rough, the older ones simply miss school. A shepherd there, asked why it matters, gives the sequence without drama: the school closes, the young families leave, only the old people stay, and then the village is gone.

The strongest case for closing these schools is not cruelty or spreadsheet worship. A school can shrink past the point where it can do its job. In a rural district in Vermont, a school of about sixty children is projected to fall to forty within a few years. Its principal had opposed closing it; then she joined the committee that was planning the closure and changed her mind. Her reason was not a budget line. It was the children. A bigger school could give them a full-time music teacher and a full-time librarian; hers could afford an instrumental music teacher one day a week. Nor is "small class" a magic phrase. The best evidence we have rates cutting class sizes as low impact for very high cost, with real gains only when classes fall far enough that teachers actually teach differently, and an accidental class of eight that formed because the town ran out of children is not a designed small-group model. It is just a thin room. Closure can genuinely help a child: when struggling schools in Chicago shut and the pupils moved into strong ones, they gained ground, nearly a month of extra progress in reading and more than two in math. In a South Dakota district that merged after eighty percent of voters backed it, an elementary principal remembered classes of six and eight and how hard it was to teach groups that small; the combined district gave its children a wider curriculum and a real team to play on. And districts elsewhere in Vermont that merged reported saving, adding, or restarting things a tiny school cannot hold: literacy support, world languages, after-school programs. Keeping every under-filled building open is not a soft-hearted free lunch. It spends scarce money on empty space instead of on children. If you have to balance the books, it is close to unanswerable.

Except the ledger's own best evidence carries the crack that undoes it. Look again at Chicago. The children gained only when they landed in a better school. The ones sent to a worse one lost ground, more than a month of reading. Closure, it turned out, was never the treatment. The receiving school was. The number that vindicates consolidation is real, and it is entirely conditional on something consolidation does not control: what waits on the other side, how long the new journey is, whether the friends move together or scatter, whether anyone at the new place will learn the child's name. Scholars who study this for a living keep landing on the same flat answer to every version of the question. It depends. The savings depend too. Vermont's mergers produced some, then watched them get eaten by higher salaries and longer bus routes.

Here is what the ledger measures: lessons delivered per pound. Here is what it cannot measure. It cannot see the children in that Vermont town who make and serve the soup at the free community lunch on Wednesdays, or the fifth grader who said that closing the school would take away part of the town too. It cannot see a school small enough that the teachers know every child's strengths across the whole building, which a parent who grew up anonymous in a class of thirty counts as the point, not a shortfall. It cannot price the length of the new bus ride, or the ferry a child rides so as not to be the only one in the room, or the reason a young family decides a village is still a place to stay. It cannot see the thing the parent on Great Windmill Street named without meaning to: that the school is the last institution telling a neighborhood it still expects children. A city with no school for its children is not planning for any.

That million-pound deficit is real money. It could teach a lot of children somewhere else. If you handed me the budget, I might close the school too, and I would not be a monster for it. That is exactly what makes this hard, and it is why the real disagreement is not about arithmetic. Both sides can do the sums. The disagreement is about what a school is. Treat it as a delivery unit for lessons and the empty one should obviously go. Treat it as childhood infrastructure, the thing that holds a street together and tells families a place is theirs, and the test changes. Closing it then has to clear a higher bar: not just better books or better lessons, but proof that whatever replaces the school also replaces what it did, the peer group, the short journey, a receiving school at least as good, and the reason a young family stays. Fail that test, and a balanced budget is just a town quietly deciding to stop being a town, one clean line at a time.

So walk back to the street. The gelato queue across the road. The building made for 170, expecting 65. The crowd that will not miss the children, because it replaced them years ago and never noticed the swap. That is the fact the ledger gets right, and the one it cannot hold: a city can lose its children and stay full. The parent left the real question hanging, the one no spreadsheet will take. Not whether the sums work. What is left of a place once the only people it still expects are the ones passing through.
