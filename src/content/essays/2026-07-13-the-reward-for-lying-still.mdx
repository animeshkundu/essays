Most mornings now, a lot of us learn how we slept before we know how we feel. The watch buzzes. You reach for it in the dark, and there is a number waiting. Seventy-two. Fifty-eight. A little colored bar of a night you were not awake for. Before your body has said anything, the number has: good night, bad night, brace yourself.

This is sold as knowing yourself better. Open the app when you wake, one maker tells you, and see how much you slept and how the last two weeks have gone. It sounds like care. More data about your own body, delivered gently, every morning.

It is closer to the opposite. The reason is not that the numbers are wrong, though they often are. It is what a number does to the thing it counts. Rest is one of the few states you can only reach by stopping trying to reach it. A score turns it into something you try at. And once you are trying, you have already lost the thing you were after.

Start with the strange part.

A 27-year-old woman came to a sleep clinic sure that her sleep was broken. She brought proof: her tracker said she spent her nights in restless sleep, at a sleep efficiency of 60 percent. She had built her two-week sleep diary by cutting the printouts from her tracker and pasting them in by hand. Her doctors went to work. They found restless legs syndrome, with ferritin at the low end of normal, and treated it; iron and a second drug stopped the restless legs completely. Over the months that followed they kept looking. A home test for sleep apnea came back clean. She deliberately lost twenty pounds. Still she reported the same unrefreshing nights, and the tracker still showed the same low numbers.

So they did the real test. They wired her for a night in the lab, the kind that reads the electrical activity of the brain itself, and watched her sleep. She fell asleep slowly, but she slept: no breathing trouble, a normal count of awakenings, and more deep sleep, not less, than is normal for someone her age. Her doctors gave her the good news.

"Then why does my Fitbit say I am sleeping poorly?"
</PullQuote>

A smart, motivated person is handed the gold-standard reading of her own brain, and she trusts the wristband instead. This is the anomaly. Any honest account of sleep tracking has to explain why she believed the cheap guess over the expensive truth: over the lab, over the exam, over the treatment that had already quieted the one disorder anyone could find.

The easy explanation is that she was simply anxious, an outlier, a worrier who would have found something to worry about anyway. Maybe. But she was not the only one. The clinicians who described her had seen the pattern in other patients too, enough that they gave it a name in 2017. Others have reported it since: people who bring in tracker data and go on trusting it after a formal sleep test contradicts it. How common this is, no one knows; it has not been counted well enough to say. But it is not the quirk of one anxious patient. It is a shape that keeps recurring, and once a behavior recurs across many people, calling them all neurotic stops explaining anything. It becomes the thing to explain.

The next easy explanation is that the trackers are just inaccurate, and better sensors will fix it. That is half true, and the shape of the error matters more than the size.

Here is what a wristband actually does. It cannot see sleep. A lab reads brain waves and the flicker of the eyes behind the lids. A watch reads your pulse and whether your arm moved, and then it guesses. And the guess is lopsided in a specific, unlucky way. Put these devices next to a real sleep study and they are superb at one thing and terrible at its opposite. In one comparison, a wristband caught real sleep almost every time, about 99 percent, and caught real wakefulness 8 percent of the time. In a larger test of seven popular devices, every one caught sleep at least 93 percent of the time, while their ability to catch you lying there awake ran as low as 18 percent.

That one contrast is the whole story in a pair of numbers. The machine is worst at exactly the state insomnia is made of: lying in the dark, awake and still. Hold your body quiet enough and the device will score your wide-awake stillness as sleep.

Now watch what that does to a person who wants a better score. If stillness reads as sleep, then the way to improve tomorrow's number is to lie in bed longer, and to lie there more quietly. One sleep-medicine professor says she has patients who do exactly this: they lie awake for hours, holding still, trying to earn a better grade from the wristband. Staying in bed longer without sleeping can nudge the number up. It feels like effort in the right direction.

It is the exact wrong direction.

The first-line treatment for chronic insomnia is not a drug. It is a short course of behavior change, called CBT-I, and its two central instructions are almost the reverse of chasing a score. One says: when you cannot sleep, get out of bed. Do not lie there. Keep the bed for sleeping, so your body stops learning to be awake in it. The other says: spend less time in bed, not more, so that sleep gets crowded and deepens. The whole method works by breaking the habit of lying still in the dark, waiting for sleep to arrive.

So the device and the cure give opposite orders. The cure says leave the bed. The score says stay, and stay still. A patient following the wristband is doing the one thing most likely to keep the insomnia alive, and being congratulated for it in the morning.

None of this proves the device caused anyone's insomnia. The doctors who named the pattern were the first to say they could not tell which came first. The incentive, though, points only one way.

And the deepest problem is not even the bad incentive. It is that the watching itself keeps you awake.

This was true before wearables, with a plainer instrument: the clock. Researchers had people try to fall asleep while monitoring a clock. Even good sleepers worried more and took longer to drop off. In people with insomnia, watching the clock was rated more distressing than a neutral display and stretched out how long they lay awake. A mind that keeps checking whether it is asleep yet has given itself a job, and the job is incompatible with the rest.

<Callout>
A sleep score is a clock with a grade attached. It runs all night and hands you the verdict at dawn.
</Callout>

And there is a known loop here, described long before any of these devices: you worry about your sleep, the worry wakes you a little, so you watch your sleep more closely, which makes you misjudge how badly you did, which feeds the worry. The tracker slides into that loop and speeds it up. It gives the anxious sleeper something to do about sleep, in bed, at night, which is the one place nothing should be done.

If you doubt a number can be that powerful, consider what a made-up one can do. In an experiment, people were told a fabricated figure for how well they had slept, a percentage of dream sleep pulled from nowhere. The next day, the invented number, not how rested they actually felt, predicted how they scored on tests of attention and mental arithmetic. Tell someone they slept badly and they will think worse, even when they slept fine. The score does not have to be true to run your day. It only has to be believed.

For most of the history of sleep advice, the counsel was about habits and the room: a steady schedule, less caffeine, a dark and quiet space, all of it meant to make sleep easier to fall into. Then came the promise of self-knowledge through numbers, and a wearable that sold a nightly measure of sleep instead of an easier way into it. Sleep stopped being something you protected and became something you scored. The kiwis and the mouth tape and the rest of the routine that now goes by sleepmaxxing are downstream of that change.

So here is what is left once the other explanations fall away. The trouble was never really the mouth tape or the magnesium or the kiwis before bed, the hacks the trend is named for. Most of that is ordinary sleep advice in a costume: some silly, a little of it risky, none of it the heart of the matter. The score is. Turning rest into a number does three things at once, and all three push the same way. It rewards the posture of insomnia, lying still and awake. It makes watching your sleep feel like tending it. And it hands you a judgment each morning that can sink the day whether or not it is true.

Rest is the reward for stopping. The score is a reason to keep going. You cannot supervise your way into sleep any more than you can grip your way into relaxing a hand. The wristband asks you to try, all night, at the one thing that only works when you quit trying.

The strongest objection is real, and it deserves its due. Sometimes the number helps. Among the same clinic cases, a 69-year-old man began tracking his sleep, saw restless nights on the readout, mentioned it to his neurologist, and got sent for a real study. It found severe sleep apnea, the dangerous kind: 54 breathing interruptions an hour. He got treatment and stuck to it, 8.5 hours a night, and credited the wristband with catching a disorder he never knew he had. A device really can surface a pattern a person would never feel on their own and push them toward care. In careful trials, when people are taught to read the data as a loose clue rather than a verdict, sleep can improve. Used as a prompt for a conversation with a doctor, the estimate earns its place.

But look at how even that man's story bends. The tracker that had pointed him toward a real diagnosis became the thing he could not put down. Session after session, he steered the conversation back to its readouts. "I know you don't like this," he would tell his therapist, "but my Fitbit tells me." The device that once led him toward the truth had become a rival to it, a second opinion he trusted over the room. That is the line. A number helps when it starts a conversation and harms when it ends one. It can be a clue. It cannot be the judge.

<Aside>
Hold the claim where the evidence holds it. Orthosomnia, the name those doctors coined by analogy to orthorexia, an unhealthy preoccupation with healthy eating, is not a formal diagnosis. No one has proven that trackers cause insomnia, and one small trial found that handing insomnia patients a tracker did not, on average, make their sleep worry worse. The honest claim is narrower, and still hard to shake: for some people, the score rewards the exact habit the cure is trying to break, and pours effort into the one thing that punishes effort.
</Aside>

Go back to the woman with the pasted-in diary. Her restless legs were treated. The iron worked, the weight came off. The lab read her brain for a night and found no breathing trouble, a normal count of awakenings, more deep sleep than is normal for her age. It did not find nothing: she was slow to fall asleep, and she still woke unrefreshed. But the one measurement she believed was the one that could not read her brain at all. The doctors offered her the treatment built to loosen exactly this grip. She did not go. Her insurance would not cover it.

We reached for these numbers because the body is a poor witness to its own rest. You cannot watch yourself sleep. That much is true. But the number cannot feel rested either. It can only be checked, and checked again. And there is a kind of tiredness no device can show you: not the kind that comes from a bad night, but the kind that comes from watching all night to make sure it was a good one.
