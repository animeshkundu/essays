Before he reads the code, he has to decide whether the person who wrote it understands it.

That is the new first step. A stranger opens a pull request against Godot, a free game engine that tens of thousands of people build games on. The description is long and clean. The tests are green, or say they are. A few years ago the maintainer would have read the change and asked one thing: does this fix the problem? Now he asks a different set first. Is this description real writing, or the confident wallpaper a language model prints? Did a human understand this code, or paste it? Did they run the tests, or were the results typed out to look run? He does this, by his own account, multiple times a day.

He is one of the project's primary maintainers, and he has given it close to a decade. Godot is MIT licensed, community driven, and runs on volunteer contributions. It is proud in its own documents of being welcoming to new contributors, of letting anyone who uses the engine change the engine. Maintainers spend a lot of their time helping newcomers get a rough pull request into a state where it can be merged. In February 2026 he wrote the sentence that got the software world's attention. AI slop pull requests, he said, had become draining and demoralizing. Then, plainer: "I don't know how long we can keep it up."

When he wrote that, the project had 4,681 open pull requests. By that July the count was 5,197. In February alone, the month of his post, 678 pull requests were opened; five months later 234 of them were still sitting there, each one a decision nobody had found the hours to make. Some of the open requests date to 2019. The work of closing them falls, in large part, on volunteers.

Everyone was told AI would make contributing easier. Lower the barrier. Help a beginner read an unfamiliar codebase, draft a patch, fix a first bug. On the contributor's side it delivered: opening a pull request has never been easier. The ease stopped at the submit button. On the other side of it, maintainers report the opposite reflex: second-guessing strangers, turning newcomers into suspects, and in some cases reaching for the switch that shuts the door. GitHub shipped a setting in February 2026 that lets a project disable pull requests outright, or accept them only from people already inside. The tool built for the moment is a lock.

So the claim here, which a lot of good engineers will hate: AI did not make open source more open. It broke the quiet bargain that made openness affordable in the first place, and the kindest thing left may be to close the door a little on strangers.

To see why, you have to rule out the boring explanations, because they are the ones everyone reaches for.

The first boring explanation is that the code is bad. AI writes buggy code, the story goes, so of course review hurts. But bad code is the easy case. It fails to compile, trips a test, and gets closed in a minute. The maintainer was precise about the range he sees: at one end, garbage that makes no sense and does not compile; at the other, and this is the end that costs, pull requests that might actually work but "may not be the right fix or well understood by their author." Broken code is cheap to reject. Plausible code you cannot trust is what eats the day. And low-effort junk is not new. In 2020, long before coding agents, a T-shirt promotion buried maintainers in spam pull requests; by two in the afternoon on the first day, at least four percent of entries were already flagged invalid. Bad contributions predate AI. This is not that.

The second explanation is volume: too many requests, not enough reviewers, so automate the triage and move on. Volume is real. But automation only reaches the part of review a machine can do, and that part was never the hard part. Continuous integration can build a change across Windows, Android, Linux, the web, macOS, and iOS, and run the style and format checks besides. It cannot tell you whether the person who opened the request understood what they changed, whether the problem is real, whether the approach is right, or whether the testing they described ever happened. curl is the clean proof. Its lead maintainer noted that pull requests had not yet broken curl, because the project runs around two hundred automated jobs and can defer human eyes until the checks go green. The machinery held. The pain arrived somewhere the machine could not stand guard: security reports, plain text, where a human has to read every claim and decide what it means. He once spent nineteen minutes reading the same function three times, hunting a buffer overflow a well-worded report insisted was there. It was not there. He wrote, "There was no buffer overflow." The report was free to make. Disproving it took a person reading the same code three times to be sure the bug was not there.

The third answer is money, and it is the one the maintainer himself believes: pay maintainers, and the slop becomes survivable. He is not wrong that funding would help. Paid maintainers could work the backlog down, and donated compute could keep a volunteer from paying out of pocket to keep up; that is real relief, and the reviewer who asked for it was asking for something that works. But money buys hours. It does not buy back the thing that actually broke, which is a signal.

The signal is this. For decades, the open bargain rested on a filter almost nobody named, because it was invisible and free. Writing a real pull request was work. You had to care enough to use the software, dig into a codebase that was not yours, understand it well enough to change one thing without breaking ten others, and put your fix up for strangers to judge. The effort was never the price of admission. It was evidence. The old writing about why open source worked said it plainly: a contributor who had gone to all that trouble was, by the very fact of the trouble, likely to have something useful to offer. Effort was a proxy for good faith and for understanding. You did not have to trust the person. You could trust the cost.

AI removed the cost and left the appearance. A plausible pull request that used to take hours now takes seconds, and it arrives wearing every sign of care: the thorough description, the confident rationale, the tests that claim to pass. The trouble that used to vouch for a stranger is gone, and worse, it can be counterfeited. So the reviewer's question changes at the root. It is no longer "is this patch right," which you can often check. It is "does this person understand what they sent me, and did they really do what they say," which you frequently cannot check without doing much of the work yourself. That question is slow, it is human, and it now has to be asked of everyone who is not already known.

You can watch the cost land whole. In May 2026, someone opened a request against an emulator project that touched ninety-two files across security-sensitive corners of the code, ending with an honest footer: generated with an AI tool. A maintainer closed it the same day, not because it was all wrong but because it could not be reviewed at all: too many changes, too many from misreading the code, no way for the author to explain them. "1200 lines of slop and 1 good fix," another wrote. Two days later the author apologized for becoming part of the problem. He had not meant harm. That is the point. Good faith was intact; comprehension was not; and without a cheap way to tell them apart, the whole thing had to go.

Now the strongest version of the other side, because it is strong, and the maintainer feels its pull harder than anyone. Gates hurt the people open source most wants. The majority of contributors are casual, and a first pull request is already frightening; one cold reception and a person never comes back. Nearly a quarter of the open source world reads and writes English less than very well, and the reflexes that flag "AI slop," the too-fluent prose, the boilerplate description, are exactly the marks a nervous non-native speaker leaves when they reach for a tool to sound clearer. The maintainer knows this. He described the trap directly: you ask a suspicious author whether they used AI, and they answer that they only used it to write the description, because their English is poor. The reputation tools turn the bias into math. One popular scorer admits that a genuinely talented developer's first ever contribution will be rated low trust by design, because it measures whether you are already established, not whether you are any good. Require a track record before a stranger gets a real review, and the stranger can never build one. The door that keeps out the slop keeps out the newcomer who is indistinguishable from it. That is not a small cost. It is the cost of the whole promise.

And still the gate may be the kinder thing, once you are honest about what is scarce. The scarce resource was never correctness; tests and time find correctness. The scarce resource is a human being willing to extend good faith to a stranger, read the work as if it were sincere, and spend real attention finding out. That willingness is finite, it is unpaid, and it is what burns away when every request has to be treated as possibly hollow. The maintainer who prides himself on welcoming newcomers is watching the welcome itself get spent down, one suspicious read at a time. Most of the gates on offer are not the lock. They are valves: an issue you must open first, a small task before you may submit, a cap on how many open requests one stranger can carry. They do not ask a stranger to prove they belong. They ask them to prove the work is worth reading before a person spends hours on it, which is only the old effort signal made a rule, moved back onto the one who now gets their code for free. Turning pull requests off, or taking them only from insiders, is the real lock, and it is where a worn-down project ends up when the valves are not enough. All of it is uglier than the old openness. It may also be the only version of openness that leaves a human on the other side still able to say yes.

Detection offers no relief, only irony. When someone suggested running an AI on the project's own servers to catch AI-written submissions, the maintainer called it horribly ironic and said he was not keen on feeding the machinery further. You would burn compute to distrust people faster. And funding, real as its help is, buys more hours of the same corroded work; it does not restore the free signal that used to make the work unnecessary.

The queue is longer now than when he wrote. The requests keep arriving, most from humans leaning on tools, a few of them careful, all of them carrying the same faint question they cannot shed: does the person behind this understand it. He is still there, opening the next one, deciding again whether to spend his good faith on a stranger he cannot afford to trust for free. He said he did not know how long they could keep it up. He did not say they had stopped.
