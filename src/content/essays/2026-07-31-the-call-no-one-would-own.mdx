She was serving at four-all in the first set, one point from the game, on Centre Court at Wimbledon. Her opponent, the local player the crowd had come to see, hit a leaping backhand that landed long. Not close. Several inches past the baseline, out by enough that the partisan crowd, pulling for the other woman, sighed. The server saw it land and looked up toward the chair, because as far as she could tell the point was over and the game was hers.

Then nothing happened. There was no sound.

That summer, in 2025, Wimbledon had replaced its line judges with electronic line calling. The calls came as recorded human voices, a word spoken the instant a ball landed wrong. This one never came. Where the voice should have said "out," there was only the sound of the crowd, waiting for a point that had, as far as the rules were concerned, not yet ended.

The chair umpire said, "Stop, stop, stop." A half beat later a robotic voice echoed him: "Stop! Stop!" He told the crowd he needed to check whether the system was up and running, because there had been no audio call. Then he picked up a phone. He made a call from his chair while both players stood and waited, and while, on the television feed, the replay showed again and again that the ball had landed well out. Several minutes passed. He announced that the electronic system had been unable to track the point, and that the point would be replayed.

Replayed. Not awarded. The ball the television replay showed landing long, that the crowd had reacted to as out, would be struck again, as if it had never been hit. On the do-over the server missed a forehand, and a few points later she was broken. At the change of ends she told the umpire: "You took the game away from me."

She was pointing at a person, and in a way she was right to. No one had made a wrong call against her. The machine did not miscall the ball; no one has said the tracking was wrong. Part of the system had been switched off by mistake, so where the call should have come, nothing did. What happened was stranger than theft. The one call that would have ended the game was there for a person to make, and the person did not make it.

The danger of handing officiating to machines is not that they miss more calls than people do. It is that they take the call that matters most in that moment and move it to where it is hardest to make: the silence right after the trusted machine says nothing.

Take the case for the machine seriously, because it is strong. On raw accuracy it is not a contest. In the lab tests behind the older challenge system, the ball tracking had a mean error of about 3.6 millimeters, and no line judge alive can match that. Back when players could still challenge a call, in the first years of that technology, 38 percent of those challenges overturned the umpire. One former champion, a man whose public image was built on arguing with officials, said that if the system is accurate then it is great, because at least you know you are getting the right call. The case runs past accuracy, too. The machine gives every player the same conditions from one tournament to the next, and it ends the old ritual of arguing with an official who might have been looking the wrong way. And the Wimbledon failure was not the machine judging badly. The tournament said the ball tracking was "working optimally." What went wrong was that a human operator had switched off part of one side of the court by mistake, for a single game. Wimbledon has since taken that ability away from the operators, so the exact error cannot happen again. Every piece of that is true. If the argument were only about who sees the ball more clearly, the machine wins, and the people in the blazers have lost the argument.

But the wound lay somewhere else entirely. Read the rulebook. It says that when the electronic system fails to make a call, "the call shall be made by the chair umpire." The authority did not vanish when the sound did. It sat exactly where it had always sat, in the chair, in one man, who later said he had thought the ball was out. Here is the part that is easy to miss. In that same silent game, the system had already missed two earlier balls, and both times the umpire made the call himself, not knowing the tracking was off. He could make the call. He had just made two of them. What he would not do was make the third, the one that would have ended the game. He had seen it out. He said so to the other player in the moment, and to the server after the match. He would not say it when saying it counted. Her own guess at why: he was, she said, "probably scared to take such a big decision."

That is the thing a better machine does not fix. A more reliable system, a backup, a locked switch: all of it keeps the voice from going silent again. None of it makes a person readier to stand up in front of the crowd on Centre Court and rule, on his own authority, that the ball was out. The system did not remove the judgment. It moved it, from many small calls spread along the lines to a single call in the chair, and then it made that one call the hardest of all. The facts were there in plain sight. The replay showed it. The crowd reacted as if it were out. The umpire himself said afterward that he had seen it out.

This is not an argument for bringing the line judges back. But it helps to look at what Wimbledon gave up, because the shape of the loss is the point. For something like 148 years the calls came from people you could see, as many as nine of them per match, in navy jackets and pinstriped shirts, standing on the lines, each of them a person you could watch make a call. There had been a lot of them: 275 by one count, around three hundred by others. In 2025 they were replaced by eighteen cameras a court. The odd part is that the human voice stayed. The system plays recordings of real staff, so you still hear a person say "out"; you just never see who, or where they are. When the recording works, no one notices. When it goes quiet, attention turns to the chair, and the chair reaches for a phone.

Tennis is not alone. Other sports have hit the same seam from different sides. In a Premier League match in 2023, a goal was wrongly disallowed for offside. The video review had done its job: it picked the right frame, drew the line correctly, and the image showed the player clearly onside. Then the official in the video room said the words "check complete," which confirmed the wrong call instead of overturning it. By the time anyone caught it, play had restarted, and the review team decided the rules no longer let them fix it. The league called it "a significant human error." The picture was perfect. The ownership was not.

The sports that handle this best are the ones that refuse to pretend the machine ends judgment. Cricket built its review system around a category it named out loud: "umpire's call." When the ball tracking is close, the marginal decision is handed back, on purpose, to the human standing on the field. Baseball, testing an automated strike zone, kept the home plate umpire calling every pitch and gave the machine a narrow job in the background; a hitter who disagrees taps his cap or helmet, the stadium screen shows the track, and a person's call either stands or is reversed, fast and in the open. Neither sport hides who owns the exception. That is the whole design, not a flaw in it.

After its silent call, Wimbledon did not bring the line judges back. It kept the electronic calls and built more technology around them. For 2026 it added video review on six courts, though that is a separate system, pointed at chair umpire decisions like double bounces and foul shots rather than at whether a ball was in or out. The change that speaks to the silence is smaller: the scoreboards will now show "out" and "fault" as visual signals, because players and spectators had sometimes not been able to hear the system. That is a fair repair. If the call comes as a sound and the sound can be missed, giving it a visible form is plain sense. But the scoreboard answers a question about hearing. It makes the call easier to catch; it settles nothing about who must speak when the system goes quiet. First you buy a cleaner decision. Then you buy more equipment to make it feel fair.

The woman who lost that game went on to win the match in straight sets, reaching the quarterfinals for the first time in nine years. That is exactly why the moment sticks, and why it is not a simple story about a robbed player. She got her win. The scoreboard came out right. What she never got was the one thing the whole apparatus exists to produce: a moment when the ball was out and a person with the standing to say so said so, out loud, and meant it. Asked how she would have felt if she had lost the match, she laughed and said she would hate Wimbledon and never come back. She was joking. She was also telling the plain truth about what she had wanted: a person in the chair willing to say what he plainly saw.

We keep building systems that are right more often than any person could be. The quieter cost is what that leaves for the person still sitting there when the system goes silent. His authority never went anywhere. The rulebook still says the call is his. But the machine had taken every routine line call off his hands and left him only the exceptions, and the exception that arrived was the largest one on the court: game point, Centre Court, a partisan crowd, nothing automated to stand behind. He had already made two calls himself that game. He would not make the third. The ball will keep landing where it lands, in or out, plain to anyone watching. What is less certain is whether the last person with the authority to say so will still feel it is his to say, now that saying so is the only part of the job the machine has left him.
