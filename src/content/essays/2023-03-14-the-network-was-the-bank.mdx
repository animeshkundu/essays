The order that seized Silicon Valley Bank described it, in the same breath, as having been "in sound financial condition" right up until the run began. On March 9, its depositors and investors tried to pull forty-two billion dollars out of it. By the close of business the bank was about a billion dollars short and could not settle its books with the Federal Reserve. The next day the state regulator took possession and handed it to a federal receiver. Forty-two billion dollars was close to a quarter of everything the bank held on deposit, and it reached for the exit in the space of a single day.

That is fast, even for a panic. A bank run has always run on fear, and fear has always been quick. But the fear used to need time to travel: door to door, branch to branch, headline to next day's headline. This time it did not.

By the end of that week two explanations were on the table, and they were the two everyone reached for. One says the bank was simply broke, and the run only made official what was already true. The other says a herd on the internet spooked a healthy business to death. Each explains half of what happened, and neither explains the timing. The whole question sits in the gap between them.

Start with the broke story, because it is the strong one. The bank had a real hole, and it dug it itself. Over the boom years it had taken a flood of deposits and parked them in long-dated bonds while interest rates were near zero. When rates rose, those bonds lost value. By the end of last year the unrealized loss on the bonds it meant to hold to maturity was about fifteen billion dollars, sitting against roughly sixteen billion of equity. Nearly the entire cushion, gone on paper. It had barely hedged the risk. A depositor with millions of uninsured dollars in that bank had good reason to leave, and no group chat was required to work that out.

Here is the problem with the broke story. The figures were public. They sat in the annual report filed on February 24, in plain print, for anyone who cared to read them. But a number in a filing is not the same as a shared alarm. For two weeks, nothing happened. The bank opened every morning. The weakness was there in plain view, supposedly fatal, and the doors stayed open anyway. If the disclosed balance-sheet weakness alone were enough to kill a bank, this one dies in February. It didn't. So the balance sheet is not the mechanism. It is the precondition.

What changed on March 8 was not the balance sheet. It was that the bank moved, in public, to shore itself up. It sold about twenty-one billion dollars of bonds, booked a loss near one point eight billion, and asked the market for two and a quarter billion in fresh capital. Read coldly, that is a company trying to fix its problem before it becomes one. Read at speed, by people with uninsured millions in the building, it was an alarm.

So the second story: social media did it. That is closer, and still too glib, because it names the medium and misses the structure. The thing that mattered was not that strangers talked online. It was who the depositors were to each other.

A run that breaks a bank in a day needs four things at once, and this bank had all four turned up as high as they go.

First, a reason to leave. Federal insurance covers a quarter of a million dollars per depositor. At the end of last year, its estimated uninsured deposits in U.S. offices, the balances sitting above that line, came to about 151.5 billion dollars, set against roughly 173 billion in total deposits: close to seven of every eight dollars. This was operating cash for companies, not the savings of households. For an uninsured depositor, sitting still in a shaky bank is not loyalty. It is a bet with the payroll.

Second, a signal everyone comes to know. The March 8 disclosure was that signal. It did not reach every customer at the same instant, and it did not have to. It became the one thing the whole network was talking about, passed along founder threads, VC emails, group chats, and phone calls until it was common knowledge.

Third, a network to carry it. This is the part that made this bank unlike an ordinary one. The customer base was packed into one world: technology and life-science startups, the venture and private-equity funds that backed them, and other large investors. Many read the same founder threads and took calls from the same advisors. The bank had spent forty years making itself the center of that world. It banked the founders, and the funds behind the founders, and the people who moved between them. On any ordinary day, that density was the product.

Fourth, a frictionless exit. Moving money no longer required standing in a branch line. It was a wire to an account you probably already kept somewhere else, sent from a desk without leaving the chair.

Reason, signal, network, exit. Those four are the anatomy of a one-day run, and you can hold them up against any bank. Weaken any one of them and the same fear is harder to turn into a same-day collapse. Leave all four at full strength and you no longer have a crowd of strangers. You have a room where everyone knows everyone, everyone is standing near the same single door, and everyone just heard the same word at the same moment. Each person walking toward the exit is being sensible. The problem is the door.

The bank's own chief executive said the mechanism out loud, without meaning to. On a call that Thursday he told clients the bank had ample liquidity, except, he added, in the case where everyone is telling each other it is in trouble. That exception is the entire business. A bank can meet any normal day of withdrawals. It cannot meet all of them at once, because it lent your money out. That is what a bank is. What it holds is not your cash. It is everyone's shared belief that everyone else will stay put.

You can watch that belief break in one person.

Late on a Wednesday night, the engineers at a robotics startup were still working when the founder saw the chatter in a group of startup founders. A few people, maybe three to five, were saying the bank news looked concerning. That was Wednesday. "The next day," he said, "that's all anyone could talk about." He did not wait for anyone to tell him what to do. That same evening he moved about half his company's cash to another bank he already used. His reasoning was not fear, exactly. It was arithmetic. "I don't know if I'm freaking out or not, but it's not worth the risk." If he was wrong, the cost was missing a little interest for two weeks. If he was right, the cost of waiting was the whole company.

By Thursday he was buried in calls and texts. By midday he had heard that three of the biggest funds were telling their companies to get out. He moved another quarter of the cash; it cleared after a couple of hours. Friday morning, his last transfer, the part above the insured limit, was still marked pending. He called himself one of the lucky ones. He knew two founders who had decided to keep their money in.

The difference between lucky and trapped was not who read the balance sheet more carefully. It was who was in which chat, and how fast they moved.

And here the network turns on itself in a specific way: every person doing the sensible thing made it worse for everyone else. A finance professor put the individual math plainly. The first group out gets all their money; the last group out may get nothing. A founder said the same thing without the tenure: "if you don't, you might lose your company, and if you do, it's not really that big of a deal." Multiply that by a thousand founders who all trust each other and act on the same afternoon, and the rational move for each becomes the ruinous move for the group. The advisors emailing their companies to pull cash were doing their jobs. They were also the wind. Afterward, one investor accused his peers of toppling the bank with their warnings. Another answered that you don't blame a depositor for taking money out of a bank. Both were right. That is the trap.

To see how much of this was the network and not the balance sheet alone, look at an older run, one that moved at the speed of a queue. In the autumn of 2007, a British lender had to go to its central bank for emergency funding. Its depositors panicked too. But they were ordinary savers, strangers to each other, scattered across a country. So the run looked the way a run used to look. People stood in lines outside branches in the September heat. Staff let five in at a time and handed numbered tickets to the last customers who would be served. In one city a pensioner camped outside from six in the morning; an ambulance came for a woman who fainted in the queue; an eleven-year-old waited her turn to withdraw the twenty pounds her grandmother had put in an account for her. One woman told a reporter she knew that standing there might be making things worse, but she had come anyway.

That run pulled out about a billion pounds on its worst day, four or five percent of deposits, and roughly two billion, about eight percent, over the following days. Serious money, but spread across days rather than compressed into an afternoon. The friction was the point. Standing in a physical line is slow, and being a stranger means you hear the news late. The queue everyone photographed was the visible, slow-motion version of what happened at Silicon Valley Bank invisibly, in a single day, with no line at all. Go back further, to the bank panics of the early 1930s, and the rumor crawled even slower, town to town, carried by newspaper headlines that took a day to print.

The trend runs one way. The more alike and connected the depositors, and the more frictionless the exit, the faster a bank goes from sound to seized. Weeks became days became hours. The line kept getting shorter until it disappeared.

The network did not stop at the people who chose the bank, either. A man in Kentucky who runs a laser-engraving shop found that a deposit he was counting on failed to arrive. The online marketplace he sells through had routed some seller payments through the failed bank. He needed that money for shipping, materials, and other bills. He had already called a supplier to push back an order for the coming week, and no one could tell him when the funds would come. He called the effect on his business catastrophic. The marketplace said about half a percent of its active sellers, out of millions, had payments delayed. Half a percent is nothing to the platform. To him it was a supplier order pushed back and shipping he could not buy. In that same window, thousands of companies were quietly doing the same arithmetic on whether payroll, due the fifteenth, would clear.

None of this splits into clean percentages. No one can say how much of the forty-two billion was set in motion by a group chat, how much by a public post, how much by a finance chief who read the annual report and did the responsible thing. The honest claim is not about proportion. It is about sequence and speed. The balance sheet made the bank able to fail. The network made the vulnerability fatal, and it made it fatal in a day.

Which points at an odd way to measure the safety of a bank, or of anything built on trust. We are trained to look at what it owns: the loans, the bonds, the capital cushion. Those tell you whether it can survive a normal week. They say nothing about how fast a bad week can move. For that you would have to measure something that shows up on no balance sheet: how much the depositors resemble one another, how many of them share the same friends and the same fears, how quickly the whole base can act on a single piece of news. A concentrated, connected, uninsured crowd is not a source of stability. It is a run waiting for a trigger.

And no one who saw it coming had a clean way to act. Warn the companies you are paid to protect and you protect them; you are also the word going around. Stay quiet to spare the bank and you gamble with other people's payroll. There is no version of that choice that keeps your hands clean, because the danger you would be trying not to trigger is the same danger that makes the warning true.

The uncomfortable part is that the thing you would flag as the danger is the same thing everyone spent forty years building on purpose. The tight community that made the bank valuable, that fed it deals and deposits and loyalty, was the very wiring that drained it. Same network, opposite outcome, depending only on the day and the word going around. Trust runs along those connections, and so does its sudden absence. They travel the same wire, at the same speed, and you do not get to keep one without exposing yourself to the other.

A bank does not really hold your money. It holds everyone's belief that everyone else will stay calm. The more tightly a community is bound together, the more completely it can decide, all at once, to stop believing.
