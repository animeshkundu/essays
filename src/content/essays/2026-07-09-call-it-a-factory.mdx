Both sides of the data center fight have settled on the wrong sentence. Data centers, the line goes, should pay their fair share. Google backs the House bill and says it has "long been committed to ensuring that we pay our own way." The Data Center Coalition says its members are committed to paying their "full energy costs." The White House pledge says the American people "should not be footing the bill for the benefit of private companies." A bipartisan bill from Kathy Castor and Gabe Evans would write the idea into federal law for any customer drawing 100 MW or more. When Google, the White House, and both parties reach for the same phrase, the phrase has stopped doing any work.

Here is the sentence that still has teeth. An AI campus is a factory. Not a public utility, not civic infrastructure, not a road or a reservoir. It is a private building full of privately owned machines making a privately sold product. The power plant built to feed it, the substation, the 500 kV line built to reach it: those are its supply chain, not the public's. The campus should pay for all of it, in full and backed by collateral, before the grid pours a single footing on its behalf. And every asset it forces into being should sit on its own books as a private cost until a regulator proves, on the record, that the rest of us come out ahead.

That last clause is where the real fight is.

Start with the strange part. The bill is already moving, and it is unusually easy to trace to its cause. PJM's own market monitor estimated that data center load alone raised the 2025 to 2026 capacity auction revenues by $9.3 billion, a jump of 174 percent. The Union of Concerned Scientists identified $4.3 billion in 2024 grid projects across seven states built solely to connect data centers, with more than 95 percent of the cost handed straight to local bills. Maryland's consumer advocate says PJM's rules assigned Maryland customers $2 billion in capital costs for transmission driven by data centers elsewhere, $1.6 billion of it recovered on bills over the next decade, about $345 for the average household. This is not the diffuse, bill-creeps-up-a-little cost of a hot summer. NERC says data centers account for most of the projected rise in North American power demand over the next decade. This is a countable class of large loads, not a public cost you cannot pin down.

Two easy explanations for why that lands on your bill both fail. The first is that growth always costs money and this is just growth. But ordinary growth spreads thin across a million customers. This is concentrated, and the wire runs to a fenced campus with one owner. The second is the industry's own answer: that data centers already pay their own way. On the electrons they have already bought, maybe so. But "pay your own way" quietly changes the subject. The bill in front of us is not for power already sold. It is for the plants, lines, and substations now being built on spec, for loads that can pack up and leave. Who carries that risk is the whole question, and it is the question the phrase is built to skip.

The rule that should have applied is a century old. When a developer asks a utility to run a line to a new tract, Florida makes the developer cover the construction cost minus four years of the revenue the utility expects to earn back: a contribution in aid of construction, paid at the gate. When Bonneville's aluminum smelters expanded, federal power law let BPA bill the new load at its higher New Resources rate instead of the cheap legacy rate, so a newcomer could not skim power built for everyone else. When the data center company Switch tried to leave Nevada Power in 2015 for cheaper wholesale electricity, regulators made it pay a $27 million exit fee so the customers it left behind would not eat the stranded plant. When Oregon looked at customers over 25 MW, it scrapped most of the line-extension allowance smaller users get and told the giants to front more of the cost themselves.

And now several states are relearning the same rule in real time. Georgia's commission, in January 2025, let Georgia Power bind data centers over 100 MW to 15-year contracts and minimum bills, so a campus cannot walk before it has paid for what was built for it. Virginia built a new rate class, GS-5, that makes 25 MW customers pay for at least 85 percent of the transmission and distribution they reserve and 60 percent of the generation. Ohio's regulators made AEP's data centers cover 85 percent of the power they subscribe to for 12 years whether they burn it or not. In June 2026, FERC told all six grid operators to justify or rewrite their large-load rules, pressing for cost-recovery deals backed by hard collateral so that, as Commissioner David Rosner put it, if the infrastructure gets built and the data center never shows up, the neighbors are not left holding the note. Every one of these is a confession. If the old default were built to make these customers pay for what they trigger, not one of these rules would need writing.

There is a reason to collect at the gate rather than trust the meter. When Century Aluminum's two Kentucky smelters walked away from Big Rivers Electric in 2013 and 2014, they took two-thirds of the co-op's load with them. The plants built to serve them did not vanish. After the first left, regulators let Big Rivers pull an extra $54 million a year from the customers who stayed, about $17 a month on a home bill; the second departure added $36 million more. A data center's whole business can chase cheaper power or a newer chip to the next county. The wire cannot follow it.

A transmission line to a data center is public the way a loading dock is a public park.

Now the strongest case against me, in full. A grid is a mesh, not a row of private driveways, and some of what a campus triggers genuinely helps everyone. FERC's own Order No. 1000 says transmission cost should fall on those who benefit, roughly in proportion to the benefit, and a fat new line into a strained region can steady the lights for a whole town. E3, in a 2026 review, found no evidence that today's rates have historically subsidized data centers; that review was funded by the industry's own trade group, but Virginia's independent legislative analysts reached the same finding of no historic cost shifting. Georgia Power argues the reverse of my thesis outright: that revenue from these giant customers will knock about $8.50 a month off the typical residential bill. And the development case is real, the same one that won Alcoa a 240 MW public-power contract at Massena to hold onto 550 smelter jobs. Force every megawatt of shared iron onto one customer's tab and you might strangle the growth that pays for the grid.

Take all of that seriously and it still does not deliver the default we run today. It delivers a test. Florida already wrote the test down: a utility may waive a customer's construction charge, but if it does, it must write down its own plant unless the commission finds "a quantifiable benefit to the general body of ratepayers." That single clause is the whole argument. Sharing is allowed. It just has to be proven, line item by line item, before the money is socialized. What we do now is the mirror image. We presume the wire is public, spread it across every bill by default, and make the household prove the harm years later, after the concrete has cured. Put the burden back where Florida put it a generation ago and most of the "shared infrastructure" claim has to earn its keep or come off the public books.

<Aside>
The thresholds vary wildly by state: 10 MW in South Dakota, 25 MW in Ohio and Virginia, 100 MW in Georgia and the federal bill, 150 MW in Alabama. Each number is a bet on where "large enough to cause system costs" begins.
</Aside>

This is the part I cannot wave away. When a utility builds a plant and a line into a booming county, the paperwork does not cleanly separate the slice caused by one hyperscaler from the slice that firms up the grid for the diner down the road. E3 and the Virginia analysts are right that the historical subsidy is unproven. But that is a claim about the past. The grid NERC now forecasts is a different animal: more than 224 GW of new summer peak in ten years, most of it data centers, with resource and transmission growth already lagging what is needed and 13 of 23 assessment areas facing resource adequacy trouble. The tangle over attribution is not a reason to socialize first and sort it out never. It is the reason to make the customer pay first and argue about refunds later, which is how a contribution in aid of construction already works: the causer pays up front, and later users who benefit pay their share back.

So stop calling it public infrastructure. When a 500 kV line is proposed through Vicky Hu's Ashburn community to feed data centers across town, a line she said "doesn't give power to our house," let the load that needs it pay the full tab, post the bond, and answer her question before the towers go up, not after they cast their shadow. Call the thing what it is. A factory pays to connect to the grid. It does not get to be the grid.
