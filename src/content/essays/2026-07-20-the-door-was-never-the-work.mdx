The consensus is that coffee badging is a cheat. You are told to come to the office. You swipe your badge, buy a coffee, let a few people see your face, and drive home to do the actual work. The company calls this misrepresenting your presence. Push it far enough and it becomes falsifying attendance, which a court can treat as cause for firing. On its face the consensus holds: if you are paid to be somewhere and you are not there, you lied about where you were.

It is still the wrong story.

Coffee badging is not a loophole in hybrid work. It is what an organization gets when it stops trusting the work and starts trusting the door. A badge swipe was never a measure of work. The moment a company makes the swipe the score, it does not root out the few who were gaming the system. It teaches everyone else the real rule: the door is the job.

Start with what the door actually knows. A badge swipe is a security event before it is anything else. The system that records it was built to keep strangers out of a building, and it is very good at that. It knows a credential asked a door to open at a recorded time. It knows the credential belongs to you. It does not know whether you then wrote a line of code, closed a sale, taught someone how the thing works, or sat in a chair reading the news with your coat still on.

The dashboards make this worse by hiding it. A widely cited office attendance index counts only your first entry of the day and compares it against a February 2020 baseline. First entry. Not the hours after. Across ten cities that gauge read 51.3 percent this summer. But a first swipe is not a day. The people who build occupancy numbers out of badge data are candid about the move from one to the other: they get from the badge to the day, in their phrase, by inference.

The swipe is a thin fact. The full day is a guess wearing the clothes of a fact.
</PullQuote>

None of this is new in spirit. Bosses have counted faces since the punch clock arrived in 1888. Being seen at your desk has long paid better than working out of sight: the research on passive face time finds that remote employees draw lower evaluations, smaller raises, and fewer promotions even when they work just as hard and just as long. What is new is that the performance now leaves a receipt. The old proxy lived in a manager's memory, where it could be argued with. The new one is a log that can be sorted, exported to a spreadsheet, and used to fire you.

Look, first, at the ordinary version of that short visit, before anyone called it evidence. A worker at an Atlanta consulting firm drove in once or twice a month and gave the office four hours, eleven to three: in after the morning rush, there in time for the catered lunch, a few hours with colleagues, out before the commute home turned hellish, then back to the real work at her own desk. Call that a con if you want, but it is really a person deciding which four hours of a day the office is worth, at a time when the average office day costs a worker about sixty dollars in lunch, commute, coffee, and parking against nineteen at home. The same short visit that one company treats as grounds for firing is, in another, just how a sane adult spends a Tuesday.

Which brings us to the people the log got attached to. In a lawsuit filed this year, forty-six former employees of a large Canadian telecom say they were fired for precisely this. Their time at the company runs from two years to thirty-two. Among them are salespeople, engineers, project managers, and people from human resources, the department usually asked to enforce such rules. The company says they were caught "swiping in and leaving shortly after," that every firing followed a thorough investigation, and, in its own account of the case, that most admitted to deliberate, repeated falsification. The workers say something quieter and harder to shake off: that the brief visit had been tolerated for years, and that some of them had been told outright by their own managers that the swipe was the whole of the ask. Their lawyer quotes the line he says some of them were handed. As long as you come in and swipe your fob, you don't have to stay the whole day.

Read those two accounts side by side and you are not looking at a fight about whether people worked. You are looking at a fight about what a two-second tap of a fob meant, and the entire case turns on it. If the brief visit was a tolerated ritual, then these are ordinary people cut loose without the severance that years, and in some cases decades, of service are supposed to earn, because a firing "for cause" erases severance. Individual claims run from $18,000 to $350,000; together they seek more than $6 million, which is the plain cash value of the difference between a ritual and misconduct. If it really was misconduct, the company simply enforced a rule. The same fob holds both stories. The allegations have not been tested in court, and the company says it will defend them.

Here the log stops being a record and becomes a lever. The suit alleges, citing documents a whistleblower handed the workers' lawyer, that managers were instructed to dismiss roughly thirty people per office, at least one from every team, to set an example for the ones who stayed. Set that beside a survey number and let the two argue. In one poll, 42 percent of workers said they feel more pressure to look present than to be productive. Read it twice. Not more pressure to look present than to look busy. More than to do the work. That is not laziness discovering a hack. That is a workforce reading, correctly, what its employer decided to count.

Now make the honest case for the door, because it is not nothing. Some work only happens in a room. Put a junior engineer near her team and the feedback on her code goes up by about 18 percent, and the research shows the gain lands hardest on the people with the least experience to lose. The valuable part is the unbookable part: the question asked on the way to lunch, the whiteboard nobody erased after the meeting, the second thought you carry to a colleague's desk an hour later. A company that wants that back is not a villain; it is trying to buy back something remote work quietly took. And when a team has genuinely agreed to share three days a week, a clean log can look fairer than a manager's memory, which plays favorites.

But watch what the door does to that honest wish. There is an old rule, Goodhart's law, that says when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. The office was supposed to produce the hallway question. So you resolve to count the hallway. But you cannot count a hallway question, so you count the swipe, because the swipe is the only part that leaves a machine-readable trace. And the swipe is exactly the part of the day that has nothing to do with the hallway question. You end up measuring the one thing that was never the point, then defending it as though it were.

The tell is who bends the rule. In one survey, managers report coffee badging more than the individual contributors below them, 47 percent to 34. The same short visit reads as misconduct when a worker does it and as strategic scheduling when someone higher up does it. A door log does not correct that asymmetry. It hides it, because the log cannot see the difference between the two, and by the end neither can the company. Enforcing a real office day is one thing. Enforcing the log as if it were that day is another. That is the real corruption: not that people optimize the badge, but that an organization loses the ability to tell the worker who came for the room from the worker who came for the reader, and then fires on the difference it can no longer see.

So stop asking whether they cheated the door. Ask what the door was ever able to see. You may have one too: a fob or a phone you tap against a reader, and a system somewhere logging that tap and filing it under presence. It has never once seen your work. It only ever saw you arrive. The question the lawsuit cannot settle, the one worth carrying out past it, is what your employer imagines it is buying in the half second it takes to log your arrival, and whether it would still know the difference on the morning you gave it nothing else.
