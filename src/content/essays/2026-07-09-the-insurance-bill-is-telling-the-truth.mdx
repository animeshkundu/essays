The politics of home insurance has settled on one job: make the number smaller. Cap the rate. Expand the last-resort plan. Negotiate the increase down and call it a win for families. In red states and blue, making the number smaller is close to the only thing officials still agree on. It is also wrong.

The premium is not the crime. It is the confession.

For most of the last century, home insurance was the boring line in the mortgage, the rounding error you paid and forgot. Now it decides whether you keep the house. Nationally it climbed from about 10 percent of the average monthly mortgage payment in 2013 to 14 percent in 2025, and premiums rose roughly 70 percent from 2019 to 2025. A bill nobody used to notice became the bill that ends the sale.

The easy explanations don't survive contact with the data.

Greed? Homeowners insurers lost money on underwriting in 22 of the 30 years from 1995 to 2024, an average loss of 4.2 percent, and they paid the most in claims, relative to premium, in exactly the riskiest places. Be honest about the seam here: in 2024, $8.8 billion in investment income turned a $1.8 billion underwriting loss into a $6.9 billion profit, so the industry is not starving. But an industry that lost money underwriting in most of the past thirty years is not running a shakedown.

Inflation? Premiums outran it. They rose 8.7 percent faster than inflation from 2018 to 2022, then 12.7 percent in 2023 and 10.4 percent in 2024, against consumer inflation of 4.1 and 2.9 percent. And general inflation does not sort itself by ZIP code. This did. The fifth of ZIP codes with the highest expected climate losses paid $2,321 on average, 82 percent more than the lowest-risk fifth, were dropped by their insurers about 80 percent more often, and filed claims that cost around $24,000 against $19,000 elsewhere.

Bad luck, then? Run the cleanest test in the data. Take two coastal counties on either side of the Florida and Georgia line, with similar hurricane exposure. Between 2018 and 2023, inflation-adjusted premiums in some northeast Florida coastal counties rose about $1,000. The Georgia side rose less than $500. Similar exposure, different bill. The economists who found the gap, Benjamin Keys and Philip Mulder, point to reinsurance as a major part of it: Florida's specialty insurers lean on reinsurers for close to 40 percent of properties, Georgia's national carriers for under 10 percent, and the cost of U.S. property and casualty reinsurance doubled over those five years.

No single one of these boring stories carries the weight alone, and honest researchers say so: the public data does not hand every premium dollar to one cause. But stop hunting for a lone villain and a plainer thing comes into view. The bill has started to price something the market spent years barely pricing at all.

Follow the money backward. A catastrophe model simulates thousands of storms or fires against your roof and your location and returns an expected annual loss. The insurer turns that number into a rate, or into a decision not to write you at all. The state regulator decides how much of that number is allowed to reach your bill. Reinsurance, the coverage insurers buy for their own tail risk, marks it up. For many mortgaged owners, it lands in escrow, which slices the annual premium into twelve monthly payments and folds them into the mortgage. That monthly number sets what a buyer can afford to bid. The bid sets the price. Climate risk does not knock on the front door. It arrives as a line on an escrow statement, and by the time it gets there it has already helped decide what the house is worth.

That is the correction underneath the anger. For years, many home prices and monthly costs did not fully reflect the fire and wind risk sitting under them. Now the premium is dragging that risk into view. Benjamin Keys says the plain version out loud: higher premiums should end up capitalized into house prices. The invoice reveals part of what the home's value was always resting on, rather than attacking that value.

Which is why most of the fixes on offer are not relief. They move the risk somewhere you cannot see it.

Florida is the clean case of laundering. The state caps Citizens, its public insurer, on a 14 percent glide path, then hands policies back to private carriers, and the capped number simply reappears on the renewal notice: Marilyn Sweeney went from $2,335 with Citizens to $4,676, then $5,147. What the cap hides in one place it spreads in another. A Citizens shortfall after a big storm can be assessed at up to 10 percent a year on nearly every property and casualty policy in the state, down to the auto, boat, and pet lines. The safe inland driver helps pay for the coast, and never sees the transfer.

Louisiana shows the human edge of the same move. After the 2020 and 2021 hurricanes, a dozen insurers writing homeowners coverage there went insolvent and the market cracked. Janet Tobias, a 68-year-old New Orleans native, lost her policy when her insurer failed and watched her premium climb from $4,000 to $11,000. The state's answer was to lean harder on its last-resort insurer, which by law must price at least 10 percent above the highest rate in each parish, then to suspend even that surcharge for the coast south of Interstate 10, and to let insurers start dropping longtime customers the old three-year rule had protected. The risk did not fall. It shifted onto the state and onto the next family in line.

North Carolina is subtler, because both sides agreed the danger was real. The rate bureau asked for a 42.2 percent average increase, up to 99.4 percent in some places. The commissioner settled it to 7.5 percent, then 7.5 percent more a year later, capped at 35 percent anywhere, and said plainly that even the lower number was enough for insurers that had paid large claims and faced rising reinsurance costs. A decision to meter the truth out slowly, sealed with a promise that the bureau will not ask again before June 2027. The gap between 42 and 7.5 does not disappear. It waits.

California spent years pricing wildfire off past losses instead of forward-looking models, which held rates below what the hills had come to deserve. In 2024 it changed the deal: insurers may finally model the future, but only if they write more policies in the places they most want to leave. Looking backward did not buy safety: State Farm moved to drop about 30,000 home and other property policies in a single 2024 filing, and the state's insurer of last resort now carries $750 billion in exposure as of March 2026, up 242 percent since 2022.

Each of these buys a few quiet years by writing the risk into someone else's future: the next buyer, the inland neighbor, the driver who never lived near the coast, the household one storm away from the assessment. That is not affordability. It is a map that keeps lying about where it is dangerous to live.
</PullQuote>

Now the honest objection, at full strength. The signal is dirty. A homeowner with a low credit score pays nearly $2,000 more a year than an identical neighbor with a high score, almost double, and in 23 states that credit penalty is at least a doubling. Black borrowers, lower-income borrowers, and FHA borrowers are more likely to be crushed by the cost. And repricing too fast does real damage: rising premiums pushed about 31,000 mortgages into delinquency in 2022, and one projection puts the toll at 203,000 a year through mid-century. Force the true price on people all at once and you do not get adaptation. You get foreclosures, and a new redlining wearing an actuary's coat.

All true. But the cure for a dirty signal is to clean it, not to switch off the meter. I would start by pulling credit scoring out of catastrophe pricing, where it never belonged; a wildfire does not check your credit score. Then do the two things suppression is built to avoid. Make the model answer for itself: Colorado now forces insurers to file their wildfire models, disclose your risk score, let you appeal it, and credit your mitigation. And pay for the roof directly: Alabama covers the full cost of a wind retrofit up to $10,000, and a fortified home earns a real discount on the wind part of the bill. Slow the shock, fund the fix, protect the person. Never falsify the price.

Because the price is the part of this system pressed closest to the physical world. A premium is a letter about where we built, and how, and what it now costs to stand in the way of fire and wind. The letter is smudged in places, and we should clean it. But you do not fix a smudged letter by burying it. The water is still rising underneath.

So stop trying to make the number small. Make it honest, and make the house safer than the number says. Price the risk. Pay for the roof. Stop laundering the map.
