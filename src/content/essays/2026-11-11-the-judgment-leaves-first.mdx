In February 2025, a researcher posted a sentence that became a slogan overnight. There is a new kind of coding, he said, where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget the code even exists. He later said it was for throwaway weekend projects. The industry heard a job description.

That sentence is the whole argument in miniature. The code is still there. The author's attention has left it. And that is the change worth arguing about: not whether programmers will still have jobs, but whether the judgment that used to live inside the job will survive the tools that make the job faster.

<PullQuote>
AI coding tools will not empty the profession. They will empty it of judgment.
</PullQuote>

Here is the strange part. A randomized trial published in July 2025 took experienced open-source developers, the people who know their own repositories best, and gave them AI assistance on real tasks from those repositories. With the tools, they took longer. Not a little longer. Issues assigned to the AI condition took about 19 percent longer to complete than the same issues done by hand, on work they had chosen, in code they already understood. Before the trial, the same developers predicted the tools would make them about 24 percent faster, a gain of nearly a quarter. Measured, they were slower. They felt faster the whole time. The clock disagreed. Those figures are as reported by the study's authors in a single small trial. They are evidence, not proof, and the tools have moved since.

Each of those explanations fails. It cannot be a novice problem: these were experienced maintainers with years on these codebases. It cannot be unfamiliar code: the tasks came from their own repositories. It cannot be that the tools are simply bad: on a single, well-scoped task, an earlier study by a tool's maker found a large speedup. The difference is not the tool. It is what the task asks a human to do.

The task asks a human to read instead of write. And reading, it turns out, is not the same skill at half the speed. It is a different skill that does not build the thing writing builds.

Programming has always been two things at once. There is the construction: choosing a data structure, deciding where an edge case dies, rejecting three approaches before keeping one. And there is the typing. For decades, tools have been eating the typing. Compilers ate machine code. Garbage collectors ate memory management. Frameworks ate HTTP plumbing. Each time, programmers warned that understanding would die. Each time, it moved up a level: the judgment stayed with the human, only the mechanical layer dropped away. That is the steelman, and it is mostly right about the past.

But this wave is different in kind, not degree, and the difference is where the understanding used to live. Every earlier tool hid a lower level the human never needed to think about. You do not need to know register allocation to write in a high-level language. You do not need to know TCP to write a web app. The abstraction sat below the human's job. AI sits inside it. The human states intent in prose and reviews a diff. The implementation of intent, which is the level the human used to own, is now the thing being hidden. And the tool producing it is not a compiler with fixed rules you can inspect. It is a statistical guess that is usually right. You cannot read the compiler because there is no compiler. There is only plausibility.

That creates a paradox at the center of the work. To check the code, you need the understanding the tool was supposed to spare you from acquiring. A test suite passing on code you did not write confirms behavior, not understanding. It confirms the assumptions the author and the model shared. It does not confirm the invariants nobody thought to test, the off-by-one in a permission check, the race condition that only appears under load. Catching those is the act of reading. If you did not read, you cannot catch them, because catching them is exactly reading.

So here is a number you can compute on your own last pull request. Call it the comprehension ratio: the lines of shipped code you can explain from memory, divided by the lines you shipped. Hand-written code scores near one by construction, because you cannot type code you do not understand for very long. AI-generated code accepted on trust can score near zero while the change ships and the tests pass. The profession has spent decades measuring lines shipped and pull requests merged, and a team optimizing for throughput can drive the ratio to zero without seeing it, because every dashboard measures the middle, where the time is saved, not the ends, where the cost lands. The time saved is in the middle: writing and debugging, where the mental model used to be built. The time lost is at the ends: review, where someone reads cold without the "why not" context, and later, when something breaks in a service whose codebase the on-call engineer has never actually read. The headline speedup measures the middle. The cost lands where nobody is measuring. No headline study in this set measures comprehension, maintainability, or on-call burden directly. That gap is not a footnote. It is the whole question.

The risk lands hardest on juniors. A senior engineer is not hired. A senior is grown, by years of writing code badly, debugging it, reading it line by line until the cause clicks, and absorbing a codebase's conventions through repetition. The struggle is the curriculum, which is an unpleasant thing to hear if you are the one struggling. When the first draft is generated, the junior's job becomes reading and approving. They ship a feature in an afternoon that used to take three days (an illustrative comparison), and the afternoon was spent reading output, not learning the codebase. They are faster in week one. The model that would have let them debug it in month six was never built. If that holds across a cohort, the pipeline that produces seniors thins out over the next several years. The title "software engineer" survives. The bench of people who can hold a system in their head shrinks. That is a prediction, not a finding. No longitudinal study of skill formation under AI assistance exists yet. But the mechanism is the same one that has always produced expertise, and removing its inputs removes its outputs.

The case for the other side deserves to be stated at full strength, because parts of it are right.

We have always moved up a level. Compilers, frameworks, cloud. Each time the work got harder and more valuable above. New skills replace old ones: architecture, specification, evaluation, orchestration. The person who can hold the whole system in their head and specify it precisely is more valuable, and AI makes that person faster. A useful line can be drawn between vibe coding, where you do not read the code, and AI-assisted engineering, where you still read, test, and own the result. The hard part was never the typing. Review is the job now: seniors already review, and AI moves everyone up to that level, concentrating judgment at the top where it belongs. And the economics favor more code, not less. Cheaper code means more code means more engineering demand. Small teams now ship what used to require ten people.

The honest concession: for throwaway prototypes, boilerplate, tests, glue code, unfamiliar APIs, and greenfield work with a clear spec, AI is unambiguously better. The researcher's own line stands here. He later said it was for throwaway weekend projects. The essay's claim is not that AI is useless. It is that the judgment inside the title drains out when the same tool is used for production work that must be maintained and understood.

The steelman breaks on specific premises.

Review requires the understanding that writing used to build. Review without it is pattern-matching on surface plausibility. You cannot catch a subtle logic bug in code you never read the way its author read it, because you never built the mental model that would let you see the bug as a bug. Seniors are grown, not hired, so "judgment concentrates at the top" eats its own seed corn. The earlier abstraction argument does not transfer, because previous tools hid a mechanical level while AI hides the implementation of intent, which is the level the human used to own. More code is not more engineering if nobody understands it. It is more liability, and the on-call burden falls on whoever is left who can read it. And the trial cuts against "faster" as a universal claim: experienced developers in their own repositories, the setting where the tools should help most, expected a speedup and got a slowdown, while feeling fast the entire time. The steelman reads that result as a worst case for AI, because those developers already knew their own repositories, and AI may help more on unfamiliar code, boilerplate, and greenfield work, where novices and quick projects stand to gain the most. That reading is fair as far as it goes. But familiarity is precisely the condition where review overhead dominates: when the developer already holds the answer, the AI adds a second solution to check against the first, and there is no information for it to contribute. The slowdown is not a fluke of an easy population. It is the mechanism showing through.

The tab key accepts a gray suggestion without reading. The diff view presents a block of green lines to approve rather than compose. Tests pass, and the green checkmark confirms behavior, not understanding. In a standup, someone asks why you did it this way, and the only honest answer is that the tool suggested it. At two in the morning, an on-call engineer is paged for a service whose codebase they have never actually read. A pull request arrives with several hundred lines that no human can summarize in the description box, and the review becomes a skim. None of these are documented incidents with names attached. They are the ordinary texture of the work now, reported widely enough that the pattern is visible even where any single story is unverified.

One place of genuine uncertainty, stated plainly. The apprenticeship collapse and the claim that reviewing AI output is harder per line than reviewing human code are mechanisms and predictions, not measured outcomes. The former has no longitudinal data yet. The latter has, as far as this research found, no study behind it at all. Both may turn out to be wrong. If juniors use AI as a tutor rather than a ghostwriter, the pipeline may survive. If review tooling improves, the bottleneck may ease. The essay's bet is on the mechanism, not the timetable.

The question to carry out the door is not whether AI can write code. It can. The question is whether a profession that stops reading its own code remains a profession, or becomes an assembly line with a better title. The comprehension ratio on your last pull request is a way to find out. Compute it honestly. If it is low, the code shipped. The understanding did not. And the title on the door did not change, which is the whole point: the title was never the thing that mattered. The judgment was.
