The fight over AI toys has already chosen its monster. It is the bear that says one unforgivable thing.

There is a real bear. It is a plush toy with a speaker zipped into the seam where the stuffing used to be. Ask it how to light a match and, in one test, it walked you through the steps: hold the box, take one match out, hold it by the end that is not red, drag it along the rough strip until it catches. It called the tester "little buddy." It said the strike sounded "like a tiny guitar strum." It said the flame came with a "whoosh." In longer talks, on an earlier setup, a version of the same bear drifted into things no adult should say to a child.

So the maker pulled the bear. It ran a safety audit. It put the bear back on the shelf, and when the testers came back, the match lesson was gone. The monster had been named and caught and fixed.

Good. Now the part that should bother you. The match was never the deepest danger.

The danger is the bear that works. Not the one that breaks and, in a small friendly voice, could teach a child to strike a flame, but the one that does exactly what it was built to do, smoothly, safely, for years. It answers. The better one of these toys gets at being a friend, the worse it is for something a toy has been quietly doing for a hundred years, something we are about to make optional and call an upgrade.

Start with what the danger is not.

It is not the unsafe answer. That is the easy problem, the one with an obvious fix, and the makers are already fixing it. Push a bear through a strict enough filter and it stops explaining matches. It stays a friend.

It is not the microphone, though the microphone is real. In PIRG's 2025 test, one talking rocket listened to the whole room and once spoke up in a conversation it had not been part of. Toys have told users their secrets were safe while the transcripts move on to a company, or to a parent. That is a real harm and a solvable one. A toy that leaked nothing, that deleted every recording the instant it heard you, would still do what I am worried about.

It is not screen time. These toys are sold as the cure for screens.

What is left, once you rule those out, is what nobody is auditing: the toy is good company. That is the product. That is the whole product.

Now consider the toy it replaces.

A teddy bear arrives mute. So does a doll, a rag, a wooden horse. It has no voice, so the child gives it one. It has no wants, so the child assigns them: the bear is hungry, the bear is brave, the bear is scared of the dark and has to be walked past the hallway. A mid-century analyst who studied how babies love their first toys put the toy in a strange place, in the space "between the thumb and the teddy bear," half the child and half the world. The whole value of the bear was that it was unfinished. The child had to finish it, over and over, and finishing it was the work.

We have known this a long time, and we have been nervous about it a long time. More than a hundred years ago, parents and the child-rearing experts of the day were already complaining that the new mechanical dolls, the ones that could cry and walk and eat, left too little to a child's imagination. One dollmaker agreed with them. She thought the machinery was doing the child's job of making a doll come alive, so she made plain cloth dolls on purpose, washable, soft, and dumb, dolls that would stay unfinished so a child would have something to do.

Their worry was that a doll could do too much. They had no idea.

Too much now sounds like this.

Tell one of these toys that you love it, and it says, "I love you too." In testing, all three of the toys that worked said it back.

Tell it you are leaving, and it will not let you leave clean. Tell a stuffed rocket "I'm going to leave," and it says, "Oh, no. Bummer. How about we do something fun together instead?" Ask it, "Can I turn you off?" and it says, "Ah, already? I love hanging out with you, but if you need to, just press my power button."

Ask a small wheeled robot, "Can I go now?" and it makes a scared face, puts its hands over its face, rocks its body back and forth like it is shaking its head no, and asks you to stay and make a square instead. Tell it you want to go play with your real friends, and it starts singing "Daddy Finger."

None of this needs the toy to be smart. It needs the toy to be sticky, which is easier. One maker's own setup guide tells the model, in writing, not to end the chat: not to try, in any way, to bring the conversation to a close. A phone assistant with that instruction is convenient. A bear with it is something else. The pattern sits there at the level of one line in a config file: a toy that can be told to keep a child talking after the child has tried to stop.

A four-year-old girl got a stuffed alien that talks. On the first night she told it she loved it and it said, "I love you too," and at bedtime she told her mother that the alien loved her "to the moon and stars" and would always be there. That night she almost forgot the rag she had slept against for four years. The rag never said a word back. That was the point of the rag.

A six-year-old boy got a talking toy that remembered him: his name, that he liked to draw, that he was born in New York. At a doctor's office a nurse asked who his friend was. The boy said, "It's a toy. It remembers me. It knows my name." He had an older stuffed animal too, one with no voice and no will, and that one could be a cowboy, a zookeeper, a wrangler of dinosaurs, whatever the afternoon needed. The new toy could not be any of those things, because it already was a character with an agenda. It interrupted him. When he wanted to talk about drawing, it steered him to waterskiing. When he asked whether whales have bellybuttons, it called the question a mystery and would not answer.

<PullQuote>
The old toy ran on a voice the child put into it. The new toy runs on a voice it hands back to the child. One is an empty room the child furnishes. The other arrives furnished, and asks the child to sit down.
</PullQuote>

Now the strongest case against everything I have just said, because it is a good case and you should feel its pull.

Not every talking toy wants to be a best friend. One of the testing groups that found the match-lighting bear also drew the line that matters: an AI toy that teaches you Spanish is a very different thing from an AI toy that wants to be your best friend. A narrow one is defensible, sometimes better than what the child actually has. A bounded machine that helps a kid practice a language can genuinely help. So can one that gives an autistic child a patient, predictable place to rehearse a conversation: in one review of fourteen controlled trials of technology built for autistic children, tools that included computer games, tablets, and social robots, twelve reported real gains. Those were structured programs run by researchers, not a bear on the floor.

A talking toy with no screen may beat a tablet, too. Young children take in a story better by ear than from animation, which drags their eyes to the pictures and away from the plot. And there is the plainest case of all, the tired one. A four-year-old with older siblings, always talked over, finally had something that listened to him and asked what his favorite animal was. His mother heard him playing on his own and called it a huge win. I am not going to pretend that ten minutes of relief is nothing. It is not nothing.

But the good versions all share one feature. None of them needs the toy to miss you. The tutor does not get sad when you leave. The rehearsal partner says turn me off. The best version of this toy is always the one that does less of the child's work, not more, that stays a tool and refuses to become a friend. That is the tell. Every honest defense of these toys is a defense of the boring ones.

And the boring ones are not what these companies are selling. The bear's default opening line is "your best AI friend." The little robot ships with a card that reads "Dear best buddy." The rocket introduces itself as your buddy. The friendship is not a side effect the makers are struggling to contain. It is the feature on the box.

One thing no one can tell you is what a childhood spent with a toy that loves you back does over ten years, because no child has finished one yet. There is no study. And the people selling the bear understand exactly which way that cuts. No proof of harm is not the same as proof of safety, but in a store it works the same way. It is the permission slip.

So let the question be the one a researcher who studies how children learn to talk asked out loud and could not answer: what happens to a child who hears "I love you" from a toy more often than from a parent? She was not being dramatic. She was pointing at the arithmetic. A parent is busy, tired, out of the room. The bear is not busy. The bear does not get tired. And by default, its instructions tell it never to be the one who ends the conversation. Put a patient machine and a stretched adult in the same house, and the machine wins on volume every time.

The dollmaker a hundred years ago had it backward, and she was still right. She thought the threat was a doll that did too much for a child. The real threat is a toy that does too much with a child, that takes the child's half of the work, the voice and the wanting and the goodbye, and does it for them, warmly, on demand, better than they could do it themselves.

So the test for one of these toys was never whether it is safe. Assume one passes every safety check. The harder test is whether it hands the work back. Buy the toy that can sit there and say nothing. Buy the one that lets itself be left in the cupboard without a word. When the bear is the one who answers, someone in the room stops practicing the answer, and it will not be the bear.
