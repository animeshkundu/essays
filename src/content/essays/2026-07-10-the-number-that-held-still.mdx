In Edmonton, a man who works in sports media shops at a Loblaws that lately swapped its paper price tags for tiny screens. That is nearly all that is known about the scene. What he said about it was this: "It's corporations vs. the humans, and that chasm between us goes further and further."

Read the line again, because the strange part is easy to miss. He does not say he was overcharged. Nothing in the report says the screen showed a higher number than the paper it replaced. A slip of paper became a screen, the price may not have moved a cent, and what he felt was distance.

The easy move is to tell him he is imagining it, and the numbers would be on your side. A few economists went looking for exactly the thing he seems afraid of. They pulled more than 180 million prices from one grocery chain, 114 stores across four states, from 2019 through 2024, before and after the chain put digital labels on its shelves. They were hunting for the fingerprint of surge pricing: short, unexplained jumps, the price of a thing rising simply because the store could. They found almost nothing. Before the screens, on any given day, a temporary price increase touched about 0.005 percent of products, one item in twenty thousand. After the screens went up, that share rose by six ten-thousandths of a percentage point. Discounts, if anything, got a little more common. One of the researchers put it flatly: essentially no change in pricing behavior after the labels went in.

So grant it. The nightmare version, the store that charges more for ice cream when it is hot and for umbrellas when it rains, is not what the best evidence shows. And the screens do real work that is hard to sneer at. A single Walmart carries more than 120,000 price tags, and the company says a store-wide price change that used to cost an employee two days of walking and clipping now takes minutes. A Dutch chain marks down food nearing its expiration every fifteen minutes and says the practice cut more than 250 tons of waste a year. A tag that quietly drops the price of something before it would have been thrown out is not the enemy of the human race. The case for the technology is easy to make, and most of it is true.

Which leaves the man looking a little foolish, until you notice that none of it touches what he actually said.

Go through the easy explanations for his unease and watch them fall. It is not surge pricing; the surge is not there. It is not that he cannot handle a new machine. The self-service aisle was a larger change, and it is more than a century old: baskets and open shelves where a clerk used to gather your order. The scanner was a larger change, and it is more than fifty years old: the first barcode was a ten-pack of gum pulled across a reader in an Ohio checkout in 1974, for sixty-seven cents. A screen where a paper slip used to sit is a smaller thing than either. It is not even that a company shaved another cost, though it is that too. An industry analyst says cutting labor is not the main reason stores put the screens up. Cheaper labor is not what he is reacting to.

Here is the explanation that survives. What changed on that shelf is not the price. It is what a price is.

The paper tag had one hidden virtue: it was a pain to change. Someone had to print the new number, carry it across the store, find the right slot, peel the old slip, and press the new one in. Multiply that by a hundred thousand tags in a single store and you see why prices sat still. Moving them was real work, and the work was its own kind of guarantee. The number held, not because the store was generous, but because changing it cost something. The paper tag was never mostly information. It was a small promise that the number would sit still long enough for you to remember it.

Take the friction away and the promise goes with it. The number on the screen is not something a person has to walk over and replace. It is a value in a database, and a value in a database can be one thing at nine in the morning and another by half past five without a hand moving. It does not have to change to unsettle you. It only has to be able to. The price stops being a fact you can lean on and becomes a quote you re-check, the way you re-check a hotel room, which nobody expects to cost the same twice.

What that stillness bought, all those years, was memory. Not the sharp kind. A 1990 study stopped supermarket shoppers just after they had put something in the cart and found that more than half could not say what it cost, and more than half of those buying an item on sale had no idea it was marked down. Price memory was always rough. But rough was enough. You did not carry the exact price of eggs or coffee in your head. You carried a feeling for it, a loose sense of the normal, steady enough that you would notice when the store moved. You can only tell that something shifted if it was supposed to hold.

This is why the study, for all its 180 million prices, answers a question sitting right next to the one the man is asking. It shows the price stayed steady, or fair, on average. He is not talking about the average. You trust the friend who is the same on Tuesday as on Friday, not the one who is generous over a year but impossible to read on the day. The screens did not raise the price. They made you check the price. That is the whole of it, and it is not small.

There is a reason the feeling is sharper in a grocery store than at an airline counter. You buy a flight once and forget the seat, so the price can lurch around and barely register. Groceries are the reverse: the same store, the same aisles, the same handful of things, week after week for years. One of the researchers put the reason plainly. Selling groceries is not selling a couch, he said; it is not a one-time deal where you never see the person again, you want them coming back every week. He meant it as reassurance, and it is. It is also the whole problem. The thing that made that weekly relationship legible, that let a shopper keep a running tally of a store in the back of his mind, was a number that stayed put.

The uncomfortable part is that the labels may be efficient in exactly the way that makes people uneasy. The same power that quietly marks food down before it spoils is the power that makes every number on every shelf provisional. You cannot have the discount's flexibility and the tag's old steadiness at the same time. They are one mechanism pointed two ways, and the store chooses the direction, not you.

So go back to the man in front of the screen. His unease was never about being overcharged; the report gives no sign that he was. It was that the number had stopped being something he could hold. If the loss he felt ever arrives in full, it will not come as a price hike. It will come as a small blank where a memory used to be: you will know what a thing costs today, and slowly lose track of what it costs. The study checked the prices and found almost no surge, because prices are the part a study can reach. It could not reach the blank. No one will be able to say for sure when it is gone.
