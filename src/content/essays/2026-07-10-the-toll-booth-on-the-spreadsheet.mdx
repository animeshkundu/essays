At the end of the calendar month, someone at the bar picks up a clipboard and starts counting bottles by tenths. Not full bottles. Tenths. A bar pours from an open bottle, then marries what is left into the next one, so a shelf is never a clean row of ones and zeros. It is partials of this, tenths of that, a sliver of something expensive. The count goes onto paper. Then a walk to the office, and the numbers get typed into a spreadsheet someone built in 2014. Then the spreadsheet gets checked against what the point-of-sale system says was sold. It does not match. It never quite matches. Next month: the same walk, the same clipboard, the same spreadsheet, the same gap.

This year, a small group did something strange with that chore. They say they work behind bars themselves. Instead of renting the software that promises to close the gap, they built their own, wrote it with the help of AI, and gave it away. Free. Open source, under a license that lets anyone read it, change it, or sell it under the same terms. It runs offline on a laptop. You install it, open one browser window, and bookmark that window, because the bookmark is the software. On the launch page they did the math out loud: the bar trade has been sold $3,000 inventory guns, $200-a-month software, and consulting charged by the bottle. Then AI changed the price of building, so they built it.

Ignore, for a second, whether their app is any good. The interesting thing is the move itself, and the question under it. Why would the person stuck holding the clipboard do the software vendor's job for free?

Three easy answers come first. None of them hold.

The first is idealism: someone who loves free software scratched an itch. But operators building their own tools is old, and it never ended the subscription before. The spreadsheet was the first version of this promise. After 1979 a manager could open a grid and change the business logic without asking a programmer. Then desktop databases let a shop build its own forms, and a version of that has shipped since the mid-eighties. Free, downloadable accounting software for small businesses has existed since 2018. Idealism has been on the shelf the whole time. The bar kept paying anyway.

The second is that the rented software is bad. It is not. The companies in this market are good at what they do. One lets staff take inventory on a phone in the walk-in, syncing to a manager who reads the numbers from somewhere else. One connects straight to the big food distributors, so prices and invoices update themselves. They run variance reports that set theoretical usage next to actual usage and flag the bottle that is walking out the door. One of the largest restaurant platforms answers the phone every hour of every day of the year, and reaches about one in five American restaurants. The bar did not build a bypass because the toll road was broken. The toll road works.

The third is the one everyone reaches for: code got cheap. Half true, and the more interesting half is the part it gets wrong. The initial code was rarely the whole expensive part. Ask someone who sells this kind of software, and they will tell you the program is not where most of the cost lived. What you are really buying is stability, support, maintenance, and someone to call when it breaks at closing. If cheap code were the whole story, the bypass would not matter, because the expensive parts would still be for sale.

So rule those out and see what is left standing. The subscription, for this exact kind of workflow, was never only selling the code, and it was never only selling the support. It was also selling the distance between two people: the one who understood the workflow, and the one who could write software. For decades those were almost never the same person. The bartender knew why bottles get counted in tenths and could not ship a program. The engineer could ship a program and had never married a bottle. The vendor stood in the gap between them and collected rent on the crossing.

<PullQuote>
That is the thing AI actually moved. Not the cost of code. The width of the gap.
</PullQuote>

A person close to the workflow can now, sometimes, describe that count to a machine and get back something that runs. Still not an engineer. Does not need to be. The first crossing just got cheaper, and the gap got narrow enough to step over.

Name the mechanism so you can test it, because it does not apply everywhere. Call it the toll booth. A tool is a toll booth when five things are true. The customer knows their own version of the job, its oddities and its failure points, better than a vendor watching from a distance can. The data is simple enough to hold in a file you can open and read. The job is mostly one person, in mostly one place. The links to other systems are optional, shallow, or a spreadsheet you export. And the cost of the thing breaking for an afternoon is low enough that the operator can carry the fix instead of paying a vendor to be on call. When all five hold, the thing you were renting was closer to a spreadsheet with a toll booth than to a real business. And the booth is starting to lose the one thing that let it charge: being the only one who could build the road.

This is where the smart money is watching the wrong door. The obvious story about AI and software is that it comes for the programmers, that the expensive people in the building are the ones exposed. Maybe. But the first market it quietly reprices may not be labor at all. It may be the long tail of narrow subscriptions sold into jobs that were never worth a company: one chore, one location, a couple hundred dollars a month, times every bar and salon and repair shop that runs the chore. The smallest one-chore versions of these tools looked too narrow to be worth defending at venture scale. That is exactly why they are soft.

Run the test and watch it sort the market.

The bar count can pass the test, but only in a narrow shape. One site. One person walking one physical route. A point-of-sale export that drops in as a file. Downtime not so costly that it demands a vendor on call. Where it passes, the reason is not that the operator is smarter than the vendor. It is that the operator knows the odd shelves, the married bottles, the export that comes out wrong, and the exact place the count fails, better than anyone reading the numbers from a screen somewhere else. The data is a list of bottles and levels. It is a file you can open and read. When all of that holds, a much lower clearing price becomes visible, and the rented version has to answer for the gap. Two inventory tools built for roughly this job sit right around two hundred dollars a month, one at $199 and one at $249, which is $2,400 to $3,000 a year before setup fees, taxes, or staff time. Set against a bottle list on a laptop, that number now has to justify itself every month, out loud, to a person who can open the whole program and read it.

Now move one door down, to the full restaurant back office, and watch the test fail. The count is not one person in one room. It is staff moving through storerooms and walk-ins with phones while a manager reads the results from home. The value is the wiring. A broader restaurant-management platform charges $350 per location, which is $4,200 a year for one restaurant and $42,000 across ten, and for that the buyer gets invoices that post themselves, point-of-sale integrations, sales data folded into a daily profit-and-loss with labor in it, and the bills paid on time. One tier over in the same rented stack, other tools wire straight to the big distributors and drop clean into the accountant's software. Break the wiring for an afternoon and the day's numbers are wrong. Here the rent is not a toll. It is insurance, and someone is accountable when it fails at two in the morning. This is not the market AI empties. This is the market that finally has to prove it was ever more than the booth.

Then the near miss, which is the part the free-software story likes to skip. Getting out of the subscription does not get you out of the work. It moves the work onto you. The people who study this call it local-first, and they are blunt about the bill: when the only real copy of your data lives on your machine, you are now the backup department, the security department, and the disaster-recovery department. The escape tools show it plainly. One popular self-hosted invoicing app warns that if you lose a single encryption key, the program will not run again, and it carries hundreds of open issues. A local accounting app that keeps everything in one file on your computer carries a couple hundred more. And the bar app, the one that started this: three stars, a handful of open issues, an installer the operating system flags as untrusted, a mobile story that barely exists, and skeptics on the forum saying it is dead on arrival without a tablet, that it looks like an opinionated front end for a spreadsheet, that a slick landing page proves nothing now that anyone can generate one in an afternoon. No measured evidence yet shows a single bar running a full inventory cycle on it in real use. Launch attention is not a customer. That is the honest state of the win.

So the toll did not vanish. For the narrowest slice of the market, a much cheaper clearing price is now visible, and the rent has to argue against something close to free. For another slice it turned into something the buyer now has to do alone, which is a different bill, not no bill. Keep the line, with one caveat: most subscriptions sell a little of both, insurance and toll, and AI is quietly making every small software company show which half it was really charging for.

Which brings it back to the person with the clipboard, who can now do what used to be out of reach. Build the tool they were renting. Hand it to the bar down the street. Keep the $200 a month. Whether they can still do it in the second year is the open part, the part nobody has earned the right to answer. Software rots. Operating systems change. The invoice photo still wants a cloud model to read it. And when it breaks on a Friday close, there is no number to call, because the number is the person who built it. They crossed the gap once. Staying on the far side is a different kind of work, and now they own it.

The vendors will tell them they will be back, and some of the vendors are right. The ones who should be nervous are the ones who cannot finish the sentence that used to be their whole business: we were worth the rent because ______. For years the blank did not matter, because the person paying could not have built the thing anyway. Now they can. The blank is the whole business, and now they have to fill it in.
