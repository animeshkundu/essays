A ball python had not eaten in fourteen months when the biologist who kept her set a rat in front of her; she struck, constricted it completely, and swallowed it whole, having lost very little muscle tone in all that time. In organ donors whose breathing had been done by machine for between eighteen and sixty-nine hours, the diaphragm's muscle fibers were less than half the cross-section of those in patients ventilated only two or three hours. After a meal, one molecule in a python's blood climbs more than a thousandfold, the single biggest spike in its plasma. Give it to obese male mice and they eat less and lose weight. A startup is chasing an appetite pill from the same biology, though every human step past the mouse is still unproven. We assume the animal with the most to teach us about our own bodies is the one most like us, a mouse we can engineer; sometimes it is the one least like us, the body that runs at full volume what ours runs too quietly to read. The distance was never the obstacle. It is the instrument.
