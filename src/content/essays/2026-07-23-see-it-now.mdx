There is a film I have wanted for years. It was never shot. This year a company made one anyway, and I have watched it again and again without deciding whether I am grateful.

On August 2, 1959, at a cramped little ground on Rua Javari, in the Mooca district of São Paulo, an eighteen-year-old forward took a pass. He was already a world champion, already something close to the best player alive, and the home crowd, whose team he was beating, had spent the afternoon on his back. What he did next took about five seconds. He lifted the ball over one defender, then over a second, then over the goalkeeper, and put his head to it and scored, and in all of it the ball never once touched the grass. No camera was there. The goal he called the most beautiful of his career was never filmed. The ball went up and, in a sense, it never came down. It stayed in the air of other people's telling.

He called it the best goal of his life. His daughter says he told her, more than once, that it was a shame no one filmed it. There is a single photograph, one frozen instant of a play that moved. Everything else lived in mouths. A reporter who was there wrote that some goals you carry home printed on your eyes, and that watching this one was like staring at a giraffe and thinking, that cannot exist. I have read those lines many times. They made me want the film that was never shot. So when I tell you a company rebuilt the goal this year, you can probably feel the pull yourself, before I have told you a single thing about how. I felt it. I still feel it.

Here is how you build a film of a thing no one filmed. A historian and her team gathered close to two thousand records: stadium blueprints, family albums, newspaper reports, more than three thousand old photographs. They sat the surviving witnesses down in front of a scale model of the stadium and a stack of pictures and let them push their memories back into a real space. They put living bodies on the actual grass at Rua Javari, in period kit, kicking a heavy leather ball, some of the best footballers of today standing in as doubles. Then the machine went to work. It mapped the dead man's face and his number 10 onto a stunt player. It turned the modern stadium back into a cloudy afternoon in 1959. It generated the crowd, and the people who followed the match at home on the radio. Last, they ran the whole thing through a film printer so it came out grainy, the way 1950s newsreel came out grainy. They never called it footage. They called it a reconstruction, which is the honest word. It is going to hang in a free museum in the city where he became famous.

This is not the first time someone gave the goal a picture. A documentary about his life rebuilt it with computer graphics back in 2004. But that version was shown as what it was, a drawing set inside a film, and a drawing does not fight a memory. You set it next to the one in your head and leave your own in charge. This year's version is not shown as a drawing. It is dressed as film shot that afternoon, down to the grain, and that changes what it does to you.

I want to be fair to this, because the case for it is not stupid and I am not immune to it. The people who made it say that even his daughter knows her father in the recreation. She has said he always regretted that the goal was never filmed, and that being able to relive it was wonderful. Its makers said they were using the tools "not to reinvent the past, but to help the world experience a real moment that was never captured on film," and that sentence is doing honest work. A witness's account in a 1959 sports page reaches far fewer people now. This reaches far more people, and reaches them in the body, the way the page never could. If that were the whole of it, I would have nothing to say, and I would just be grateful, and I would send it to my father.

And yet something in me will not settle, and I have spent a while trying to talk myself out of it, because the obvious objections all fail. It is not that the film lies. It asserts nothing false; the man existed, the goal happened, the label is honest. It is not that people will take it for real footage. One reviewer said that when the goal finally plays it looks a little more like a video game than life. But even if every viewer reads the label and knows exactly what it is, the feeling still does its work, the way you can still tear up at a photograph you know was posed. It is not even bad taste, because his daughter found it wonderful, and who am I. Each of those is a real worry and none of them is mine. What is left, when I clear them away, is smaller and harder to say.

Watch what the reconstruction actually did to the goal. For more than sixty years the goal was not one thing. It was an argument. Some who saw it swore it was three lifts of the ball; some said four; one account split the difference and called it three and a half. The ground held about ten thousand people that day, and by the time anyone thought to count, nearly a hundred thousand claimed to have been there. It stayed contested. It grew in the telling. That argument was the truest record it had. And a reconstruction cannot hold an argument. It has to choose. It chooses three lifts, one weather, one crowd, one exact path for the ball, and it renders the choice so finely that it looks developed in a darkroom in 1959. The machine did not lie to me. It did something I have less defense against. It took a true uncertainty and answered it, beautifully, and made the answer look like a memory.

This is what I have come to believe, and you are welcome to think I am wrong. Longing is not evidence. The most dangerous history is not the kind that lies to you. It is the kind so lovely, and so much what you wanted, that it quietly teaches you to treat the wanting as if it were a witness.

There is a reason this works on us, and it is not that we are fools. In a study published this year, people were shown old photographs that a program had nudged into small motion: a blink, the start of a smile, eight seconds of it. When the picture felt authentic, the moving version scored about six out of seven for emotional force, against about five for the still one. And it was that force, not any measure of accuracy, that predicted whether people passed the image on. Feeling is the part that travels. Underneath sits an older, colder finding. Memory holds images with startling fidelity but is careless about where they came from; researchers call the gap source monitoring. You keep the scene and you lose the tag. A year from now I will still be able to see that goal: the lifts, the grain, the gray sky the film chose. What I will have misplaced, the way everyone misplaces it, is the small word stuck to it: reconstruction. The picture stays. The caveat rots off.

And this is a goal. A game. Nobody is hurt if a football match gets a picture it never earned. So follow the same wish up the slope. A phone will now bring a still photo to life in a few seconds: a blink, a turn of the head, the start of a smile. One person, watching a long-dead father blink, said their heart dropped to the floor every time. A museum brought a dead painter back onto a screen to take a selfie with visitors on the way out. A documentary gave a dead cook a voice he never recorded and had it speak three lines no tape ever caught, one of them out of a private email to a friend: I am wondering, are you happy. The director said, plainly, that you would not be able to tell which lines were real, and the man's widow said she had not known it was done. Keep going, all the way up. A family is handed images of a dead soldier, and a daughter has to look at one and say: that is not my father.

These are not the same act. One asks the family first and one never does; one stops at what the record holds and one paints straight past it. Those differences are not small. They are most of what is right and wrong in the whole business. But something does not change as you climb. Most of them arrived as feeling a half second before they arrived as fact, and every one counted on you not to mind the order.

I have to grant that it can be done well, or the argument is too cheap. There is a project that lets you sit and ask questions of recorded survivors of a genocide and hear them answer, and nearly all of its enormous care goes into one thing: making sure the machine never puts a single word in a witness's mouth that she did not say. A woman sat for a week under more than a hundred cameras, answering a thousand questions, so that when she could no longer answer in person, a stranger could still ask and hear her own true answer. When a reporter asked her projection something no one had thought to record, it did not invent a reply. It said, more or less: that is a very good question, and I am sorry, I was never asked it. That refusal is the whole difference. It marks the edge of what is known and stops there. It spends the feeling to walk you back to the witness. The goal film has a great deal behind it: nearly two thousand records, a real pitch, a heavy ball, a family's blessing, a historian and her team who gathered every fragment they could find. What it does not have, behind its most persuasive frames, is a camera. And it spends the feeling to walk you away from that missing camera, not back toward it.

So I go back to the ground on Rua Javari. For more than sixty years the ball went up and never came down, because no film ever caught it landing, and the one drawn version never made me stop seeing my own. Now it comes down. It comes down in grain, on a loop, in a museum, and I have watched it come down, and here is the part I did not expect. I can no longer picture the goal any other way. Before, reading that it took five seconds and the crowd went quiet and a beaten defender ended up under his own net after trying to clear it, I built something dim and shifting and mine. Now their version lies on top of mine and will not lift off. When I reach for the goal I get their weather, their crowd, their three lifts, their choice. The reconstruction did not take the truth from me; the truth was gone long before I was born. It took the last thing that stood in for the truth, which was my not knowing.

There is one more thing the record keeps. After he scored, he turned to the crowd that had jeered him all afternoon and punched the air. He said, years later, that the gesture meant something like: see it now? He had done a thing people did not want to believe, in front of people who did not want to believe it, and he was daring them to admit they had seen it with their own eyes. More than sixty years on, a machine turns to the rest of us and answers him. See it now. And we do. We see it now, in fine gray grain, in a version good enough to keep, a thing that reached us as feeling before it reached us as fact, a thing we wanted so badly that we let the wanting stand in for the camera that was never there. I keep thinking about the ten thousand who were there, and the hundred thousand who say they were. That gap used to be the goal's own way of staying alive: too loved to hold still, swelling every time it was told, never once agreeing with itself. The film does not swell. It plays the same way every time, and it has already chosen, for all of us, what the goal looked like. Some of those hundred thousand were lying, and some had only heard it told so well that they could see it. Now we can all see it. I do not know what to do with a machine that closes the distance between those two, and hands the liar and the witness the very same picture.
