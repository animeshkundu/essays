In July 2023 a professional surfer posted screenshots she said were texts from her ex. One was a list, headed "Plain and simple: If you need," and under it a set of things: surfing with men, modeling, a photo of herself in a swimsuit, some of her friendships. If she needed them, the message said, he was not the right partner. The message called these boundaries.

A boundary, several therapists said, is a limit you set on yourself, not one you hand to someone else. But scroll the same thread and she is reaching for the same words back at him, writing that she felt "gaslit," getting the reply "You have not been gaslit." Two people, one vocabulary, each holding it like a verdict. This is what a clinical word can do once it leaves the room it was built for. It stops opening a question and starts closing one, filing the other person under unwell or unsafe, so that before they can answer they first have to prove they are not what they were just called.

She kept the screenshots, she wrote, "in a hidden folder for a reference for myself, when I was feeling like I was losing my mind." The same word that lets you finally name your own reality is the one that lets someone else paint over it: the same word, in the same hand, spelled the same way. From the inside, you cannot always tell which way yours is pointing.
