A 26-year-old in Cambridge has logged 850 films. Her father has one rule. He will not watch anything rated below 3.5.

She is the one who got him onto it. She has kept the diary for about four years, and she is on it, she says, more than almost anything else; it is her main way of tracking what might be worth seeing. A low rating does not stop her. Her father is stricter. Below 3.5 out of five, he says no. That is the border, and it holds.

She still goes to the cinema as much as she can, partly because her local one is falling apart around her. A lot of the seats are broken. A whole screen sits out of action. An obvious lack of investment, she says, and it worries her, so she keeps buying tickets. Hold the two pictures side by side: the daughter in the failing dark room, keeping it alive by showing up, and the father who will not begin a film until a number clears his line. Both are true. Both are where she lives.

The 3.5 is not his. It belongs to strangers. It is the community average shown under the poster, built from everyone who tapped a half-star to five-star rating into a movie diary called Letterboxd, and it is there before he has seen a single frame. There is a small coincidence tucked inside it: 3.5 out of five is the exact line Rotten Tomatoes draws to call an audience score positive. The father's private cutoff lands on an industry threshold.

That number is for sale.

The company that keeps it was bought in 2023 by a holding firm, which took 60 percent at a valuation of $50 to $60 million. Three years later it is being shopped again, and the figure floated in the reports is $250 million. Call it four to five times the earlier price, in about three years. The reported audience grew in that stretch, though nobody agrees how much: the Guardian put it above 26 million users, Variety above 30 million members as of June 2026. That growth is real. It is not, by itself, enough to explain the jump.

The rest of the explanation is sitting around the table. The reported suitors are not ad networks. Some of them make films and decide which ones you are shown: Netflix, Sony Pictures, Paramount. The others are there for colder reasons: a large private-equity firm, a media spinoff, a Reddit co-founder. It is the first group that makes the price legible. Why would a company that already makes movies want a diary of what strangers thought of movies?

Ask most people what could go wrong if one of them bought it, and they name the obvious thing: ads. Banners between the reviews, a paywall on your own diary, the slow spoiling of one more good thing. That fear is real and it is the wrong one. Nobody floats a quarter of a billion dollars for the right to sell banners to film nerds. Ad space is not why a studio would be in the room. Something else is.

Think about what the app actually holds. Not opinions, exactly. Structured ones. On any film page you tap that you have seen it, that you liked it, a rating, a place on your watchlist, a log with the date, a spot in a list, and a tag for how or where or with whom you watched it. One of its own example tags is with:dad. Every one of those taps is two things at once. It is a private memory. It is also a market signal. Tens of millions of people have spent years telling the truth about what they watched and how they felt about it, for free, because it felt like keeping a diary. The diary is the asset.

It did not start as an asset. It started in 2011 in Auckland, built by two web designers as a side project, and one of them described the idea plainly: a social film diary, perhaps with lists. He had grown up carrying rented tapes home in a backpack on a BMX, running horror marathons at a friend's house, waiting on copies of a British film magazine that reached New Zealand six to nine months after the films opened, if they arrived at all. Scarcity was the water he swam in. What he built was a cure for forgetting: a place to record what you saw and what it meant, so it would not slip away. For years that is what it was.

Then, quietly, it turned. In December 2025 the company opened a rental store inside the app. The shelves were not stocked by a studio. They were programmed from the users: millions of watchlists, reviews, and behavioral signals, read for demand. One shelf was called Unreleased Gems. The chief executive framed it to the industry without flinching. Members, he said, reveal what is really happening, an old action film suddenly trending, a festival title from two years back still landing on watchlists, and the store lets the company act on it. His phrase for it: harness this interest to get films to the people who want them most.

Harness. The diary of what films meant had become a map of what people could be moved to press next. Nobody had to sell the company for that to happen. It happened on its own, because the data was sitting there and it was too useful to leave alone.

A record built to remember what movies meant to people becomes valuable as an instrument for deciding what those people watch next. A diary of private taste, floated at a quarter of a billion dollars: the clearest reason is that the taste can be read forward, into what gets made, rented, surfaced, and sold. It is why the buyers circling it are studios and streamers and not ad men.

The father who will not start a film below 3.5 is not a warning about what a new owner might someday do to the app. He is a small working example of what the app already does. One man has handed a piece of his evenings to the average opinion of strangers. He is not the reason for the price; he is one visible instance of it. Multiply that instance by the millions who trust the number, and you have the thing the buyers actually want. In one internal survey, more than 63 percent of users said they trusted the app's average rating more than any other rating metric on the internet. That trust is the product. And it is not one stubborn father: another user watches her partner see a 2.1 and refuse outright, the number closing the film before it opens.

Now set an owner on top of that trust. Not one that fakes the number; it would not need to. The number sits in the middle of an apparatus that a buyer would also own: which films fill the rows, which titles land on the store's shelves, what you see first when you open the app looking for something to watch. This is not a paranoid hypothetical; the arrangement already exists elsewhere. Rotten Tomatoes has belonged to a major media company for years. IMDb is owned by Amazon, which also sells and streams the films it lists. The book lovers' version of this app, a place people built to remember what they read, was bought by Amazon in 2013, and the reviews stayed, which was the point: the archive of taste was the thing worth having. Nobody needs to touch a score. You only need to decide which titles sit in the row, which get surfaced first, which forgotten film gets rescued and which stays shelved. The wheel does not have to be yanked. A hand resting on it is enough.

The honest counterargument is stronger than the ad panic, and it deserves saying. Capital is not the same as corruption. This app has outgrown the small-tool phase; a service with tens of millions of members that wants to stay mostly free needs real money for servers, moderation, clean film data, more countries. The firm that bought it in 2023 made, by most accounts, few noticeable changes in three years. The founders still own 40 percent, and the company says any decision about its future would involve them. And the rental store cuts both ways: one of its first titles was a thriller that had sat unavailable anywhere since it premiered at Cannes in 2023, and the store brought it back within reach. If member demand drags a buried film back into daylight, the diary is not being strip-mined. It is being read the way its users hoped. The right buyer, bound by the right rules, could fund the dull, necessary work and protect the thing that makes it worth protecting.

That is all true. And it points at the same uncomfortable fact from the other side. Every good version of the sale depends on the buyer using the taste graph only under rules that protect the people who built it: no thumb on the scale for its own films, no quiet reshuffling of what reaches whom. The whole case for the sale being fine is a case for restraint that no contract in the reports requires. None of the suitors has promised, in public, to keep a wall between owning the films and running the surfaces that decide which films you meet.

The deeper trouble is that the shift everyone is watching for does not need a villain, or even a sale. No one sat the father down and told him to let a crowd of strangers weigh in on his evenings. The rule is just there now, reported as his: below 3.5, he passes. Be careful with what that proves. One rating entering one household's choice is not evidence that an app rewired a man's whole relationship to film. It is smaller and stranger than that. It is one number arriving in the room before the movie does, and a person letting it decide.

There is the dark room she is trying to keep alive by showing up, broken seats and all. And there is the app that might, if the owner is patient, drag a lost film back onto a screen somewhere, and might, if the owner is not, quietly narrow which films she and her father are ever offered the chance to want.

Both futures run through the same 3.5. It is her diary and it is somebody's asset. For now it is still just a number under a poster, and it still has to clear the father's line before he will watch. The question was never whether the app gets ads. Nobody has to touch the number he trusts. The question is who gets to decide where it appears, what sits beside it, and which films are allowed to reach him before it does.
