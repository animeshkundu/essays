Somewhere near Kyoto, a tea bush is sitting under a tent, in the dark, on purpose. It has been shaded for weeks so it will make less of the bitter compounds and more of the sweet ones. When the best leaves come off, they are steamed almost at once, dried flat, stripped of every stem and vein, then fed into a stone mill that turns so slowly it takes about an hour to grind 40 grams of powder. Forty grams is maybe a dozen drinks. One mill, one hour, a dozen cups. The mill crawls on purpose too: push it faster and the friction scorches the powder and kills the color.

That is the slowest, finest version of the stuff. Most of what you drink is not that. But the whole boom is dressed in its clothes. The word on the chalkboard is "ceremonial." The color is the color of the shaded leaf. And then someone drops a few grams of green powder into a cup, pours ice and milk over it, spoons strawberry puree on top, photographs it, and finishes it before the light changes.

A drink whose finest form is built, at every single step, to make you wait has become the perfect fuel for people who won't. The warm reading of this, the one you'll hear at the counter, is that after years of speed we are finally relearning ritual, buying ourselves a small daily ceremony, slowing down. I don't believe it. The boom shows the opposite. We did not relearn the pause. We kept its image: the color, the calm-sounding words, the photograph. We let go of the waiting. In the ritual it borrows from, the waiting was never decorative.

The cheapest explanation is that this is a fad, a green cousin of the cronut, here until the feed moves on. It isn't. Fads don't reorder supply chains. Japan's matcha output went from 1,430 tonnes in 2012 to 4,176 in 2023, and more than half now ships abroad. At the opening auction for the leaf that becomes Uji matcha, the price hit 8,235 yen a kilo in May 2025, about 1.7 times the year before and past a record that had stood since 2016. A farmer whose family has grown tea for six generations pulled 1.5 tons last year instead of his usual two, because the summer had cooked the bushes. New fields take at least five years to come in, so this season's demand cannot be answered this season. A buyer who mills leaf on the other side of the world says out loud that he expects prices to climb harder still. You don't get any of that from a fad. You get it from a habit that has set like concrete.

The next explanation is that it's just caffeine, the ordinary reason anyone drinks anything at eight in the morning. But the numbers won't cooperate. An eight-ounce matcha carries somewhere between 38 and 89 milligrams of caffeine. The same size coffee carries 100 to 120, and it waits on nearly every corner. Yet in Paris people line up before the shops open, and in London a good matcha latte runs six to eight pounds. Either way, they are choosing the smaller dose. If you were optimizing for the jolt, that is a strange trade. So caffeine alone doesn't explain it. Here I owe you the honest limit: I don't have a clean survey of why people buy the green cup, so I can't hand you the average drinker's motive. But I can read what the marketing sells, and it is not the stimulant. It is a feeling about the stimulant: that this one is the virtuous kind, calm instead of jittery, a wellness choice you can hold up to a lens. The words come back to you verbatim, as calm, focused, all-day energy. It is fuel that photographs as self-care.

The third explanation is the strong one, and I want to give it its full weight, because most of it is true. It says this is simply how living things survive. Food cultures do not last by sitting still under glass. They travel, they get picked up by people who didn't grow up with them, and that adoption is precisely what keeps them from dying. This isn't some Western raid on a sacred object, either. A tea house in Kyoto that opened its doors in 1717 publishes its own recipes for iced matcha and matcha lattes; one tells you to shake a single gram in a flask of ice water and calls it casual, quick, and easy. The tradition's own teachers say plainly that every generation has looked for a version of tea suited to its own era. And the alternative has a real cost: tea farming in Japan has been shrinking for years, the young increasingly file it under their grandparents' tastes, and a wave of foreign demand can help support the growers who still raise a roof of poles and mesh over their fields to shade the leaves. Some of the new drinkers do go deeper. They buy the whisk, learn the sifting, and find that making it properly forces them to stop and stand still in the middle of a loud day. For them the sweet green cup wasn't the end of anything. It was training wheels.

All of that is real. Hold onto it. Now look at what the market actually makes easy.

The easiest matcha to buy in the boom, the one on the permanent menu, the one handed across the counter in minutes, is not the stillness. It is the nine-fifty cup in Los Angeles with milk and a butterfly-pea cream top. It is the New York menu that files "ceremonial grade" powder (a grade no Japanese authority actually defines) under names like Earth Glow and stirs in blue spirulina and taro for the color. It is cream, syrup, fruit puree, and a photograph taken before the first sip. The slow version still exists. It is a tasting counter with six seats that you have to already know about to find.

<PullQuote>
The market didn't outlaw the pause. It made the pause the thing you have to seek out, and the pose the thing that finds you.
</PullQuote>

Here is the line I'll defend. Adaptation is healthy when it pulls people toward the thing being adapted, and it curdles into performance when it lets them keep the image and skip the thing entirely. The boom isn't hollow because some people use it well. It's revealing because using it well is now the effortful path, a whisk and a wait and a counter you have to already know about, while using it as a prop is the most visible version, the one handed over in minutes. That is the whole asymmetry: one path has friction and hides, the other is frictionless and easy to find. You don't have to know what any one person wants to see which door is easier to walk through. We tend to walk through the easy one. We reach for our phones.

And matcha is not special. That is the part that should keep nagging at you after you've put this down. Matcha is only where the contradiction happens to be legible this year, because the making of it is so visibly, almost comically slow that the speed of the drinking sticks out against it. The same move is everywhere once your eye is trained. We took sitting quietly and turned it into a streak counter. We took the walk and turned it into a step count worth posting. We took reading and turned it into a shelf of spines. The structure is identical every time: find a practice whose entire value is that it slows you down and makes you attend to one thing, keep its surface intact, and quietly optimize away the slowing down. The pause becomes a metric. The ritual becomes content.

I am not standing outside this. I feel the pull too. That's exactly why it's worth noticing instead of scolding: the pull is real, and reaching for the camera before the cup is the easy, almost automatic move.

The ceremony this drink comes from turns on one small, strange idea: that the exact meeting in front of you will never happen again in quite this way, so what it asks of you is your attention, right now, before it's gone. The photograph reaches the other way. It says don't just be here, hold onto this, so it lasts, so it travels, so it can be seen. Most of the time you can only really do one of them. So the next time you're standing over a bright green cup that borrowed its whole look from a bush that spent weeks in the dark and, somewhere upstream, a stone mill that can take an hour to grind 40 grams, you get to pick which one you're actually having. Watch which hand moves first.
