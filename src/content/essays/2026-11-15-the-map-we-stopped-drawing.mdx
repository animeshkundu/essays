I learned a city the way a cab driver learns one, badly and on a scooter. I was twenty-two, new to London, and I had a moped with L-plates and a route sheet I could not read while moving. So I called the turns out loud before I took them, the way a student calls them to an examiner, and the city came in through my ears and my hands and the rain on my visor. By the end of that first month I could get from my flat to the college without thinking, and by the end of the second I could get there by three different routes depending on the weather and the traffic. I did not have a map. I had something better: a feeling for the shape of the place, the way the streets leaned toward the river, the way one road always felt longer than it was. That feeling is what I mean by a cognitive map, and it is the thing I am worried we are losing.

A cognitive map is not a picture you carry in your head. It is a working sense of where things are in relation to each other, the kind that lets you point toward home with your eyes closed, or take a detour when a road is closed without starting over. The psychologist Edward Tolman gave it the name in 1948, after watching rats find food in mazes and noticing they could take a shortcut they had never been taught. They were not following a memorized sequence of lefts and rights. They were navigating from something like an internal model of the space. Later work found the machinery: place cells in the hippocampus, which fire when an animal is in a particular spot, and grid cells in the entorhinal cortex, which lay down a repeating coordinate system. The map is not metaphor. It is a physical structure in the brain, and it is built by moving through a place, paying attention, and getting lost a few times.

Turn-by-turn navigation gives you the next turn, and only the next turn. It does not ask you to hold the whole route in your head, or to notice the landmarks you pass, or to build a model of where you are. A person can follow turn after turn and still not know where they are. Psychologists call this the difference between route knowledge and survey knowledge. Route knowledge is a chain of instructions: left at the church, right at the roundabout. Survey knowledge is a picture of the whole area, the kind that lets you improvise. Active navigation, where you work it out yourself, builds survey knowledge. Passive navigation, where you follow directions, builds route knowledge at best, and sometimes nothing at all. The phone is not a map. It is a set of instructions, and the difference matters more than it looks like it should.

<Aside>
I am aware that "digital amnesia" is a marketing term, not a diagnosis, and that no one has measured a whole generation's spatial memory against a baseline from before smartphones. I am also aware that the numbers I am about to use are commonly cited and not always verified. The count of streets in London, the years of training, the size of the hippocampus in a taxi driver: these come from studies and reporting I have read, and where the evidence is thin I will say so. What I am not aware of is any reason to think the opposite is true, that following a blue dot for a decade leaves the map-making part of the mind untouched. The burden of proof, it seems to me, should sit with the people who want to replace a human capacity with a device.
</Aside>

The evidence that moving through space changes the brain is old and strong. In 1971 John O'Keefe found place cells in rats. In 2005 the Mosers found grid cells. In 2000 Eleanor Maguire and colleagues scanned the brains of London taxi drivers and found that the back of the hippocampus was larger in drivers than in controls, and that the size correlated with years on the job. In 2011 she and Katherine Woollett went further and scanned people before and during training for The Knowledge, and found the hippocampus changing as they learned. The brain is plastic in the specific, mechanical way that means practice reshapes it. The question is not whether the map-making machinery exists. It is whether we are still using it.

The question of whether GPS use shrinks the hippocampus is a different one, and I should be careful with it. A 2020 study by Louisa Dahmani and Veronique Bohbot found that heavier habitual GPS use was associated with worse spatial memory and with less hippocampal activity during navigation tasks. That is an association, not a demonstration. It does not tell us which way the arrow points. Maybe people with weaker spatial memory lean on GPS more. Maybe both are driven by something else, like how much a person walks. The honest thing to say is that the causal claim has not been made, and that the mechanism, turn-by-turn navigation bypassing the map-building process, is at least plausible. I am not going to pretend the science is further along than it is. I am also not going to pretend it is irrelevant.

Before I argue for keeping the map, let me argue for losing it properly. Navigation apps are a genuine accessibility tool. For a blind person, or a person with dementia, or anyone whose spatial memory has been damaged by illness or injury, turn-by-turn directions are not a convenience. They are independence. For a driver in an unfamiliar city at night, they are safety, in the sense that a person looking at a phone is less likely to be looking at the road, and a voice that says "in two hundred meters, turn left" lets them keep their eyes up. The safety benefit is reported rather than measured in the sources I have read, but it is plausible and I would not want to argue against it. The efficiency argument is real too: route optimization reduces miles driven, which matters for fuel and for emissions, and the induced-demand literature suggests that making driving easier does not simply add up to more driving in a simple way. And there is the plain argument from cognitive economy. Human memory is finite. Offloading the route to a device frees attention for other things, and we offload constantly: calculators, calendars, the written word itself. Clark and Chalmers made this case in 1998 under the name "the extended mind," and it is a serious argument, not a lazy one. If a tool extends my mind without shrinking it, I should use the tool.

Each of these points has a scope, and the scope is where my disagreement lives. Accessibility is real, but it is not an argument for habitual use by people with intact spatial memory. The safety case for voice guidance is strong and I accept it; the safety case for following a blue dot into a lake is the automation-complacency problem, where a system that usually works trains its user to stop checking. Efficiency is real, but a shorter route is not a longer life, and the time saved is minutes, not years. Cognitive economy is the strongest of the lot, and I take it seriously. But spatial memory is not only a stored route. It is the substrate of a kind of knowing: where you are, how places connect, what a neighborhood is. Offloading a phone number costs you a phone number. Offloading the map costs you the city. The question is whether the extended mind can be extended this far without the original mind shrinking, and the honest answer is that nobody knows yet. My leaning, and it is a leaning, is that a part of the mind is not a thing to trade for minutes unless you have a reason, and convenience is not yet a reason. It is a habit.

London's black-cab drivers still have to pass The Knowledge, and the shape of it tells you what a human map actually costs. About twenty-five thousand streets. Around three hundred and twenty standard routes, called runs. Roughly twenty thousand points of interest. Two to four years of study, most of it done on a scooter, reciting turns aloud into the rain. The exam is oral: an examiner names two points anywhere in London and the candidate has to produce a route, and the examiner can change the destination mid-route. There is no written test, because the point is not what you can look up. It is what you can do under pressure, in your head, in real time. The cohort of licensed drivers has fallen from roughly twenty-five thousand to roughly fifteen thousand over the past decade, and GPS is the underlying infrastructure of that change, even if it is not the whole cause. I am not holding up the cabbie as a standard anyone should meet. Most of us will never memorize twenty-five thousand streets, and we do not need to. The point is narrower: a capacity that took years to build and a working lifetime to maintain is being quietly replaced by a device, and the replacement is not being weighed, only adopted.

I do not have a clean ending for this, because the question is open. The map we stopped drawing is not only a cognitive map. It is the map of a place we actually know, walked and weathered and held in the body, as opposed to a place we pass through while a voice tells us where to turn. The examiner in the black-cab test does not ask whether you can find the destination. He asks whether you can find it when the destination changes. That is the test the rest of us are failing without knowing it: not can we get there, but can we get there another way, can we find our way home when the phone is dead, can we say what the next street is called without looking. I do not know whether a city you cannot draw from memory is still, in any sense, yours. I think it might not be. But I think the question is worth carrying, and I would rather leave it open than close it with an answer I cannot support.
