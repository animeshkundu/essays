A software development manager met each of his seven people once a week. Then the meetings moved to every other week. In the empty week the work did not stop, and neither did the reporting. His software agent talked to each of their software agents, gathered what they were doing, and passed feedback back down the line. Nobody had to book a room or find a time. The updates arrived on their own, tidy and on schedule, in the gap where a conversation used to be.

He said it seemed to work.

Not a breakdown. Not the tool failing in some obvious way. A manager watching a system run smoothly, the reports flowing, the boxes filling in, and deciding to leave it anyway. He had joined the company in June of 2025 and walked out at the end of the following April. When he described what he had been part of, he did not reach for the words layoff or efficiency. He called it a drug trial. He said it was too early in the experiment. He said, "I didn't want to be the guinea pig."

People leave jobs all the time, and most of the reasons are boring. So what was he running from? The easy answers do not fit him.

He was not a man who hated the machine. He used the tools. He said the setup seemed to work for his team. After he left, he took a job outside Silicon Valley and was content to watch from a distance. Whatever drove him out, it was not a grudge against software.

It was not simple cost-cutting either, though there was plenty of that. In one survey of a thousand US hiring managers in 2025, eighty percent were bracing for a recession, and eighty-three percent said they had already started cutting costs, streamlining, or leaving seats empty. But cost has been cutting managers for decades, and managers keep coming back. They were roughly nine percent of the American workforce in 1983 and thirteen percent by 2022, having survived every downsizing wave anyone bothered to name. Companies have run the flattening experiment before, and the managers have a way of coming back under one name or another. This wave felt different.

What made the wave new was the claim underneath it: that the software could now do the part of his job that used to require him. Not the typing. The knowing. His employer's chief executive had told investors the company was flattening teams, elevating individual contributors, and starting to see work that used to take a big team get done by a single very talented person. A lot of people believe that claim, and a lot of them are right.

Here is the strongest version, and it deserves to be stated without a sneer. Much of what we have called middle management was never judgment. It was traffic. A manager collected status from six people, turned it into a slide, carried it up a level, carried a decision back down, and scheduled a meeting to make sure everyone had heard. One big retailer's chief executive described his own company's version of this as "pre-meetings for the pre-meetings for the decision meetings," a chain of managers reviewing a thing before anyone was allowed to decide it. That is not coaching. That is latency wearing a lanyard. When the work already lives in tickets, commits, documents, and chat logs, a machine can read all of it and write the summary faster than the manager ever could. One payments company said the quiet part in public: in a firm where the work is already machine-readable, "there is no need for a permanent middle management layer." A crypto exchange called the layers a "coordination tax" and started building teams of one person and a set of agents. They are not wrong about the traffic. AI eats the traffic.

So concede it cleanly, because it is true and because the rest depends on being honest here. A large share of management was overhead. Middle managers were about a third of the layoffs in 2023. Job openings for them at the end of 2025 sat forty-two percent below their 2022 peak. Some of those roles existed only because a company had no other way to pay and promote a good engineer, and some existed only to relay information that now relays itself. AI makes that kind of manager easy to delete. Good.

But look closely at what the machine actually removed first, because it is not what the headline says. It did not remove management. It removed the paperwork around management: the status collection, the recap, the drafting, the ticket summary, the part that left a trace. And the moment companies pulled that layer out, they did something strange. They did not enjoy the silence. They rushed to fill it, and the thing they filled it with was measurement.

A large bank built dashboards that ranked its engineers by how much they used their AI tools, sorted them into "non," "light," and "heavy," and listed each person's most recent active day, office location, and reporting line. Nearly seventy thousand people were provisioned; about twenty-four thousand were counted as recently active. The bank said the numbers were not used in performance reviews. Some of the engineers feared being flagged anyway, and one of them warned the others to check whether they had landed on what he called the "naughty list." At a media company, a manager wrote to an engineer who had used an AI tool exactly once in thirty days, asking, by a deadline, what had made it "hard to get started." The same company's dashboard turned tool use into streak badges: ten days was "uncommon," thirty days was "epic." At a consulting firm, workers were expected to use AI on three-quarters of their working days, and one of them explained how easy that bar was to clear: "You can just run a prompt." You could even automate the prompt to fire on the weekend, so the day would count while you were not there.

Notice what every one of these systems measures. It measures that the tool was touched. It cannot measure whether the work was any good. That gap, between the thing that is easy to count and the thing that actually matters, is not a small technical flaw. It is the whole problem, and it is exactly the spot where the manager used to stand. One chief information officer who tracks his engineers' AI use refuses to rank them, because he learned that a number tied to a reward becomes a target and stops telling the truth. His company had used "percentage of code written by AI" as a metric for three or four months, then dropped it. "Activity," he said, "is not valuable to our business." A big retailer's own internal engineering playbook warned its people about exactly this trap: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Which is the counterintuitive thing this manager had seen before he left. The middle manager is not the layer AI lets you delete. In a company where the machine now does everything that leaves a trace, the manager becomes the one thing that does not: the layer that decides whether the green dashboard is telling the truth. Call it the trust layer. It does not need the old title; it needs a person with the time and the standing to overrule a clean number. It is load-bearing precisely because it cannot be counted, and it is easy to cut for exactly the same reason.

You can see its weight in the places it goes missing. The same 2026 research that found agents multiplying inside companies, fifteen times as many year over year, also asked where the value came from. When workers said they were getting real value out of AI, the things they tied it to were less about the person and more about the setting around the person: the culture, the talent practices, and a manager. In that survey, those organizational factors were linked to about twice the AI impact that individual effort was. One finding was sharp on its own: when a manager openly used the tools himself, the workers under him reported about thirty points more trust in the machine. A second pointed the same way. Advanced users were far more likely to say their manager set a standard for good work and made it safe to be wrong out loud. These are people describing their own workplaces, not a controlled trial, so take them as a strong hint rather than a proof. But the hint points the same way as the plainest number in the pile: eighty percent of workers said they would stay in a job because they had a manager they trusted. Trust never shows up on the adoption dashboard. It shows up when someone quits, or does not.

And here is why the gap between the meetings is not a soft, human-interest worry but a hard, structural one. A single bad answer from a machine is manageable. You catch it and move on. But bad answers approved at scale compound. One team, sped up by AI, ships more than before. The team downstream that has to approve the work drowns in the volume. A flaw nobody caught gets built on by the next team, and the next. A management professor put the cost in one line: cutting the layer means "one fewer layer of kicking the tires." You move faster, and you break more things. The manager had said a version of this himself. He had not watched anyone wave a broken AI decision through. But he could see the shape of the day it would happen, once enough people were rewarded for speed and no one was paid to notice.

The numbers already work against noticing. After one payments company cut forty percent of its staff, some of its engineering managers were handed as many as a hundred and seventy-five direct reports, where a former lead put the old number between six and twelve. Across the wider economy the average manager's span of control climbed from about eight reports in 2013 to more than twelve in 2025, and ninety-seven percent of managers now also carry a workload of their own on top of the people. In the same body of research, managers who have one real conversation a week with each person do substantially better. At seven reports moved to every other week, that conversation is already being rationed. At a hundred and seventy-five, it is not arithmetic anymore. It is a fiction. One manager who went from zero reports to twenty-one described the road there: "Our meetings became transactional because we only had time to discuss the most urgent issues." Then she burned out and quit. The private conversation, the one where a person admits they are lost or that the dashboard is lying, is the first thing the calendar loses and the last thing a company notices is gone.

None of this is the first time a company promised that flatter would be faster. But the old flattening, the real one that economists measured across more than three hundred large firms in the 1980s and 1990s, was not deletion. It moved authority upward: more leaders reporting straight to the top, paid more and trusted with more, not fewer people doing the same work with no one watching. Even when the supervisors thinned, the managers did not vanish. Their work migrated. The job stopped being about command and became about coordination and coaching, which is harder to see and harder to cut cleanly. Delete the title and you often just hide the work, until it grows back under a new name: player-coach, org lead, directly responsible individual. The layer has a way of reassembling itself, usually after something has already broken.

So go back to the manager and his calendar, the weekly slot that became every other week, the agents trading updates in the gap. The haunting thing was never that the system failed. He said it seemed to work. That was what worried him. The agent updates could not certify whether the machine was managing his people or only producing a clean record of having done so. A guinea pig is not afraid the drug will fail. It is afraid the drug will look fine on the chart while something the chart was never built to measure goes quietly wrong in the body. He got to step out of the trial and watch from a distance. Many others are still in it, on the other end of an agent update, in a gap that someone has decided nothing important was living in. They will find out the way you always find out a trust layer was load-bearing: not the day it is removed, but later, when something falls and everyone turns at once to the empty place where the person used to stand.
