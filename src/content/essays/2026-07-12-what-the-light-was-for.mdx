There is a small light on a pair of Ray-Ban Meta glasses whose main job is to warn the people nearby. It sits near the right hinge and blinks when the camera is recording, so a person across from you can tell, if they happen to catch it. In a July 2025 white paper, according to The Verge, Meta reserved that light for one narrow case: active capture, the moments when a photo or video is actually being saved. During ordinary AI features, like scanning a menu, it would stay dark. The stated worry was that a light which comes on too often stops meaning anything. People get used to it.

Then, in July 2026, The Verge reported, citing the Financial Times, that Meta was building prototype "super sensing" glasses. They could record audio without stopping and take a photo every few seconds, then let the wearer ask an assistant about whatever they had seen and heard. A photo every few seconds is an abstraction until you carry the arithmetic out: at one every five seconds, that is seven hundred and twenty an hour, and something over five thousand across a working day. The exact interval is not public; the number is only the reported phrase multiplied. In that mode the light would stay off. One proposed version would not even keep the footage. It would pull metadata from the images and audio, upload that, and throw the raw capture away.

Read those two decisions together and you get something quietly upside down. The signal that warns you a camera is working goes dark in the very mode where the sensing almost never stops.

A device that helps you remember should be the most harmless thing in your bag. Instead, the people around it keep flinching. A journalist who wore Meta's glasses for a month wrote that her two most common questions from other people were "Why?" and "Are you filming me?" When she got home, her boyfriend froze at the sight of them. A reviewer at Wired described an "immediate, almost visceral discomfort" in everyone he showed them to, and noticed that people stopped wanting to look him in the eye. The wearer is having a pleasant experience. They are logging a meal, catching up on messages, keeping their hands free. The person across from them is having a different one. Why would a machine for remembering make people pull back from you?

The answer most people reach for is privacy, and it is shallow. What is at stake is not your data. It is your ability to be forgotten by the person next to you, and these glasses remove it whether or not they ever save a thing.

<PullQuote>
Forgetting, it turns out, is the floor under how we share a space with strangers.
</PullQuote>

Take the three easy explanations first, because ruling them out is how you find the real one.

The obvious one is that you might be filmed. That answer does not survive the facts. Phones have filmed us for twenty years, and we made a rough peace with them, because a raised phone is a visible act: you can see it and step out of frame. The sharper case is one where nothing was filmed at all. At a Manhattan salon, a woman getting a wax noticed her aesthetician was wearing Meta glasses and asked, mid appointment, whether they were live. The employee said they were not charged and were only a prescription; the company later said staff keep them off during appointments. There was no evidence the glasses were recording. The room had already changed. What she lost was not a video. It was the ability to rest in the plain fact that no camera was in the room.

The next explanation is that your data will be stored, brokered, sold. That fear is well earned in general. But the reported super sensing design cuts against the simplest form of it. One version keeps no raw footage or audio, only the thinner metadata pulled from them, and Meta says its message recall runs on the device with end-to-end encryption. That metadata is still a record, just a smaller one. And if the whole harm were the saved footage, the version that saves none of it should calm you. It does not. The unease arrives before the storage question is even asked, which means storage is not the root of it.

The last explanation is that the technology is new, and people fear new things until they stop. Except this reaction is thirteen years old. In 2013, before Google Glass was widely sold, a Seattle bar banned it. The owner's reason had nothing to do with data. He said people came to his place to be not known. That hardware is dead and mostly forgotten. The camera on the face is far better now. The flinch is exactly the same. More than a decade of familiarity has not dissolved it.

So strike out the footage, the archive, and the novelty. What is left is the one thing these glasses are built to remove, and sold as removing: forgetting.

Here is the bargain that makes public life bearable, stated plainly. When you sit across from a stranger on a train, the two of you are holding a quiet agreement that you will each pass out of the other's memory within the hour. That expectation is what lets you be unguarded in front of people you will never see again. It rests on three conditions. You can tell when you are being recorded. You can do something about it if you object. And you can trust that the moment will fade. Call them notice, refusal, and decay. A memory aid stays humane when it leaves those three intact for the people near the wearer. It turns extractive the moment it takes them away.

This is not a theorist's worry. In a 2026 study of camera glasses, the people being filmed consistently wanted more consent and more control than the wearers were willing to give, and in the most sensitive settings most of them said they would do something to defend themselves. The study was run in China, and its authors expect the tension to travel. The bargain is already contested; the glasses are one side quietly rewriting the terms.

The clearest picture of what rewriting them looks like came from two Harvard students in 2024. They wired a pair of Ray-Ban Meta glasses to a face search engine and some public databases and made a demo they called I-XRAY. In the video, the wearer walks a subway platform. You can read the red tiles behind him: HARVARD. A sign says ELEVATOR. A train sits at the edge of the platform. He approaches a man in a white patterned shirt and glasses, one hand in his pocket, and opens as if they had already met, with a first name and where the man worked. On the wearer's phone, blurred in the video, are the results of a face search that ran while he walked up.

Follow the chain, because the chain is the whole point. A glance becomes a video frame. The frame becomes a query to a face search engine that looks for where that face already appears on the open web. A match becomes a name. The name becomes a route, through people-search sites and public records, into addresses, phone numbers, relatives, an employer. None of it ran on the glasses; the face recognition ran on a laptop, and Meta is right to say its glasses have no such feature built in. The two students never released the code, and said they built it as a warning. The one who posted the video asked, in the text above it, whether we are ready for a world where our data is exposed at a glance. That is the part to sit with. This was the warning.

Set the man on the platform against the three conditions. He had no notice. He had no chance to refuse; the search had already run while the wearer walked up, before the conversation began. And there is no decay, though not because the demo kept him. The face search finds where a face already sits on the open web; from a match it reads a name, and people-search sites and public records turn that name into an address, a phone number, relatives, an employer. Those records do not fade. He looked straight at the camera when he was addressed, and then he smiled. The record shows that and no more.

Now run the same template across a case that uses the same hardware and comes out the opposite way. A low-vision accessibility tester, who was born with a condition that limited his sight and has lost more of it since, uses Meta's glasses with an app called Be My Eyes to play video games. He moves his head instead of angling a phone, and a volunteer on the other end tells him whether an enemy is in front of him, whether he is walking in the right direction, whether he is about to walk into a wall. The calls sometimes run past the task, past an hour, into ordinary conversation. When money or medical papers are involved, he routes the call to a private group of people he trusts rather than to a stranger.

The eye-level camera is identical. The moral weight is not, and the three conditions tell you why. The person on the other end is chosen and consenting. The help is asked for, not taken. And when the matter is private, the call routes to people he picked rather than whoever is passing. A wearer who teaches her glasses to recognize a colleague who agreed to be learned is not doing what the subway demo did, even though both are called face recognition. The difference is not the sensor. It is whether the people being remembered kept their notice, their refusal, and their right to fade.

Which is where the argument has to be honest about its own soft spot. For some people, forgetting is not a mercy. It is a wall. A woman left severely amnesic after a brain illness wore an automatic camera that took several hundred pictures a day; watching them back, she recalled about eighty percent of recent events, against roughly half from a written diary, and nothing at all a month later without them. Tell her the lapse is a kind of freedom and she would rightly stare at you. The pictures gave her back her own life.

But notice what her camera remembered: her life, with her consent, on her side of the glass. That is the line the super sensing design proposes to cross. It is sold to remember the wearer's day. It does that by turning everyone else in the room into part of that memory too, without their notice, with the light off. The assistive case is real, and it is not a defense of the ambient one. It is the control that shows exactly which variable turned poison. Move the remembered person from the wearer's own life to the stranger across the aisle, take away their notice, and the same gentle machine becomes a way of remembering people who were never asked.

<Callout>
A test you can carry into any room: do not ask whether a device is recording. Ask whether the people it remembers can still be forgotten by it. Notice, refusal, decay.
</Callout>

By that test, the light going dark is not a small interface choice. It removes the first condition, notice, and once notice is gone the other two fall with it: you cannot refuse what you never saw, and you cannot count on fading from a record you did not know was made. The reason given for switching the light off, that a light which blinks too often stops being noticed, is an admission that notice was doing its job, and is now in the way.

The deepest cost lands before a single file is saved. The moment you can no longer assume the stranger across from you will forget you, you stop being able to be unguarded in front of them, and something ordinary and free goes out of the space between you. You feel it as the flinch. It was never really about the footage.

There is a small, unremarkable kindness you perform for strangers all day without noticing. You let your gaze slide off them. You forget the face on the platform, the phone conversation two seats down, the stranger lost in a screen across the aisle. It costs you nothing, and it is most of what makes a crowded place survivable. The glasses are being built, in part, to relieve you of that work, and to keep the light off while they do it.

The man on the platform looked into the camera and smiled. That is the ordinary thing to do with a stranger's glance, and the whole bargain used to be that it cost you nothing: the two of you would slide out of each other's memory by the next stop. His glance did not get to slide anywhere. It had already been turned into a face search, and the records it pulls back do not fade on their own.
