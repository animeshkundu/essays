In the story we tell about appetite, a loud one is a verdict on the person. If you cannot stop eating, you are weak, or greedy, or hiding from something you will not face. The cure is character: more discipline, less self-pity, a smaller plate and a firmer will.

Medicine never fully believed this. For decades it treated hunger and body weight as partly biology, and built appetite drugs to act on it, several of which were later pulled for grave harm. But the moral story held in public. What the GLP-1 drugs added was a felt before-and-after: many patients could suddenly set the noise beside its absence, and that comparison makes the biological account much harder to wave away. Ask people taking these drugs what changed, and many describe not willpower but a sound going out.

A nurse who spent her adult life waking in the middle of the night and eating from the refrigerator until the urge let go says the night hunger simply stopped, and that the drug let her forgive herself for years she had blamed on weakness. In brain scans, a drug in this class dampens the response to food cues in the reward and salience regions, and when researchers block the receptor, the effect is largely blocked. A survey of five hundred fifty people on semaglutide, asked to remember their food noise before the drug and score it now, put the median at thirteen out of twenty, then six, though a number recalled after the fact is a soft kind of measure. The inner demand people had been told to master turns out to be, in large part, a signal. You can turn it down with a needle.

That is a real finding, and it should have changed how we talk. Instead it has licensed a worse idea: that a quiet appetite is the same thing as a healed person.

It is not. The quiet tells you something changed. It does not tell you whether the change is recovery. The same drop in hunger that means recovery in one body can mean relapse in the next, and at three weeks in, from the outside, no one can reliably tell them apart. We watch the noise go down and call it getting better.

Start with the case that made the headlines. A woman, forty-four, had chased the feeling of being full for more than twenty years. In college she once stood in her kitchen with her coat still on and ate an entire pizza, a bag of cookies, and a gallon of mint-chip ice cream before she took the coat off. She had made herself so good at throwing up that she could do it almost without a sound. She still has calluses on her knuckles from it. She had done the inpatient program, the antidepressant approved for exactly her diagnosis, the psychiatry, the nutrition counseling, the group therapy, and none of it held. Then she heard the phrase "food noise" on a podcast about weight-loss drugs and thought, in her words, what do I have to lose, I'm actively killing myself right now. Her family doctor wrote her a prescription for Ozempic, which is not approved for her illness and not covered for it, so she paid between $350 and $1,200 a month out of her own pocket. About three weeks in, the running list of what she would eat next went quiet. She ate a normal bowl of cereal and felt satisfied, she said, for the first time she could remember. Four years later she is in remission from bulimia and binge-eating disorder, up about ten pounds, at a weight she calls healthy.

If the story ended there it would read as a miracle, which is the easy way to read it. It does not end there.

Consider what the same appetite effect did to other people, on drugs in the same class. A woman who had lived with anorexia for more than a decade told her doctors, plainly, that shutting off her appetite was a bad idea. She was put on the drug anyway, over the objection of the psychiatrist who knew her. She relapsed almost immediately, and when surgery later forced her to stop for a month, every thought about food came back exactly as it had been, a return she called horrifying. Another woman's doctor praised her weight loss while her blood work came back malnourished; she was eating three or four hundred calories on many days, living on the appetite suppression, losing her hair, her gums bleeding, joking with a friend on a similar drug about getting scurvy. A patient switched medications during a shortage and the next day was living on water and Diet Coke: no solid food for thirteen days, twenty-one pounds gone in those thirteen days. Another, titrated upward through a telehealth app with no counseling, lost nearly all hunger and thirst after a dose increase, dropped to four or six hundred calories a day, and stopped drinking until her kidneys registered the cost, her creatinine climbing from 0.6 to 3.6 before she was hospitalized for acute kidney injury.

None of this is the drug breaking. The appetite went down, the cravings went down, the food noise went quiet: that is the effect the drug is built to produce. What differs is the body it happened in and the care around it. In the wrong context, the wanted effect keeps going until it becomes malnutrition, dehydration, a kidney in failure. The drug acts on the signal. It does not act on what the signal meant. It has no opinion about why the appetite was loud, or what the person will do once it goes.

The fair version of the other side is not the reckless one. It is the careful clinic: a patient with binge eating disorder and real medical obesity, screened before the first dose, watched by a prescriber who talks to an eating-disorder specialist and a dietitian, with a written plan to stop if the wrong signs appear. Some clinicians say they see that patient get better, and I believe them. That is a real practice and a real result, and it is not what happened to the woman on water and Diet Coke.

But grant the best-run version, and the core problem is still standing. The thing that tells the careful clinic the drug is working, fewer binges, less food noise, some weight coming off, is the same thing the disease produces when it is winning. Monitoring catches the gross harms: the malnutrition, the dehydration, the creatinine climbing. It does not answer the harder question of whether, in this person, the change is recovery or a relapse wearing a better disguise.

Three comforting readings are on offer, and each falls short.

The first is that this is really a weight-loss story, and the eating disorders are a side note. But the noise in the head can change early, before the scale has moved much. That is why the drug reaches past the scale into the disorders of eating themselves, into binge eating disorder, whose defining symptom is a loss of control over food and not a wish to be thin.

The second is that you could just screen the sick people out. Except the woman in remission and the woman who relapsed both had diagnosed eating disorders before the first injection, and the drug carried them in opposite directions. Screening flags who is at risk; it does not tell you which way the drug will push a given patient. And screening is a weak point on its own. The researchers who study this say no standard protocol exists for catching an eating disorder before a prescription is written; the specialties that write these prescriptions get little eating-disorder training; some telehealth services ask only that the patient be honest. In one sample of adults who already had an eating disorder, about a third had taken these drugs, roughly one in ten in a way that counts as misuse.

The third is that it is only nausea, that people eat less because the drug makes them queasy. But the biology separates the two. In mice, satiety and aversion run on different circuits, and the drugs cut eating even when the sickness pathway is switched off. The relief is not a stomachache. It is real, and that is exactly what makes it dangerous, because a genuine, comfortable, welcome calm is much harder to argue with than a side effect.

What is left standing, once those go, is the uncomfortable thing. These drugs act on the signal and not on its meaning. Hunger, it turns out, is biology you can treat. But a disordered relationship to food was never only hunger. It is control, and ritual, and fear, and a way of managing a feeling that has nothing to do with the refrigerator, and the appetite was only the part you could see. Recovery is more than a quiet appetite: regular meals, no purging or restricting, less fear of gaining weight, a life not organized around the scale. Turn the appetite down and you have not touched the rest. You have removed the noise that used to tell everyone, the patient included, that something was wrong.

This is where the two halves of medicine that handle food have spent decades walking in opposite directions. One half treats obesity, and it measures success by the number going down. The other treats eating disorders, and it learned, the hard way, that the number going down is often the disease winning. Bulimia entered the medical literature in 1979 as a self-perpetuating loop: an irresistible urge to overeat, then vomiting or purging, wrapped around a morbid fear of becoming fat. The whole illness is built to make less eating and less weight feel like victory. Now a drug can deliver that feeling on a weekly schedule, and the two halves of medicine cannot agree whether to celebrate.

What should stop the celebration is that the evidence is thinner than the enthusiasm. The cleanest test we have, a seventeen-week randomized trial of a drug in this class for binge-eating disorder, found no significant difference in binge episodes between the drug and the placebo. The strongest claim for treating bulimia rests on one psychiatrist's estimate that the medicine is almost an instant cure for a quarter to a third of his patients, which is testimony, not a trial. Only two drugs carry federal approval for any eating disorder at all, and neither is one of these. No major medical organization has endorsed this use. We are, in effect, running the experiment first and building the evidence afterward.

The relief may not even hold. In one closely tracked patient, an implanted electrode showed the craving signal in her brain fall silent on the drug, then return, along with the food preoccupation, after about five months. In a sixty-week trial, the drug cut hunger and food preoccupation more than placebo at twenty weeks, but by weeks forty and sixty that gap was gone, even as people kept eating less. The measured relief can fade while the lower eating goes on. For patients whose disease is built around restriction or purging, some will get its reward, thinness, lose the thing they actually took the drug for, and still be afraid to stop.

Underneath all of this sits a question: why are so many people reaching for a diabetes and obesity drug to treat an eating disorder at all? Part of the answer is that the illness has been starved. Federal research on eating disorders ran about $53 million in a recent year, roughly a fifth of what went to schizophrenia. Seventy percent of people with bulimia and more than half of those with binge-eating disorder still have symptoms after the best therapy we offer. When the standard of care leaves most patients still sick, an injection that makes the noise stop is not a temptation. It is a rational act of desperation. The woman in remission has since spent $44,000 of her own inheritance funding research into why the thing that saved her works. That is not what a solved problem looks like.

So name the idea to refuse. Not the drugs, which are doing something real. Not the patients, who are not wrong about their own relief. The equation is this: that a quiet appetite is a well person, that a smaller body is a healthier one, that fewer binges this month is recovery. That equation is the oldest mistake in this field wearing a new and very convincing disguise, because now the patient feels it too. The relief is genuine. It is evidence that a symptom changed. It is not proof that a person got well.

The one thing we do not know, and should say plainly and once, is this: no one has built the instrument that could tell, when the food noise goes quiet in a given person, whether they are recovering, or trading one symptom for another, or handing their disorder the cleanest tool it has ever had. There are questionnaires that flag a disorder. There is none that reads the silence and says which kind it is.

Until someone builds that instrument, the honest response is not relief. It is a question. When the noise stops, someone has to keep asking what the noise was for, and to keep asking after the weight comes off, after the praise starts, after the patient herself has decided she is fine. The woman with the calluses on her knuckles is four years into remission, up about ten pounds, at a weight she calls healthy. And here is the part no one can yet answer, not the doctors, not the researchers she keeps emailing, not the woman herself: whether the same quiet that gave her back her life is, in someone standing right beside her, the sound of the disease closing over their head.
