Judge an hour by what it leaves you, not by how good it felt while it passed. A lab in China ran close to that test on eighty-two young men who all watch a great deal of short video: phone switched off, a digit on the screen, a key to press if it matched the one from two beats back. The heaviest averaged about six hours a day, counted from a month of their own phone records. They got about as many right as the lower-use men in the same heavy-using sample. They were just slower, and less efficient. Small, all male, one country, an association and not a cause.

The easy verdicts miss it. The clips are not stupid, and they are not too short: sixty seconds can carry a recipe you cook that night, or a sea shanty that became a number-one single. What is new is the loop. It is tuned for whether you finish and what to serve you next; nowhere on that dial is a reading for what you keep. Memory is not what it is built to reward. It hands you a great deal and asks nothing back, and it teaches you to call the emptiness a rest.

The warning is smaller and stranger than the panic. Those students were not broken, not wrong, just slower, and even that is only linked to the feed, never charged to it. The system has no reason to care what remains, because remaining was never what it sold. So ask your own week what the feed never will: what did the hours leave you, and would you know if the answer were nothing?
