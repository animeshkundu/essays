Here is a strange fact about the nose. You can lose your sense of smell and be the last to know.

In a Wisconsin study of a few thousand older adults, testing found that about a quarter had a real, measurable smell problem. Asked straight out whether they did, fewer than one in ten said yes. So most of the people whose noses had gone dim did not know it. Nobody loses a quarter of their eyesight and shrugs.

We have always held the sense a little cheap. In the 1960s the field filed taste and smell under the "minor senses." The Nobel committee that honored the scientists who finally worked out how the nose reads a molecule called smell "the most enigmatic of our senses." One patient who lost theirs was told by a doctor: "You're lucky! You won't have to smell the diapers." No big deal, the doctor said.

Now hold two facts next to each other. This is the one sense you can lose without noticing, the one a doctor might congratulate you for losing. It is also the one whose loss, followed all the way down, quietly takes apart a person's safety, their appetite, their marriage, their memory, and their sense of who they are. Both are true. A loss too small to feel arriving. A loss that reaches everything. Something in that does not add up, and the gap is worth walking into slowly, because a machine is now being built inside it, and we are about to misread what the machine does.

First, rule out the boring explanations.

Maybe the ranking is simply right, and people report little because there is little to report. But the evidence does not hold that up. People with no sense of smell miss the smoke from a fire in their own kitchen. They eat food that is months spoiled. Some lose weight they did not mean to lose. Whatever is happening, the loss is not minor. The not-noticing is a separate puzzle.

Maybe smell is only about pleasure, then. Coffee, wine, cut roses. Lose it and you lose a luxury, not a limb. But pleasure does not explain danger. Among American adults over seventy, one in five cannot identify the smell of smoke, and one in three cannot identify the odorant added to natural gas so a leak will reach you. A sense that stands between you and a house fire is not a garnish.

And there is the pandemic story, the idea that COVID invented the problem. Also no. Smell loss was already common before 2020, sitting in about a fifth of the general population, mostly from ordinary colds and sinus trouble, from head injuries, and from age. COVID did not create it. It walked into a dark, crowded room and turned on the lights. In May 2020, health officials added loss of smell to the official symptom list, and millions of people met, all at once, a loss that a smaller number had been living with alone for decades.

So the ranking is wrong, the loss is not about pleasure, and it is not new. Why, then, can you lose this sense and barely notice?

Here is the answer that fits. Smell did all of its work without ever telling you it was working. Less than fifteen percent of the air you breathe even reaches the patch of tissue that smells; the rest just moves through. Most of what you call the taste of food is actually smell, arriving up the back of your throat while you chew, and you credit your tongue for all of it. The sense ran underneath attention, all day, and never sent up a signal that said "still here." You never noticed it because noticing was never its job.

Hold onto that, because it sets up the mistake. A sense that worked invisibly cannot be measured by the one thing it never did, which is announce itself. Yet announcing itself is exactly what we are about to use to decide whether we have fixed it.

There are two different repairs hiding inside the word "restore." One gives back detection: you can tell an odor is there, and you can name it. The other gives back reach: the coffee smells like the coffee you knew, the rain smells like rain, your kitchen smells like home. The first one we are getting close to. The second one we have not shown how to measure, much less restore.

<PullQuote>
A device that lets you name a smell has not given you your sense of smell back. It has given you a caption.
</PullQuote>

Look at the device. It is real, and the reasoning is physical, not magical. In a lab in Ohio, a researcher has built two small things you put in your nose, a foam plug and a clip, and he compares them to a hearing aid. In some people the smell system is not dead; the air just is not reaching it. So the plug is shaped to steer more air up toward the sensor. "You're amplifying the smell signal," he says, "instead of the sound."

One of the people who tried it is a pastor in Ohio, fifty-one, who lost his smell to COVID in the spring of 2025. For a year he had not smelled chocolate or coffee. With the plugs in, he could. He called it amazing. He said he could smell just about everything. They let him take a prototype home, and because he is the cook in his house, he sometimes slips the black plugs into his nose to take a whiff of what he is making. His wife laughs at him. The plugs stick out; he says it would be strange to wear them in public.

But watch what the study actually measured. On a nine-item test of whether you can name a smell placed in front of you, the average score moved from 4.30 to 5.11. A real gain, and a small one. For a man who had gone a year without coffee, still worth something. What they did not test is threshold, the fainter question of how little odor you need before you catch it at all. And buried in the study's own pages is a line from a patient that should slow the celebration. He can tell the odor is coffee. But, he says, it does not smell at all like the coffee he used to know. He recognizes it. He does not have it back.

The people who built the device are honest about this, which is the part I trust. The researcher guesses you need some working smell left for the plug to amplify at all; hand it to a fully dead nose and it has nothing to boost. He has no long-term data on people wearing it through an ordinary day. Another researcher, one who builds smell implants and so has every reason to root for this, notes that naming an odor is not the same as detecting a faint one, and that the device sits in the nasal cavity, where people are usually very sensitive, though some of his patients would reach for something like it when they needed it. So the honest description is narrow: for some people, it hands back the caption. Whether it hands back the coffee is a different question, and right now the answer is that we do not know, and often it will be no.

Which forces the question the excitement keeps skipping. What, exactly, was smell holding up, that a caption does not replace?

Start with safety, the part everyone will grant. A woman is working in another room while her oven catches fire. The dog barks; she thinks he wants to play; she comes down to a kitchen full of smoke she cannot smell. After that she is afraid to cook. This is the part a smoke alarm and a gas detector can cover, which is the strongest thing the other side will say, and they are not wrong to say it. Notice, though, that the smell device is not what covers it. A plug that lifts a naming score by a point was never shown to catch smoke, or gas, or spoiled food.

Then food, because with smell gone, food goes with it. Flavor was mostly smell. People start eating by texture and temperature and duty. They compare it to chewing cardboard. One woman lives for a stretch on bread and cheese because it is all she can stand. Another keeps grapes around as her one safe food. Some lose weight; some eat more of the loud, sweet things that still register. A smoke alarm does not touch this. Neither does naming coffee.

Then the deepest part, and here I will keep it plain. A woman can no longer kiss her partner. A man's body wash makes his wife sick to stand near him. People stop trusting their own smell and build rules to cover for it: shower on a schedule, replace the deodorant by the calendar, ask someone else whether they are clean, because the sense that used to answer that is gone. A parent writes that they have a two-year-old daughter and have never once smelled her. Rainy days stop smelling of wet ground.

That last set is not danger and it is not pleasure. It is reach: the way smell kept you in contact with your own body, with the people close enough to touch, and with rooms you can no longer get back to. In mice, researchers can switch off a small set of long-lived cells born on the first day of life and dim a liking the animal formed in its first weeks. In people they can only ask, and the researchers who study this call smell an exceptional cue for reaching an old memory, with one catch: the smell has to be exact, the way a password has to be exact. Lose it and the roses are the smallest thing you lose. You lose one of the doors back into your own life.

And there is a cruelty inside the loss that the word "restore" hides completely. For a lot of people, smell does not go quiet and stay quiet. It comes partway back wrong. Coffee turns to sewage. Meat and onion turn to rot. The kitchen turns twice: first to nothing, then to something foul. A woman in England ends up in tears trying to cook, because the oven fills the house with a smell like decay the moment it heats. Another woman ate from a pizza eight months past its date and was sick the next day, because her nose no longer warned her; for months it had been lying to her. For these people a partial return is worse than the silence. Whatever "restored" is supposed to mean, it cannot mean this.

Smell is secondary, and the numbers are not close. That is the strongest case against everything I have just said. Around the world, some 2.2 billion people have a vision problem, 430 million a disabling hearing loss, 15 million a spinal cord injury; 57 million live with dementia, the seventh leading cause of death, and 619 million with low back pain, the leading cause of disability on earth. There is no line like that for smell loss, because it does not rank at that scale. Most smell loss heals on its own: after COVID, about three in four recover within a month and about ninety-five percent within six, leaving a few percent with a lasting problem. The one part that can actually kill you, the danger signal, is already covered by cheap alarms. So if you have a fixed pile of research money and a fixed number of surgeons, you spend them on eyes, ears, spines, and minds first. You would be right to. Smell is near the bottom of the triage list, and a triage list is not cruelty; it is arithmetic.

I think that case is correct about the ranking and wrong about the conclusion, and the reason it is wrong sits back in the anomaly we started with. People cannot report a loss they cannot perceive. The low ranking of smell is partly a trick of the sense being invisible even to the person losing it. When patients underreport, the quiet is the reason, not the size of the loss. Sit with the accounts people actually gave and you do not find an inconvenience. You find someone rebuilding safety out of alarms, eating for fuel, afraid to be close to anyone, and unable to get home.

I am not saying smell outranks sight, hearing, a moving body, or a clear mind. It does not. But notice where the other side and I actually land, because it is the same place. We both agree: do not confuse safety with self. They say smell is minor because the safety part is covered, so the rest can wait. I am saying the safety part was always the smallest part, and a machine that restores it while we call the result a cure is confusing the two in the opposite direction. Both of us are pointing at the same error from opposite ends. Restore what you can restore. Just do not lie about which one you restored.

Because the real risk here is not that we do nothing. It is that we declare victory on the caption. A study moves an identification score by a point, and the lab result becomes a claim that smell is back. A device lets a man name coffee. And underneath all of it runs a quiet agreement to stop asking the harder thing, whether the coffee ever smells like coffee again. The day we can prove smell "restored" on a nine-item test is the day we can stop paying for the part the test does not measure.

So picture the cook again. Black plugs sticking out of his nose, leaning close to the food he is making, stealing a whiff of his own dinner while his wife laughs at him. After a year, with the device in, he can smell something of what he is making. That is not nothing.

I keep getting stuck on what he got back. A warning system is one name for it, the kind a smoke alarm on the ceiling already covers. A caption is another, so he can tell you it is coffee. Or maybe, for one leaned-over minute over the food he is making, it is some thin version of the thing smell was always secretly doing, which was to put him back inside his own kitchen, his own body, his own life. We are starting to build the devices and trials to bring it back, on the promise that it is the last one. We have not yet built the test that could tell him whether it is.
