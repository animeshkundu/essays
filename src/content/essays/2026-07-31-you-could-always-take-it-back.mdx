A writer wore an AI pendant to her book club, and the room changed before anyone said a real word.

The device hung around her neck: a small white pebble with a glowing light in the middle. She had texted the group beforehand to warn them it might be recording. One friend answered with a vomiting emoji. Another, a lawyer, wrote back, "No one talk about anything privileged and confidential!" When she walked in, everyone groaned at the glowing puck on her chest. Then a man in the group looked at it and said, "Tell it I don't want it recording anything I say." She lifted the device toward his face so he could tell it himself.

No secret had slipped yet. Nothing private had even been said in the room. And still the evening's first act of speech was a request not to be on the record. People who came to talk about a book were now managing an object, and the object had quietly taken charge of the room.

That pebble was one of the milder devices in the category. It was built to be a companion, a thing to talk to. The serious versions are built to remember. They are small recorders you clip to a collar or wear on a cord, and they promise perfect recall of everything you say and hear. One of them, a pill-shaped gadget that costs fifty dollars and runs for a week on a charge, has no controls at all except a mute button. It sits in the background, listening, and it lights up only when you silence it, so the glow means the opposite of what you would guess. A reviewer who tested one put its appeal plainly: "More than once I've used it to win a bet by proving what I said days earlier."

Hold onto that sentence, because it sounds like nothing. Winning a bet. Proving what you said. Who could object to being right?

Here is what it costs. The first price of perfect recall is not that we forget less. It is that we speak less freely, because a conversation stops being a rough draft the moment one person can call up the transcript.

Consider a strange thing the makers do. One company built a pendant designed, in its own words, to record continuously, around the clock, so the wearer never has to turn it off. The hardware is built for always. Then the same company's instructions beg you not to use it that way. Turn it on when you want to record, they say. Turn it off when the conversation is over. Do not leave it on and recording at all times.

Why build a machine for always and then plead for sometimes?

The boring explanations do not hold. It is not battery: these things run for days. It is not storage: one pendant keeps thirty-five hours of talk on the device and weeks more on your phone. It is not even the law, exactly, because the company has already thought about that and written you a script to read aloud. ("This conversation is important to me and I want to remember what you say without taking notes. Is it ok if I use this Pendant to capture the conversation?")

Rule those out and the most revealing explanation is the one the makers will not put in the ad copy: a recorded conversation is a different conversation. The machine works fine. It is the talk that stops working. So they protect the talk from the machine.

To see why, follow what happens to a sentence after you say it. It becomes audio, then a transcript, then a summary, then a row of labels marking who said what. It becomes searchable. On one app the search box invites you to "ask about anything you've said or heard." You can type a word and find every time it was spoken. In some versions you can click any word in the transcript and hear the voice come back from exactly that point. You no longer have to remember the argument. You have to remember one word, and the machine remembers the rest.

That is the courtroom feeling, brought home to the kitchen. A conversation used to live only in two fallible memories. You could be asked what was said, but there was nothing to hand over, nothing to play back. Now it lives in a file: a transcript, a summary, a clip of your own voice that someone else can produce.

Start with the harmless cases, because they are real, and they are most of what these devices do. Who agreed to see the movie on Thursday. Who promised to call the plumber. Who actually said they would be home by six. In a house where these questions curdle into standoffs, a record can feel like peace.

Then climb one step. The thing you said while tired and took back an hour later. The opinion you floated just to hear how it sounded, then dropped. The cruel half-sentence in the middle of a fight that both of you agreed, without agreeing, to pretend was never finished. The apology that came out wrong the first time. Every close relationship runs on a steady supply of these: words said badly, on the way to being said better, allowed to dissolve.

A rough draft is a private thing. It is where you are permitted to be wrong out loud. Take away the permission to dissolve, and you have not made people more honest. You have made them more careful.

We already have a preview of careful, from the one room where recording arrived first. In one industry survey, eighty-four percent of people who had sat in meetings with an AI notetaker said they changed how they spoke when it was on. The number comes from a company that sells the tools, so treat it as a direction, not a measurement. Lawyers now warn boards that a verbatim machine record, unlike old-fashioned minutes, captures the side comments and the half-formed thoughts, and that people go quieter when they know it exists. One investor who backs a recording startup described the new baseline flatly: "Now, I assume that everything, even coffee meetings, are being recorded." That assumption used to sound paranoid. It is becoming manners.

The honest objection is that blur was never free. It always had a price, and someone always paid it. Human memory is not a recording; it is a story we rebuild each time, and we rebuild it in our own favor. The calmer person, the more confident person, the one with the better words for what happened, tends to win the argument about what happened. "Are you sure? You tend to have a bad memory" is a whole strategy folded into a sentence.

For the person whose memory keeps getting overruled, a record is a fair hearing. And for some people the mercy is simpler than that. A woman with severe memory loss wore a camera that photographed her day, then watched the images back, she said, like watching a movie. With the camera she could recall about eighty percent of her recent experiences afterward, where a written diary had left her with about half. Cancer patients who record the appointment play it again at home, alone or with family, and finally hold onto what they were told. That is not coldness. That is a person handed back a piece of her own life.

So the line falls in one place: between a record you chose, for a reason, with an end, and a record that is simply always running because the device was built that way and nobody turned it off. The first hands back agency. The second slowly rewrites what it feels safe to say at your own table.

Because the quiet thing blur was doing, all along, was keeping conversation cheap. You could say the half-thing and let it go. You could be wrong at noon and forgiven by dinner, partly because no one could produce the noon version anymore. Forgetting a harmless conversation was not a failure of the old world. It was one of its mercies. It is how people stayed married, stayed friends, stayed on speaking terms with the version of themselves that existed last year.

In December 2025, the company behind one of these pendants was bought, and it wrote to the people who had been wearing it. They had a choice to make about the record of their own lives. They could export it: request the whole stored history the device had built, the transcripts and lifelogs and chats, with the audio too if they checked a box to include it. Or they could delete it, by typing their own email address and pressing a button that said Delete Account Permanently. Keep the record, or erase it. There was no third button for the way it used to work, when a conversation was simply gone afterward, on its own, the way conversations went.

The next time you are about to say the wrong thing to someone you love, on the way to saying the right one, you may catch yourself glancing at what they are wearing. That glance is the cost. It arrives long before anyone plays anything back.
