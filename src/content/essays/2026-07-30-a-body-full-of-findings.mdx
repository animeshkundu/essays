The pitch is certainty. Lie still in a magnet for about an hour, a weighted cover on your chest and a mirror in the mask so you can watch a show, and let it read you from the crown of your skull to your upper thighs. No radiation. The scan itself uses no dye and no needle. Walk out, the promise goes, knowing what is inside you. One company scans for more than five hundred conditions and calls the result a virtual physical, a look inside the body that an ordinary annual exam cannot give. The price runs from about twelve hundred dollars to several thousand, rarely covered by insurance, paid by people who feel fine.

What the machine actually hands a healthy person is a report. And in a body with no symptoms, the report almost always says something. In the main reviews, about ninety-four to ninety-five percent of full-body scans turn up at least one abnormal finding. Roughly one to two percent ever turn out to be cancer, and even that number moves with the study and the protocol.

Those two figures are the argument. The scan is sold as early detection. What it reliably produces is not certainty. It is a finding. And a finding, in a well person, is mostly the beginning of work.

Hold one case against that, the one that seems to break it. A man who makes his living on reality television got a routine full-body scan and expected a routine result. The scan turned up a lesion in his brain instead. A dedicated MRI showed a blueberry-sized thing that looked to be a glioma, and the next step was surgery to cut it out and test it. Keep him in mind. Almost everything here turns on what his case proves, and on what it does not. First the ordinary machinery, the part the brochure sells to everyone else.

## Why the search finds so much and settles so little

Point a broad, sensitive search at a population where the disease you fear is rare, and you will turn up abnormalities far more often than disease, because bodies are lumpy and most lumps are nothing. Cleveland Clinic, summarizing the research, puts it plainly: about ninety-five percent of people scanned have at least one abnormal finding, and more than ninety percent of those are harmless cysts, nodules, and other changes that do not affect your health. Roughly thirty percent of people get sent for more investigation. Fewer than two percent are even suspected of cancer, and about one percent turn out to have it.

You walk in carrying a vague, unanswerable question, am I sick, and you walk out carrying a specific, named, scheduled one: what is this 0.4-centimeter thing on my kidney, and when do we look again.

That relocation is the product. A finding can do exactly four things to you. You can name it and carry it. You can watch it. You can chase it. You can act on it. Only the last, and rarely, is the catch the advertisement promises. The other three are the ordinary output of looking everywhere at once, and they are where most of the money and most of the worry actually go.

## Name it and carry it

A healthy woman in her early twenties, someone who trains most days of the week, tried the scan out of curiosity and a low hum of health anxiety. The office felt like a spa, with calming music. She changed into scrubs in a room that locked, lay under a weighted cover, wore a mask fitted with a mirror and headphones so she could watch Netflix, and held a panic button. The portal report arrived in less than one business day. The verdict: normal for her age.

And yet the report named things. A deviated nasal septum. A seven-millimeter lesion in the sinus, consistent with a mucus retention cyst. A 0.4-centimeter spot on the left kidney, consistent with a cyst. A hemangioma, a benign tangle of blood vessels, in a bone of her spine. None of it needed treatment. She left with a list of small facts about herself that are now hers to remember, and the standing invitation, built into the product, to come back and do it all again next year. That is the ordinary outcome of a full-body scan: an inventory, plus a date to come back and read it over.

## Watch it

A woman in Chicago had the scan, in a hospital gown, and four days later her phone rang. Something had been found. She needed a CT. The CT showed what she later called a very tiny aneurysm. A neurologist told her it was small enough that there was no emergency. She now has a scan booked for the following year to make sure it has not grown, has been told to watch her blood pressure and her stress, and to call 911 if she gets a sudden severe headache or loses her sight.

Nothing was cut. Nothing was cured. A well day was converted into a surveillance schedule and a short list of symptoms to be afraid of. This is the quiet version of what the scan sells: a date on the calendar, and a lowered threshold for fear. Peace of mind arrives as a recurring appointment.

## Chase it

Sometimes the finding has teeth. A magazine editor who took the scan got a call, a week before her official results, about a potentially urgent lab value: a fasting glucose of 38, drawn after she had fasted until a mid-afternoon appointment. Her scan flagged hemorrhagic ovarian cysts as a moderate finding, and a note on her brain reading "FLAIR hyperintense lesion, right frontal lobe," which the patient app translated as "small vessel ischemia," and which the reviewing doctor cautioned did not always match the clinical read. That doctor's own summary of the whole exercise was honest: it is a see-a-lot test, not a see-everything test.

The chase is not free, and it is not always benign. In a large population study, people who were told about an incidental finding went on to have more biopsies than people who were not, and more of those biopsies came back showing nothing wrong. The scan itself is non-invasive. The needle it eventually sends into a kidney or a prostate is not, and a biopsy carries its own small risks of bleeding, infection, and injury. A scan advertised as the safe way to look inside you can end with a piece of you removed to be examined, because the picture raised a question the picture could not answer.

## Act on it

Now the case I asked you to hold, in full. It cuts against everything above, and it deserves its full weight.

The reality-television figure did not post his scan results, he later explained, because the scan had found a lesion in his brain. A dedicated brain MRI followed. There was a blueberry-sized lesion, he said, that looked to be a glioma, a kind of tumor. The next step was a craniotomy to remove it and test it. "Definitely wasn't expecting this," he said. "I think it's one of those things where you're like, oh, something like this'll never happen to me, and here I am." His wife wrote that no matter what happened, they would face it together.

This is the strongest possible argument for the scan, and it is not a statistical one. Sometimes a person who feels perfectly well is not, and the image is the first thing to raise the question. A neuroradiologist's version of the case is exactly this: a scan can catch a cancer before it spreads, an aneurysm before it bleeds, a narrowed artery before it strokes. No amount of overdiagnosis math dissolves the man on the table with a real thing in his head.

But look closely at what this case proves and what it does not. It proves the scan can find something serious in someone who felt fine. It does not prove that scanning millions of well people saves more of them than it harms. Those are different claims, and the entire argument lives in the gap between them. Finding a cancer earlier is not the same as saving a life. A screening test can make survival look longer simply by starting the clock sooner, without moving the day you die. It tends to catch the slow, indolent cancers and miss the fast, lethal ones, because the slow ones sit still long enough to be caught. The only way to know a test actually saves lives is to run the hard experiment and count the deaths in both groups. For full-body MRI in average-risk healthy adults, that experiment has not been done.

## The finding the brochure never mentions

There is a fifth output, and it is the clean report that may have been wrong.

A man paid about twenty-five hundred dollars for a whole-body scan that reported no adverse finding in his brain. Months later he had a massive stroke. In a lawsuit that is still unresolved, he alleges the scan had in fact shown a sixty percent narrowing in a major brain artery that no one flagged. The claim has not been proven in court, so hold it lightly. But the possibility is the point. A clean scan is only the absence of a flag, which is not the absence of disease.

The companies say as much in their own fine print. One lists what its scan can miss: very small cancers, lesions in the lining of the mouth and throat and gut, the heart and its vessels, fine lung detail, colon polyps, and some of the smaller vessels in the brain. It does not replace a mammogram, a colonoscopy, or a low-dose lung CT. So a normal result is its own kind of task, the task of believing you are fine on the strength of a test that never claimed to see everything.

## The test you can carry out the door

Here is the rule the cases hand you. When someone offers to look inside you, do not ask what the test can find. Ask what a finding would do to you, and whether finding it earlier would change what happens to you.

For a few tests we can answer that, because someone did the work. Low-dose CT scans of heavy smokers cut lung-cancer deaths by about twenty percent, roughly three fewer deaths per thousand people screened over seven years, a real number earned in a trial of more than fifty thousand people. Even then, a quarter of those scans came back positive and most of the positives led to more tests. That is what a screening test with a proven benefit looks like: a defined group at real risk, a measured drop in death, and a known price paid in false alarms. Full-body MRI in average-risk adults has the false alarms. What it does not have, yet, is the measured drop in death. The strongest reviews call the evidence heterogeneous and at serious risk of bias, with no long-term outcome data and no proof it prolongs life or saves money. In 2023 the American College of Radiology said the evidence was not sufficient to recommend total-body screening for people with no symptoms, risk factors, or family history.

None of this is new. Before the magnet there was the whole-body CT boom, and a 2005 model of scanning healthy fifty-year-old men found it would buy, on average, about six days of extra life at roughly twenty-five hundred dollars a head. Medicine even has a word for what these scans keep producing: the incidentaloma, the abnormality you were not looking for and found anyway, almost always harmless, each one a small engine for the next test.

The exception is precise, and it proves the rule the marketing blurs. People born with a syndrome like Li-Fraumeni carry a cancer risk that passes ninety percent in women by age fifty, and for them, annual whole-body MRI is surveillance that measurably improves survival. Point the same machine at a healthy thirty-year-old with no family history and you have changed what it is. Same pictures. Different product. The value was never in the scanner. It was in the risk of the person lying in it.

Even the promised calm is not quite what it seems. Among people told they had an incidental finding, about one in ten reported strong distress while they waited weeks for results, and nearly three in ten reported moderate-to-severe distress once the results came. The honest counter is that the same line of research found no measurable difference in mood or quality of life two and a half years later, and that most people, including the frightened ones, were very satisfied with the exam. But mostly temporary dread, sold by the appointment, is a strange thing to file under peace of mind.

## The question, sharper than before

Which returns us to the man with the blueberry-sized lesion. The last time he spoke publicly, the surgery had been pushed back a week. He had switched hospitals, and he had taken up running, training for a marathon, because he wanted something to aim at during, in his words, all this brain stuff. He called the delay the waiting game, and said it was tricky, because part of him wanted it over and part of him kept asking why he was rushing to have someone open his skull. The operation had not yet happened. What the thing in his head would turn out to be, how early, how dangerous, whether the scan bought him years or just handed him the worst month of his life ahead of schedule, none of that was known. It still is not.

He does not know either. That is the ending the brochure cannot print. He went in to buy certainty, and what he walked out with was a question, sharper than the one he arrived with, and no way yet to answer it. The rest of us are being sold the same trade, one healthy body at a time, and told it is the same thing as knowing.
