In a semester course, a student team had been shipping features and meeting milestones. By weeks 7 or 8 they hit a wall. A simple change now broke something they hadn't expected. When their teacher met with them, they blamed messy code and poor architecture. The real problem surfaced slower: no one could say why the system was built the way it was, or how its parts fit together. The product still ran. Its builders were afraid to touch it.

That fear is the argument. Code no person can explain is debt, whatever wrote it. Writing by hand tends to force a rough model to form, because you can't fight a thing into working without learning how it works. A coding agent can hand you a branch after the checks have run and let you merge it without building that model. The trouble was never that a machine wrote the code. It is that no accountable person owns the explanation.

So book the generated change as debt until someone can say, before you ask, what it changed, why, and where it will break.

Green checks are not that someone. Short of one, you don't have software. You have a running product, and a team deciding who is brave enough to touch it.
