Washing your face is one of the few things a person can still finish. There is a rinse, a towel, a moment when the task is plainly over and you step away from the sink. A haircut ends. A workout ends. Even the vainest grooming has a floor you reach and walk off.

Now keep the same sink, the same boy, the same ordinary wish to look a little better, and add one thing: a number that rates his face, and a place to send the number so it comes back rated again. The wish has not changed. The stopping point has. There is no rinse that ends it now, because the task was never really the face. The task was the score.

That is the part of the worry over looksmaxxing that keeps getting missed. The alarm usually points at the extremes: boys tapping their cheekbones with hammers, ordering testosterone off the internet, chasing a jaw with a chin implant. Those cases are real. They are also the visible edge of something more ordinary and easier to miss, and the ordinary version is where the actual mechanism lives. Looksmaxxing is dangerous not because it makes boys vain, but because it turns a face into homework: something measured, ranked, improved, and never marked done.

Start with the puzzle, because it is a real one. The soft core of looksmaxxing is, on its own terms, good advice. Wash your face. Get a better haircut. A lot of it is what a doctor would tell a teenager anyway: treat the acne, lift with good form and supervision, move your body every day, see a dentist about your bite instead of trying to fix it yourself. And the boys themselves often report that it helps. One boy who ran a daily jaw routine described it as "more like a self-care, self-improvement session," and said the results left him "more satisfied with myself, a little more confident." If the advice is sound and the boy feels better, where is the harm supposed to be? Clinicians are worried anyway. The puzzle is why.

Take the easy answers one at a time. None of them holds.

It is not that boys suddenly became vain. The wish underneath is old and was mostly neglected. Boys were handed almost no language for wanting to look less awkward, and told to man up if they raised it. One clinical guide frames the pull as "more about belonging than it is about beauty." Wanting clearer skin and a body you chose is not a pathology. It is adolescence.

It is not the extreme stunts. Bone smashing gets the headlines and the view counts, but even the clinics sounding the alarm call it uncommon, and a prominent looksmaxxing influencer says there is "little evidence that anyone is actually doing it." Much of the soft edge looks like cleanser and gym and a haircut reference saved to a phone.

It is not simply the misogyny of the origins. The practice was born in incel forums, and that lineage matters. How far the mainstream version has moved from those roots is genuinely unclear: some who promote it say the woman-hating got left behind, yet the ranking ladder, the one that sorts faces from "subhuman" to "Chad," came through intact. Whatever was shed, the danger was not in the part that got cleaned up.

Comparison does not explain it either. Adolescence has always run on comparison; if that were the whole story, there would be nothing here worth a fresh essay.

Rule those out and one explanation is left standing. What is new is the scoreboard. The face got a number, and the number got a place to go.

Here is the mechanism in its strongest form, stated so you can test it. Ordinary self-care tips toward the trap as four things attach to it. First, a measuring vocabulary: not "I want to look better" but named parts scored against named ideals, eye shape and jaw angle and canthal tilt, plus a rating ladder, carried out of the old forums, that sorts whole faces from "subhuman" to "Chad." Second, an audience that hands the measure back: a forum thread, a peer group, an app, a bot built to judge faces. Third, a record to beat: the progress photo, the before-and-after, the saved side profile. Fourth, the promise that the next small intervention will move the number. None of the four is strictly required, and the habit can turn without all of them. But lock all four together and you have the pure case: the face becomes a task with a grade and no final page.

That assembly is the real history of the thing, and it is stranger than "boys got insecure." The incel forums it came from dealt in verdicts. Your bones are your fate; you are doomed; sit with it. The mainstream version kept the ranking and bolted an assignment onto it. The score can still be brutal, but now there are steps to take before tomorrow. A verdict became homework. That sounds like mercy. It is the trap.

So the claim is narrow and, I think, worth disputing: the harm is not in the content of the assignment, and it is not measurement itself. A dermatologist grades acne; a dentist scores a bite; a coach tracks a lift. Those measures have a floor, and someone qualified holds the ruler. What looksmaxxing does is different. It turns the ruler on the features that mark a boy's rank among other boys, hands it to him to use on himself, and attaches no floor at all. That a face has been made an assignment at all is the whole problem. A finite fix has a floor: treat the acne and you are done. A rank does not; there is always another point to chase. Strip out every lie and every cruelty, and as long as an endless rank governs the habit, the shape of the damage stays.

Look at what the assignment does to one day.

It began, for one boy, in seventh grade, when he noticed other boys had sharper jawlines than his and thought he would like to get closer to that. TikTok showed him rubber chewing blocks and chin exercises. He took up a jaw routine he found online, which involved reciting the alphabet while stretching the muscles. This was during the pandemic, so his classes came over Zoom. When they were done for the day he would go into the bathroom and run the whole routine, and it would take about an hour sometimes. He did it every day after class. He took photos to track the progress. And he put it plainly: "I didn't feel like I was done with school until I finished my jawline routine."

There is no crisis here, no hospital. Just a boy folding a face routine into the end of the school day, the way you might a chore, the last item before he was free. That is what a scored face looks like from the inside. It is not drama. It is a to-do list with his own jaw on it.

Now set the machine beside him. Jaw exercises like his are sold as a way to sharpen a jawline, and there is no evidence they reshape facial bone; one orthodontists' group calls the science behind such jawline claims "as thin as dental floss." The exercise might tone a muscle, or steady him a little, and he says it gave him something. What it could not do is remake the bone. He did it anyway, every day. Whatever he got from it, the structure around the routine did not need the jaw to change. It needed the routine performed, the photo taken, something sent toward the score. And notice what he is not: he is not a cautionary tale who regrets it. He says it helped. Both things are true at once, and the second one is what makes the first so hard to see.

Run the same template on an older case and you watch the final page recede. A man in his twenties, in finance, got pulled in around sixteen. He learned the words for features he had never noticed: short chin, wide nose, high hairline, eyes set too far apart. His line for what the vocabulary did is the whole mechanism in a breath: "You don't see a lot of these things until someone else points them out and then you can't stop seeing them." He started soft, with skincare and diet and hair. Then a nose job. Then Botox, threaded eyebrows, teeth whitened and straightened. Around ten thousand pounds on his own face. When last interviewed he was weighing chin surgery to extend his chin vertically by a few millimetres, and he said he hoped it "would be the last big thing," so he could "move on. That's what I'm hoping, anyway." Each fix is sold, to himself, as the one that finally closes the file. The file does not close. That is not a flaw in his willpower. It is what a number is for.

And it can end somewhere physical. A maxillofacial surgeon who does that chin operation about once a fortnight described it plainly: go in through the mouth under the lower front teeth, saw off the end of the chin bone, move it, hold it with a titanium plate, let the bone grow back into the gap. That is the far end of the word "jawline." It starts as a word for a jaw, a thing chased with chewing gum and rubber blocks, and ends, for some, as a bone sawed and pinned. The surgeon, to his credit, vets people over several meetings and turns some of them away.

Then there is the version where the human judge has been removed entirely and the scoreboard runs itself. A nineteen-year-old watched a stranger get his face rated in a live "mog-off" at four in the morning, opened the site the next day, and was matched against another stranger while green dots crawled across both their faces and the software compared their canthal tilt and the ratio of nose to face. It scores faces one to ten. It lets one face "dominate" another, awards points, and sorts everyone into a running rank, the way a chess ladder does. A week in he had played hundreds of these and was on a two-hundred-win streak. Elsewhere, boys point a face-analysis app at themselves and get back a rating and a list of what to work on; the popular ones carry tens of thousands of reviews. This is the endpoint the scoring logic points at: a face converted into a number, continuously, by a machine that never closes for the night and never says enough.

The numbers around all this are softer than the panic wants, and honesty means saying so. The best survey we have, of a little over three thousand young men across three countries, does not even measure looksmaxxing; it measures how many follow masculinity influencers, which is a wider, blurrier thing. Nearly two in three do. And the group most drawn to that feed came back looking contradictory: more optimistic about their own futures, sixty-four percent against fifty-eight, and at the same time more likely to work out while injured, forty-seven against thirty-nine, and to have used steroids, nineteen against eleven. The same feed, more hope and more harm at once. The study is a snapshot and cannot prove the feed caused any of it. There is no clean count of how many boys run jaw routines after class. What we can measure is the weather around the thing, not the thing itself, and no one has shown that the content hands a boy the disorder.

What we do know is the shape. The behavior clinicians already watch for is checking, comparing, seeking reassurance, and doing it for hours a day; the preoccupation settles, more than anywhere else, on the face; and it lands hardest in the years when the face is a moving target and self-worth is thin. Looksmaxxing did not invent that loop. It packaged it, gave it a vocabulary and an app, and handed it to boys as a form of self-improvement.

Which brings the argument to its hardest test, the case that should have gone wrong and didn't. Take the same wish and the same habits, pull out the scoreboard, and the harm does not show up. A fifteen-year-old hears his classmates talking about cologne at lunch and in the hallways, gets curious, borrows his father's bottle of Armani, goes to the mall with friends, and learns to tell parfum from eau de parfum. Same age, same impulse to present better, same self-care. But there is no rank, no photo to beat, no app returning a verdict. It ends when he caps the bottle; he says it is "about confidence" and moves on with his afternoon. Or the thirteen-year-old who tried mewing, decided he did not care for it, and shrugged that it "kind of just became kind of a meme." He held the measure loosely, so it never took hold of him. These are not controlled experiments, only cases, but they keep pointing the same way: nearly the same inputs, minus the scoreboard, and the thing stays small. The grooming was never the load-bearing part. The scoreboard was.

So here is a test you can carry out of this. A psychotherapist quoted on these maxxing trends offers the cleanest version of it: "If no one was looking, or no one was able to see the quantifiable results, would I still be pushing this hard?" That question is the whole border between the two faces of the same habit. On one side, self-care that ends when you leave the bathroom. On the other, a score that asks you to come back. The soap tells you when you are done. The number never does.

And this is why the narrow claim matters more than the extremes. If the harm were the hammers and the hormones and the woman-hating, you could imagine fixing it: better moderation, honest science, a kinder app. But clean up the misogyny, throw out the pseudoscience, make the grooming advice medically sound, and you have not solved the thing. You have only built a tidier machine for never being finished. A scale can stop at ten and still have no real top, because the next comparison always reopens the file. Reopening is the whole point of the loop.

Go back to the boy in the bathroom, now that you know what he was doing there. The Zoom classes are over for the day. He is done with school. He is not done. He recites the alphabet and stretches the muscles, an hour some days, on a jaw that at that age is still, on its own schedule, becoming whatever it is going to be. He takes a photo for the record. He says it made him more confident, and maybe it did. What does it do to a person to spend the very years his face is being made treating that face as a thing to be graded, by a grader with no last page?
