Groceries break the oldest rule of borrowing: the thing you buy should outlast the payments. A financed mattress is still under you when the last installment clears; a week's dinner is gone long before the fourth payment lands.

In self-reported surveys, nearly a third of pay-later users, 29%, have used the plans for groceries, more than double the share of two years ago, and nearly half, 47%, paid late in the past year. Call it recklessness and you miss it. A borrower interviewed this summer walked through the loop: she can never get caught up; she pays an installment, another cost lands, the money is gone, she still needs food, so she finances the groceries again. "That balance is never going away."

A no-interest plan really can be cheaper than a card, which is a claim about cost. Whether food should be financed is a claim about time. The checkout is selling something stranger than cheap credit: the few seconds of feeling paid up at the very moment the paying starts. Groceries are the one thing gone before the bill is, so the feeling has to be bought back, week after week, right about the time you need groceries again.
