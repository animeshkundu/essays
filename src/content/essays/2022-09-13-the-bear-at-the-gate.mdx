Five days after the Queen died, Royal Parks, the body that oversees Green Park, asked the public to please stop leaving marmalade sandwiches.

Teddy bears and balloons too. But it was the sandwiches that gave the notice its strange weight. They had begun to go moldy among the flowers. The parks said the food was bad for the wildlife. So the people at the gate were asked, politely, to stop leaving food out for a dead woman.

I cannot decide whether that is the silliest thing people have brought to those gates this week or the most honest one, and five days in I have given up trying to keep the two apart.

The sandwiches have a source. In June, for the Platinum Jubilee, the palace released a short film it had kept secret for months. The Queen sits down to tea with Paddington Bear. Paddington lifts his red hat and shows the marmalade sandwich he keeps under it. The Queen opens her handbag and says she keeps hers in there. Then the two of them tap their china teacups with spoons in time to a rock song. The palace said afterward that inviting the bear to tea had been "too fun to miss." The coverage this week called the sketch one of the most joyous moments of a Jubilee that, even at the time, had a valedictory tone. After the death, the film looked different again.

So the bear at the gate is not random. It is a quotation. This week Paddington was everywhere among the thousands of bouquets outside the palace: the toy in the red hat and blue duffel coat, held by a woman who said, simply, "Paddington is a British institution." There were toy corgis. There were drawings, many of them; the youngest children just coloured in the bear. One child drew Paddington by Big Ben, next to the Queen in her crown. One drew him under a rainbow: "we will always miss you." And one drew the Queen from behind, holding the bear's hand, with the words: "I've done my duties Paddington, please take me to my husband."

That one stops you. The words are the child's.

I have been trying to work out what all these objects are actually for, because the obvious answers do not hold. A toy cannot tell you what the person who left it felt. It can only tell you that a public form was there, and that a great many people reached for it.

The first obvious answer is that this is sentiment, grown adults indulging in kitsch. But the child's drawing is not kitsch. It is about dying, in a child's hand. Whatever else is happening, real feeling is passing through these silly objects, not being faked by them.

The second answer is that this is ordinary celebrity mourning, the same reflex that fills a pavement whenever a famous person dies. That is closer. But listen to what the mourners actually say, because they do not talk about a celebrity. They talk about time. A man on The Mall the night she died said he had never known anyone else, that he had never known a king. A woman said they had all felt so secure, for so long, in how she did things. A young hairdresser said he had somehow thought she would live forever. Even people who said they were not monarchists respected her for one thing: continuity. The people quoted here are not describing a personality they knew. They are describing time, security, continuity. Sixty-nine percent of Britons told a poll this week that they had never once met or even seen her. They are grieving a fixed point.

The third answer, and the strongest, is that none of this is spontaneous at all. The bear is a professionally produced meme, secret for months, then broadcast to millions. The tribute simply followed the distribution of a recent television image. And within days the beloved symbol had become a wildlife problem the parks had to manage. This is the deflating, accurate account, and it is right about the mechanism. But it explains the wrong thing. It explains why the object is Paddington and not something else. It does not explain why a handed-down image, a bear and a sandwich, slotted so instantly into a grief that clearly came before it. The sketch worked, in June and now, because it married the newest thing, a palace broadcast, to some of the most familiar private things there are: a children's bear, a sandwich, a cup of tea. It gave people a socially safe container for something they had no words for.

Which is the thing I keep arriving at. These people are not mourning a woman. They are mourning a container.

For seventy years her face arrived at the most ordinary intervals, in the most ordinary places there are. It was on the coins going soft in a pocket and on the stamp licked onto a birthday card. The same profile has been printed on British stamps something over two hundred billion times. Her initials were on the postboxes. Her face was on the banknotes in a wallet, and had been since 1960. Once a year, on Christmas Day, her voice came out of the television into the front room. When she was crowned in 1953, twenty-seven million people watched, out of a country of thirty-six million. That is not fame. Fame is a spike. This was the opposite of a spike. It was a low, steady hum under everything, for longer than most people alive have been alive, and a hum is exactly the kind of thing you stop hearing until it stops.

A scholar who studies brands put the mechanism more precisely than any mourner could. The monarchy's legal ownership, he wrote, sits with the monarchy. Its emotional ownership sits with the people. That sentence is the whole strange transaction. People had been quietly lodging their private years inside a public institution they did not own a single share of. The bear and the sandwich at the gate are what people bring when that account is suddenly closed.

Here is what I think the mourning is really about. The deepest thing a long-lived institution gives ordinary people is not what it decides or how it is run. It is that it holds still long enough for them to store part of their own lives inside it. And the price of that, hidden the whole time, is that when the institution finally changes, they find out that a piece of their private life was being kept somewhere they never owned and cannot follow.

You can already watch it being taken back. The coins will turn over across years, because there are too many to recall at once. The old stamps stay valid. The postboxes already standing will not be changed, and some still being made will go up wearing her initials anyway. And some of it changed overnight, while people slept: Queen's Counsel became King's Counsel by the next morning, and an usher at the Old Bailey said "God save the King" for the first time since 1952. The small nouns you grew up inside were edited without your consent, because they were never yours to keep.

I should be honest that the container did not hold the same thing for everyone, and for some it held nothing warm at all. When she took the throne in 1952, more than seven hundred million people lived under British imperial power, and what the institution stored for many of them was not private tea but public injury. Even at home the week had an edge under the softness. In a public square a barrister stood with a police officer and a blank sheet of paper, working out whether he could be arrested for writing three words on it. Not everyone was ever invited to keep the same thing inside the container, and what some kept there was not tender. That does not break the thesis. It sharpens it. The reason an institution matters so much is precisely that private meaning collects inside it, and private meaning is never evenly shared.

And yet the plainest evidence is the mourners' own account, and I do not think it should be explained away. Before she died, eighty-one percent of Britons told a poll they saw her positively. After, seventy-six percent said they were at least a little upset, and forty-four percent that they had welled up or cried. One woman travelled four hours by train from the north to stand at the gate. She did not say she was performing a national mood. She said the sketch was the last time the Queen did anything public, and that it had made people feel an affinity. You can file that under habit if you like. But a habit strong enough to put someone on a four-hour train is not a small thing. It is a whole life of tiny contact, coming due.

The sandwiches had begun to go bad in the flower beds, and the parks were asking people, kindly, to stop. What they were being asked to stop was feeding her.

So I will leave it where the child left it, and just turn it around to face you. Somewhere in your life is a thing you have poured years into and do not own: a song that belongs to the band, a place that belongs to a company, a holiday that belongs to a calendar someone else prints. You will not know how much of yourself you left there until the day it changes hands. That drawing of the Queen walking off with the bear got there first, without any of this argument. You give a thing your years. One day it walks off with them. And you find yourself standing at a gate you cannot pass through, holding a bear, not entirely sure who it was for.
