On a September morning in 2025, a developer who helps maintain some of the most-used code on the internet checked his phone in the middle of a long week and a panicky morning. There was an email. It said his account on the npm registry needed a two-factor reset, and it came from support at a domain, npmjs.help, that someone had registered three days earlier. In his own words, it looked shockingly authentic. He was on his phone, trying to knock one task off a list, so he tapped the link instead of typing the real address himself. It slipped past him.

Within minutes, eighteen packages he helped maintain got new versions. The names are ones almost any JavaScript developer would recognize: chalk, debug, ansi-styles, the small quiet utilities that sit three layers down under everything. Together they were downloaded more than two billion times a week. The new versions looked like dull patch bumps. Inside, a file had been rewritten with scrambled code that ran in the browser, watched for cryptocurrency wallet addresses on the page, and swapped them for the attacker's own. It even chose a look-alike, an address close enough to the real one that a person glancing at it would not flinch. The screen still looked correct. The money would just go somewhere else.

The eighteen poisoned versions went out in a single tight burst, all between 13:12 and 13:20 UTC. The core window in which they were live was roughly two hours before npm pulled them. The teams best protected that morning were not the ones with the sharpest scanners or the fastest response. They were the ones who happened to be a little behind.

The rule we were taught runs the other way. Stay current. An out-of-date dependency was the thing that got you. That belief was not ancient; it was built up over about a decade of tooling. Semantic versioning gave releases numbers you could reason about. Lockfiles, standard in npm by 2017, made a dependency tree reproducible and auditable. Bots arrived to watch registries and turn eligible new releases into pull requests, and security alerts learned to flag any package that fell behind a known fix. When one of those bots launched its routine-update feature, it opened with the creed in a single sentence: keeping your dependencies updated is one of the easiest ways to keep the software you build secure. Lag was neglect. Freshness was hygiene.

So here is the strange thing. In July 2026, one of those bots, GitHub's Dependabot, started doing the neglectful thing on purpose. For ordinary version bumps, the ones with no known vulnerability attached, it now waits at least three days after a release is published before it will even open a pull request. The company that spent years turning freshness into a virtue now ships lateness as the default. A habit that strong does not reverse itself for no reason.

A reversal that sharp needs a reason, and the dull ones do not fit. It is not that updates suddenly matter less: security updates, the ones tied to a known vulnerability with a patch already out, still open the moment they land, exactly as before. And it is not some rare edge case being papered over. In the year ending May 2026, the advisory database published more than six thousand five hundred npm malware advisories. The problem is not exotic. It is a daily weather pattern. What GitHub actually says is narrower and stranger: three days is meant to push a project past the short window where many of these poisoned releases live and die.

Strip the dull explanations away and one is left standing. The threat model inverted. For most of the history of dependency management, the dangerous package was the old one: the version sitting in your lockfile with a hole in it that everyone, including attackers, already knew about. Every day you waited on that was a day you were exposed on purpose. But the September attack, and a run of others like it, points the other way. The dangerous package can now be the brand-new release that nobody has had time to distrust yet.

Follow the mechanism and you see why speed became the liability. An attacker gets a publishing credential, sometimes by stealing a token, sometimes, as in this case, by phishing a tired human. They push a new version. Because many projects accept new releases inside a version range, that version is instantly eligible to be installed without anyone typing its number. A bot built to grab the newest release sees it, opens a pull request, and a continuous integration server runs the install, which can execute the package's own scripts. Two hours is plenty of time for all of that. The reflex we had trained, be first, be current, be fast, was pointed straight into the one window where being fast is exactly wrong. Speed had been standing in for diligence. Quietly, it had become the attack surface instead.

The case for waiting is not that three days is a magic number. It is simpler and harder to argue with. When you line up the recent mass-impact registry attacks, their live windows are measured in hours, not days. The September wallet-rewriting incident: about two hours. A compromise of a widely used blockchain library in December 2024: roughly five. A poisoned release of a popular HTTP client in 2026: about four. A hijacked browser-detection package back in 2021: about four. A three-day pause steps clear over every one of those windows. An independent review of twenty-one well-known supply-chain incidents from 2018 to 2026 estimated that a seven-day age gate would likely have blocked the bad install in eleven of them and narrowed the window in two more. The author is careful to say it is not a controlled study, and it is not. But you do not need a controlled study to notice that a three-day wait beats a two-hour poisoning.

The defense could be as thin as an accident. One downstream team caught the malicious version of debug only by accident: by the time their build ran, npm had already pulled the package, so the build failed looking for a version that no longer existed. The broken build was the warning. They were saved not by a security tool but by being slightly slow and slightly lucky. The cooldown is an attempt to make that luck into a policy.

Now the honest part, because a delay dressed up as wisdom deserves the strongest push. The best argument against waiting is not "never wait." It is that waiting, aimed at the wrong target, destroys a real defense. For a known vulnerability with a fix already published, every day of delay is a day you are exposed on purpose, which is precisely the exposure the old guidance spent years trying to close. That is why the cooldown pointedly does not touch security updates, and why serious teams can set it to zero. Some teams are genuinely safer taking the update the moment it lands: the internal library owners and compatibility testers whose actual job is to find breakage early.

And there is a deeper trap. A cooldown is a timer, not a test. It proves that a release is three days old, nothing more. If a pipeline treats the clock running out as a green light, the danger has not left; it has only changed shape, which is exactly what has happened in setups where the age check passed before the real test suite had even started. Waiting can also strand you: an aged version opens a pull request, a bug turns up, the fix ships a day later, and now you can see the fix but cannot pull it until it clears its own three-day clock.

And the wait catches none of the patient attacks, which are the frightening ones. In 2018, malicious code hid inside a popular package for over two months before anyone noticed. A three-day pause would have waved it straight through. When a maintainer sabotages their own package, or leaves a backdoor dormant for a season, or the build system itself is compromised, the clock does nothing at all. So waiting is not safety. It is quarantine: narrow, with an end date and an escape hatch. It buys time against exactly one kind of attacker, the smash-and-grab one who publishes something poisonous and gets caught fast.

Which means the real move here is not "slow down." It is that the responsible act split in two, and we had been treating it as one. Fast for the old, known-bad version. Patient for the new, unknown one. Both instincts were right; they were just answers to different threats we had bundled under a single word, hygiene. The whole difficulty is teaching a bot, and a tired reviewer at nine in the morning, which of the two is actually on the screen.

Notice how well the old reflex hid this from us. For years we read speed as care. A fresh lockfile felt responsible. We rewarded the people and the machines that got there first, and never noticed we were rewarding being first, not being right.

So go back to that morning. The safest thing thousands of teams did was nothing. They did not scan the poisoned release or race to catch it. They simply were not first in line, and the line was where the danger was. That should feel like relief, and it mostly does, until you notice what the whole thing rests on. Waiting only works because someone else refuses to wait. Some of them go first on purpose: the researcher watching the registry, the maintainer who wakes to the alarm, the scanner that trips on the file, the registry team that pulls the version. Others were just early, installing the release fresh before any warning existed. The three days you take are borrowed from both. The cooldown does not remove the risk. It moves it upstream, onto whoever still goes first. Being a little late is a fine defense, as long as you can count on someone else being right on time.
