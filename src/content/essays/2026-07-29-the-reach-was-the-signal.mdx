Meta keeps defending a light.

It is a small white bulb on the front of the frame. The company made it bigger than the one before it, two millimeters instead of nine tenths of one. It blinks once for a photo and keeps blinking for the length of a video. It has no off switch. Meta says it tested the brightness so you could catch it in daylight. This summer the company went one step harder: try to cover the light, and the camera stops; try to pry it out or destroy it, and now the glasses shut the camera off entirely. Whole news cycles have turned on whether that light is bright enough, honest enough, hard enough to beat.

It is the wrong argument. The light was never the thing we lost.

What we lost was a gesture. For years, without anyone deciding it, the smartphone taught everyone near you a piece of body language: to record me, a person had to reach, lift a rectangle, aim it, and go still. That reach was a signal. It was crude and it was honest, and it handed the people around you a half second to step out of the shot before the shot existed. Camera glasses do not erase that half second by recording more. They erase it by making the act of recording look exactly like the act of looking.

Here is what does not fit. Meta keeps hardening the signal, and the worry does not go away. In the back half of 2025, shipments of smart glasses grew 139 percent over the year before, and Meta took 82 percent of the market. Roughly seven million pairs sold across the year, by widely reported counts. Reuters still called the whole category a small market and a specialty gadget as late as last November, so this is fast growth from not very much. The sales climb and the unease sit in the same room. Privacy is now the thing a rival company advertises against and the thing Meta defends with a hardware redesign, even while the glasses sell better than ever. No one has cleanly counted whether these glasses leave the people around them more or less comfortable in an ordinary room; what the record shows is a real, documented discomfort with no tidy number under it. A brighter light was supposed to settle it. It did not.

The easy explanations do not hold.

It is not simple fear of a new machine. We ran this exact experiment with the camera phone. The first one shipped in 2000. By 2003 and 2004 the headlines were already about candid cellphone cameras and a camera phone backlash, locker rooms and health clubs and who might be shooting whom. And then we adapted. We learned the new object, and the panic drained away. It drained away because there was something to learn: a phone raised toward you meant one thing, a phone in a pocket meant another. We got a cue, and we read it.

It is not fear of being recorded as such, either. We own the recorders. Nine in ten American adults own a smartphone. We film our own dinners and our own children and our own faces, we post the clips, we go live. If the raw fact of a camera in a public room were the wound, we would have quit years ago. We did not quit. We made it a habit.

And it is not only that the light can be missed, though it can. A reporter wrote this year that he stood talking with someone wearing the glasses and never saw the indicator until the wearer pointed it out. Fair. But Meta made the light bigger and brighter, and the unease did not lift. It would not lift even if the indicator were perfect.

The reason is that the light asks the bystander to do work the raised arm never asked of them. To read the light you have to know the product exists, catch a small blink, stand close enough, sit out of the sun, look at the right point on a stranger's face, and trust that the blink is telling the truth. The raised arm asked for none of that. You did not need to know the make of the phone. You did not need to be told what a lifted rectangle meant. You just had to see an arm go up. One signal lived in the world, where a body announced it. The other lives on a chip, and asks a stranger to become an expert in a device they never bought.

It gets thinner than that. Meta's own bystander document splits the camera into two jobs. There is active capture, the saved photo or video, and there the light is on. And there are the AI features, where the camera looks at the world and reads it, a parking sign, keys lost in a room, the label on a bottle, and there the light can be off. The world it reads includes whoever happens to be standing in it. Their stated reason is almost too honest: if the light blinked every time the camera looked, people would learn to ignore it. So the company that built the signal is now rationing it, on purpose, to keep it meaningful. Which means the plain rule a bystander would reach for, no light means the camera is not working, is not true. No light can mean the camera is looking and simply not saving.

The whole history of recording is the history of this signal going quiet. In 1888, Kodak put a camera in ordinary hands and promised you press the button, we do the rest. Easy, but you still held up a box and pressed the button. In 2000, the camera climbed inside the phone, an object you also used for a hundred things that were not photography, and its presence stopped telling anyone your intent. In 2013, a cafe in Seattle barred anyone wearing Google Glass, and the owner said out loud the sentence every later fight would circle. With a camera, people see you doing it, he said, and they can step out. With the glasses, they don't have a chance to do that. He wanted his customers comfortable, not, in his words, like they're being watched. Each step moved the camera nearer to something people already expected to see, until it reached the last hiding place, a pair of sunglasses, a face turned your way.

What that half second was for shows up clearest where the law was never the point.

A woman in Vancouver is stopped on the street at night by a man running a bad pickup line. Who let the dogs out, he asks, then asks again when she looks confused. She is halfway through the confusion when she clocks the glasses, and the thought arrives a beat too late: oh, he probably just filmed that. Nothing illegal happened. She said so herself; she had no expectation of privacy on a public street. What she lost was smaller and worse than a legal right. It was the ability to know, while it was happening, that she might already be someone's content.

In Delhi, in March of 2026, a crowd gathered for a trans rights march. The organizers understood the stakes and posted volunteers to stop people filming, because many in that crowd had not come out to everyone in their lives. A man in camera glasses got the footage anyway. Weeks later one protester, who had been there in a green saree with a shorn head, learned by text, while packing a giant carton during a house move, that a clip of them had traveled across the internet. I'm being clocked everywhere now, they said. Everyone recognizes me from the meme. The stares, they said, came painted with a smirk or angry intolerance. The volunteers had a line and a rule and a plan. The one thing they no longer had, once the camera looked like a face, was the old mechanism that had always done the work: the sight of a raised phone a person could go and stand in front of.

Now the gentle version, which is stranger than the harsh ones. This year a writer took two pairs of these glasses on his honeymoon to a Spanish island. His wife was unsure at first and ended up wearing hers nearly all day, because they were also, simply, good sunglasses. They played music through the arms of the frames without shutting out the world around them. When a bottle of shampoo leaked across a bag, she pointed the glasses at the Spanish label and asked what was in it. No stranger objected the whole trip. And still the friction was there. It had only moved. He kept the camera off the two of them in swimwear. He watched who drifted into the background of his shots. He worried that someone nearby might not want the glasses turned their way.

Look where the gesture went. It did not vanish. It climbed inside one man's head, into a private checklist that only he could see. That is the real trade. The reach used to be out in the open, in the world, where a stranger could read it and act on it. Now it lives in the wearer's conscience, and the person across from you has to hope it is running. Most of the time it will be running. The old signal meant you did not have to hope. You could just look, and know.

Here is the strongest case against all of this. The raised phone was never consent; it was a warning at best, and you missed it too. We have been soaking in cameras for twenty years, and the glasses did not start it. And they can leave a person more present, not less: eyes up instead of buried in a screen, an evening lived instead of managed through a slab held at arm's length. Push it to the case that should stop anyone cold. A blind woman puts on the same glasses and a menu is read to her. A dropped bag is found for her on the floor. A hospital waiting room is described, the museum on the corner is named, the hot dog carts out front noted. A blind grandfather has the glasses read a picture book aloud and describe the drawings, so he can talk with his three-year-old grandson about the colors on the page. The camera that unsettles the stranger is the camera that lets a blind man read his grandson a book.

Grant every word of it. It is true, and it still leaves the loss standing. The feature is one feature in all of these scenes. What lets the grandfather read the book is exactly what unsettles the woman on the street: capture with no reach, a camera that works while the body only looks. You cannot keep the help and delete the loss, because they are the same thing seen from two sides. And the uses are not handed out evenly. The grandfather is real, and so is the man working the sidewalk for footage, and no light on a frame can tell you which of them is sitting across from you. Neither can a rule that pulls the harassment videos down after they post. A punishment after capture is not a signal before it.

So stop arguing about the light. Make it as bright as the sun. It will still be a thing you have to know to look for, on a stranger who has no reason to help you see it.

Notice, instead, the small motion you have already started to miss. The next time you cannot tell whether the person across the table is looking at you or recording you, watch what your own body does. It goes hunting for the raised arm. It reaches for a cue that used to be there, finds nothing, and quietly files the person under maybe. We are not about to lose our privacy in any legal sense; the law is not the thing that changed here. We are losing something the law never gave us and never can, the plain animal ability to tell, in a room, whether we are part of the record. Nobody wrote it down. It was only ever a person lifting a phone, and everyone near them knowing what it meant.
