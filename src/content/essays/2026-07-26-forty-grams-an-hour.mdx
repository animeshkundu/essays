There is a number I have not been able to put down. A traditional stone mill, its face cut with grooves for tea, grinds about forty grams of matcha in an hour. Forty grams is twenty of the little two-gram scoops that go into a cafe cup. So one hour of a stone wheel turning is twenty drinks, before anyone sifts the powder, ships it, or whisks it into hot water. I came to matcha with a smaller question, whether the drink in a cafe cup means anything at all, and somewhere inside the answer I hit that number and could not set it back down.

Here is the scene that put it there. A woman in Dublin fell for matcha the way people fall for things now, sideways: her usual order was sold out, she reached for matcha instead, and then she couldn't find a version of it in her own city she liked, only too much milk or bitter clumps. So she flew to Japan. Her shop tells it plainly. She went, in its words, with a whisk and a passport. She spent time in Uji and Wazuka, walked tea farms, toured factories, drank a cup with farmers whose families had grown tea for five generations, and came home with a supplier and a plan. In July of 2024 she opened a small counter inside an eighteenth-century townhouse in the middle of Dublin, steps from the main shopping street, tucked among the jewelers and the hair salons. It was meant to be a pop-up. Demand held, so she kept it.

Then success arrived, and it arrived as a limit. Her supplier put a cap on how much she could order every few weeks. She had crossed a continent to buy straight from the source, and the source's answer, as the shortage spread, was that she could have less than she wanted.

Here is the part I keep turning over: the more people wanted what she made, the less of it she could get.

The easy explanation is that a fad ate itself. Social media found matcha, made it a color and a prop, and the crush of new buyers is just noise that will pass. I don't believe it, and the reason is that you can see this demand in the places where noise doesn't show up. In June and July of 2025, one payments company counted just over 1.6 million matcha drinks sold across more than two thousand British cafes in two months. At the same shops open a year earlier, the number of matcha transactions had more than doubled. A chain worth around half a billion dollars dropped the word "Coffee" from its name and repainted itself the pale green of a matcha latte. That is not a rumor about a trend. A great many people, in a great many places, are buying the same thing on purpose, again and again.

The second easy explanation is that someone is to blame. A woman posted her honeymoon haul from Japan, a suitcase of pastel boxes and jars, and the comments turned on her. "This is why there is a matcha shortage in Japan," one wrote. It is a satisfying story because it has a villain, and it is also arithmetic that doesn't work. No single suitcase caps a Dublin cafe's standing order. The scolding is easier than the thing it stands in for, which is that the wanting is real and the supply is a physical object that will not hurry.

Because that is the actual bottleneck, and it is boring in the way true things often are. Matcha is not ground-up green tea. It is tencha: leaves grown for weeks under shade cloth that starves them of light on purpose, trading bitterness for sweetness, then steamed, dried without rolling, stripped of stems and veins, and turned to powder on that slow stone wheel. The good leaf is picked once a year, in spring, by hand, often by people whose average age in Japan is somewhere in their sixties. The number of households growing tea there commercially fell from about 54,000 in 2000 to about 12,000 in 2020. You cannot meet a doubling in demand by asking a stone mill to spin faster, because if you speed it up the powder comes out coarse, and the color and the smell leave with the fineness. Forty grams an hour is not a quaint detail. It is the ceiling.

So here is what I have talked myself into, and I know a fair number of people will stop reading right at it. The shortage is not proof that popularity ruined the craft. It is the first time the craft has been counted at all.

For most of its life as a global drink, matcha got to pretend its slowness was free. The shade, the once-a-year picking, the aging farmers, the stone wheel: all of it sat behind a bright powder that a cafe could scoop into ice and oat milk in seconds, and the menu price never really carried the weight of any of it. A cheap thing that is secretly expensive stays cheap only as long as few people want it. The moment enough people want it, the hidden bill comes due. A shortage is a cost becoming visible.

This is true of nearly anything slow that a crowd wants all at once. A quiet town becomes the place everyone wants to live, and suddenly the very thing that made it worth wanting, the space and the quiet, is the thing there isn't enough of. The value was always there. It just went uncounted until demand did the counting. Scarcity is what popularity looks like when it finally runs into a real limit and has to put a number on it.

I want to be fair to the other side, because it is strong, and I am not sure it is wrong. The best case against me is that scale doesn't count the craft at all; it counts the look of the craft. In that version, the shade growing and the hand picking and the stone milling don't get more respected as matcha spreads. They get flattened into words on a menu. "Ceremonial grade," a phrase with no standard meaning in Japan, coined by Western sellers, lets a customer feel they bought reverence without learning a single thing about what they drank. The drink becomes a base for syrup and cold foam and a photograph, and the tea itself ends up the least important thing in the cup. And the shortage doesn't reward the careful. It rewards whoever holds a contract. A global coffee chain and an ice cream company take much of the supply while the small, serious buyers are rationed, in the words of one Tokyo wholesaler, a little bit here, a little bit there, waiting one month, then three, then five. If that is the real picture, popularity didn't rescue the craft. It kept the shell and threw out the work.

I can't wave that away, because most of it is happening. When a cafe can't get good powder, cheaper and grassier powder fills the gap, and the cure for grassy powder is more sugar and more milk, until the tea is a rumor inside a dessert. A barista who would love to whisk every cup by hand, and does when it's quiet, telling you where the leaf came from while she works, switches to a shaken concentrate the moment a line forms, because the alternative is ten more minutes an order and customers walking out. None of that is a moral failing. It is just what speed does.

So the honest version of my claim is narrower than I would like. Popularity can rescue a craft, but only when the demand pays for the parts of it that are slow, instead of pretending speed and reverence are the same thing. Pay for the shade and the once-a-year harvest and the stone wheel and the cold storage, in real prices and in truthful words, and the craft gets to go on existing at scale. Keep the aesthetic and refuse the bill, and the critic is right, and you are drinking a photograph.

The strange thing is that the bill is actually being written. Auction prices for tencha, the leaf before grinding, ran to more than double the year before, a record. A famous Kyoto tea house told its customers the plain thing a seller hates to say, that the new harvest could not be sold at the old price. That is the market, clumsily and late, learning what the craft costs. A price is not the same as a value, but it is the first honest number the slow parts have ever carried. It looks like damage, and some of it is. But leaf that now sells for more than twice what it did last spring is not the obvious tragedy in this story.

Which brings me back to the woman in Dublin, and the one detail I can't get past. Asked about the shortage, she said that, thankfully, she hadn't had to change her prices or her menu yet. She sells a thirty-gram tin for twenty-eight euro, about fifteen cups, and for now she is holding the line. Yet is the whole word. She flew across the world for a slow thing and built a fast one on top of it, and the cap every few weeks is only the slow thing underneath, reminding her it was there the entire time.

So I keep coming back to the wheel. Forty grams an hour, one spring a year, a leaf that takes as long as it takes no matter how many people are waiting. Whether we ruined matcha was never the real question. The real one is whether we are willing to pay what slow really costs, or whether we only ever wanted it to look that way. I can't tell yet which one we are. I think we are about to find out.
